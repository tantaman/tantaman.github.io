Note that the cage is only the current cage. That there will be new cages. That the enemy is not the young thing.

# The Wheel: Chapters 7–9

*Turn, Engine, Fuel*

---

## Chapter 7: The Turn

*The wheel illustrated — what happens when the cage is broken*

**Epigraph:** Judges cycle — delivered, peace, forgetting, oppression, crying out, deliverer, delivered. The historians who named the wheel before we had the word for it.

---

### Opening: The Anomaly

The most liberated, rights-expanded, materially comfortable generation in human history. Also the most anxious, depressed, lonely, childless. By almost every psychological and social measure, the arc bent — and this is where it arrived.

The question is not whether liberation happened. It did. The question is what happened to it.

---

### I. The Victories Are Real

Don't underplay them. Slavery abolished. Suffrage won. Legal equality established. Formal systems of caste and exclusion dismantled across the Western world within living memory. These were genuine achievements against genuine resistance. The arc did bend. Anyone who begins here with cynicism has not paid attention.

The problem is not that nothing changed. The problem is what keeps happening *after* things change.

---

### II. The Historical Cases

Three instances of the wheel completing a turn, shown in enough detail that the pattern becomes undeniable before it is named:

**The French Revolution → Napoleon**
Fifteen years. The vocabulary of liberation — liberté, égalité, fraternité — intact. The structure of power: reconcentrated, now in a single man who understood himself as the Revolution's completion. The guillotine had cleared the field. The field filled again.

**The Russian Revolution → Stalin**
A decade. The industrial infrastructure retained. The security apparatus expanded and redirected. The vanguard party — designed as the instrument of liberation — becoming the instrument of a new tyranny more total than the one it replaced. The revolutionaries who survived long enough to see it mostly did not survive much longer.

**The Civil Rights Movement → the DEI industry**
The longer, slower turn. The vocabulary of the movement absorbed into corporate compliance departments, foundation grant cycles, university administrative structures. The PMC as institutional capture — here the Marx resurrection earns its place. Not Marx's voice, but the book's: what would the author of *Das Kapital* make of the class that inherited his vocabulary while serving interests he spent his life anatomizing? The credentialed class that speaks liberation while extracting. The movement's grammar, now on a Fortune 500 website.

---

### III. Language Capture

The wheel's most elegant mechanism because it feels like victory while it's happening.

"Diversity." "Empowerment." "Community." "Sustainability." Each began as disruptive grammar — words that named what the system excluded or destroyed. Each is now marketing copy. Each has been absorbed so completely that the original charge has inverted: the words now legitimate the systems they were coined against.

This is not theft exactly. No one decided to neutralize the vocabulary. The grammar traveled through the same status networks described in Ch 6 — absorbed by proximity, reproduced by aspiration, until the words retained their warmth and shed their weight. The tongue that cannot say *class interest* can still say *equity*. The cage got new curtains and called it progress.

---

### IV. The Judges Cycle Named

Now name the pattern directly. Not as cynicism — as structure. The wheel is not evidence that nothing matters or that resistance is futile. It is a description of what happens when genuine liberation runs without the transformation of the self doing the liberating. The victories were real. The wheel turned anyway. Why?

That question belongs to Ch 8. Here: establish that it turns. Establish the pattern with enough historical weight that the reader cannot dismiss it as selective pessimism.

Close with Ecclesiastes. Qohelet has watched long enough to be past surprise. The sun rises. The rivers run to the sea and return. Generations come and go. *Is there a thing of which it is said, see, this is new? It has been already in the ages before us.* This is not despair. It is the long view. The wheel is not a modern problem. It is the shape of human history when nothing from outside the wheel intervenes.

---

## Chapter 8: The Engine

*Why the wheel turns — the external mechanics of capture*

**Epigraph:** 1 Samuel 8 — Samuel tells them what a king will do. Take your sons. Take your fields. Take your flocks. They say yes anyway. The anti-amplifier arrangement replaced by monarchy by popular demand.

---

### Opening: The Question

The victories were real. The wheel turned anyway. The analysis in Ch 7 showed the pattern. This chapter shows the mechanism. Not why people are weak or corrupt or insufficiently committed. Why the wheel turns structurally — what physics, operating on any revolution by any people with any degree of sincerity, produces the same result.

---

### I. Power as Attractor State

The amplifier theory translated into the book's register. Not the essay's analytical mode — the phenomenological mode.

The salmon run. The granary. The printing press. The algorithm. The credential. Different nouns, the same grammar: wherever a resource can be concentrated, defended, and converted into durable advantage, power concentrates. This is not a human failing. It is a property of systems. Water flows downhill. Power concentrates. The revolutionary who wins inherits the infrastructure of the system they defeated — and the infrastructure does what infrastructure does.

The Jacobins did not set out to build Napoleon. They built a centralized administrative state capable of coordinating a nation at war, because that was what winning required. Napoleon stepped into a machine that was already running. He did not corrupt the Revolution. He completed it — completed the logic of the instruments the Revolution had built to survive.

The principalities framing from Ch 6 earns its keep here: the thing being captured is not the people. The people change. The structure remains. The institution that began as a movement develops survival interests. The vocabulary that began as critique becomes a professional credential. The power that was seized to be distributed gets concentrated again, because concentration is what power does when the counter-pressure relaxes.

---

### II. The Remainder

Every system produces a remainder — the people the new order does not include, cannot include, or actively excludes in the process of consolidating its gains.

The French Revolution produced an aristocracy dispossessed of property and a church dispossessed of land, both with generational grievances and nothing to lose. The Thermidorean reaction was not a conspiracy. It was the remainder overturning.

The Civil Rights victories produced a white working class that experienced the gains as losses — not because they were, materially, but because the grammar of the movement did not have a place for their grievance, and grievance without a grammar becomes resentment that waits. The remainder finds its voice eventually. It always does.

This is not an argument against the victories. It is a description of a structural feature of any political change: the new order has edges, and the people at the edges will eventually push back. The question is never whether the remainder will act. The question is when and in what form. The wheel depends on this. Without the remainder, the wheel stops.

---

### III. Will as Engine

Revolutions run on will — on the concentrated moral energy of people who have decided that the current arrangement is intolerable and who are willing to pay costs to change it. This is real energy. It produces real change. It is also capturable by the same structures it set out to dismantle.

The mechanism: will requires organization to be effective at scale. Organization requires institutions. Institutions develop interests. The interests of the institution eventually diverge from the interests of the movement. The will that powered the movement is now powering the institution — which is to say, the will has been captured and redirected.

This is not betrayal by bad actors, though bad actors exist. It is institutional physics. The NAACP, the AFL-CIO, the Sierra Club — each was the organizational expression of genuine moral will. Each became, over time, an institution with a budget to protect, a staff to pay, and an interest in the continuation of the problem it was formed to solve. Not because the people inside them were corrupt. Because that is what institutions do.

The rebel's trap from the original Ch 5 draft belongs here: every fight is still the ego swinging. Every rebellion is still the self trying to save itself by other means. The cage anticipated the rebel. It built a cell for him too. He is right about everything and going nowhere. The will is real. The will is not enough.

---

### IV. Institutionalization

Brief. The movement wins, builds institutions to protect the gains. The institutions develop survival interests that diverge from the original purpose. Not corruption. Institutional physics. One mechanism among others — named, not belabored.

---

### V. Generational Forgetting

Named briefly here. Deferred to The Ground for full treatment — it lives more naturally there, adjacent to The Table's material and the practices that interrupt the forgetting.

The pattern: those who build know why they build — they faced the emptiness, stood at the edge, understood why walls exist. Those who inherit do not know — they received without cost, live inside walls whose purpose they never learned. The walls feel like prisons. They tear down. Their children face the emptiness. And build again.

The prodigal is not an aberration. The prodigal is the pattern. This is the wheel at the generational scale. 

*The Table* has already said this more beautifully than analysis can. Name it, gesture toward The Ground, move on.

---

### Closing: The Engine Seen Whole

Power concentrates. The remainder overturns. Will gets captured. Institutions calcify. Language inverts. The generation that inherits doesn't know what it has. Any one of these mechanisms alone would be manageable. Together they form a closed system. The wheel turns not because people are evil but because these forces operate on any system, any revolution, any people — regardless of their sincerity, their intelligence, or the justice of their cause.

This is where the sociological analysis reaches its limit. The mechanisms are real. But the mechanisms don't fully account for the wheel's persistence, because they locate the problem in the system. And the system is made of people.

Which raises the question Ch 9 will answer: what if the problem isn't the system?

---

## Chapter 9: The Fuel

*The anthropological bottom — the self that would break any system*

**Epigraph:** Exodus 32 — Forty days after liberation from Egypt. Before Moses comes down. The golden calf. The wheel turns in forty days, not because of external capture or the remainder, but because the animal needs something to worship and will manufacture one if none is provided.

---

### Opening: A Thought Experiment

Suppose Ch 8's mechanisms were solved. Imagine a revolution that produced no remainder — the gains were distributed so completely that no edge cases were left outside. Imagine the will was never captured — the institutions remained genuinely accountable to the movement. Imagine the language never inverted. Imagine the next generation somehow inherited the memory.

Would the wheel stop?

Dostoevsky thought not. The underground man doesn't need deprivation or injustice or the remainder's grievance. He needs only the Crystal Palace — the perfect system, rationally organized for human flourishing — and his own freedom, which he will exercise precisely by refusing it. *Shower upon him every earthly blessing... and even then... he would play you such a trick that you would be amazed at him.* Not from stupidity. Not from ingratitude. From freedom. From the need to prove that he is a man and not a piano key.

This is the deepest mechanism. And it isn't a mechanism. It's us.

---

### I. Mimetic Desire

We want what others want. This is not a moral failing — it is the structure of human desire, which Girard spent a career describing and which the previous chapters have been circling since Ch 4's identifying self.

The infant desires the toy only when another child reaches for it. Remove the other child; the toy sits forgotten. The adult desires the position, the status, the recognition — not because these have intrinsic value but because others desire them, and desire is social before it is personal. The algorithm knows this better than we do. It doesn't sell products. It sells images of others wanting products.

This produces rivalry before it produces anything else. Two hands reaching for the same bread — conflict is born before any system of property exists to adjudize it. Two hearts wanting the same glory — enmity is born before any ideology names it. The wanting was never even original. It was borrowed. And now people die for the borrowing.

The wheel's fuel is not injustice, not scarcity, not bad systems. The wheel's fuel is mimetic desire running through human communities, generating rivalry, generating the violence that produces scapegoats, generating the temporary peace of unified opposition, generating the next cycle of desire and rivalry when the scapegoat is gone. The cage changes shape. The fuel is constant.

---

### II. Violence From the Beginning

The amplifier essay ends with an honest concession: the problem is not the systems, it is us. The Ju/'hoansi are not egalitarian because they lack dominance impulses. They are egalitarian because they actively suppress them in an environment where suppression is possible. The impulse is there. The salmon run activates it.

This matters for the wheel because it closes the political exit. If violence and domination were installed by bad systems — by capitalism, by colonialism, by any contingent historical arrangement — then replacing the system would, in principle, replace the violence. This is the socialist's convenient blindness: locate the source of domination in an institution young enough to be slain, and you preserve the fantasy that your hands are clean, that the will to power is a disease you have not contracted.

But violence is at the salmon run. It is in the first amplifier, which precedes any system that has ever been critiqued. It is in the Tlingit who owned the fishing sites before a European boot touched the shore. It is in the Aztec skull rack and the Cahokia burial of living servants. These are not capitalism. They are us. The system is downstream of the animal. You can change the system. The animal remains.

---

### III. Dostoevsky's Final Argument

The underground man as the closing of the political solution.

Not an eccentric. Not a clinical case. A type — the type that every utopia produces as its first and most reliable product. The man who will not be organized, managed, optimized, or liberated on someone else's terms. Who will suffer freely rather than flourish on a schedule. Who will kick over the Crystal Palace specifically because someone built it for his benefit.

This is the argument that political theory cannot answer. Every cage is an elaborate configuration of forces designed to manage human behavior toward better outcomes. The underground man is the proof that management is not transformation. You can build a better cage. The animal inside it is unchanged. And the animal, given sufficient pressure toward rational happiness, will eventually exercise its freedom by choosing the irrational — not from stupidity, but from the irreducible need to be something other than a well-managed output.

All political theories are elaborate cages. Better cages than worse cages — the distinction matters enormously for the people inside them. But cages. The distance from a well-designed cage to a poorly designed one is the distance that politics can travel. The distance from a cage to something else is a different kind of distance entirely.

---

### IV. Therefore: Transformation, Not Rearrangement

The logic is now complete and the implication is unavoidable.

If the problem were the system, better systems would solve it. The wheel shows that better systems are necessary but insufficient — the mechanisms of Ch 8 operate on any system.

If the problem were the mechanisms, eliminating the mechanisms would solve it. The thought experiment at the chapter's opening shows that it would not — Dostoevsky's man would still kick it over.

If the problem is the animal — the mimetic desire, the violence at the salmon run, the underground man's need to refuse — then the only exit is transformation of the animal. Not management of the animal, which is what every political theory attempts. Transformation. Something that changes what the animal wants, not what it is permitted to do.

This is not a political program. There is no political program for transforming the self. Every attempt to make one becomes another cage — the most total cage, the one that reaches not just for behavior but for the interior.

What transforms the self is not a program. It is a practice, a community, a long patience, a grace that cannot be engineered. It is the thing The Ground will try to describe.

---

### Closing: The Biblical Bottom

The golden calf. Forty days after the most dramatic liberation event in the Hebrew Bible. The people are free. The oppressor is drowned. The law is being given on the mountain. And they build a god out of their jewelry because they cannot bear the silence and the waiting.

This is the wheel at its most elemental. Not captured by power. Not overturned by the remainder. Not betrayed by corrupt leaders. Built by free people, voluntarily, in the absence of the oppressor, because the animal needs something to worship and will manufacture one rather than wait.

Moses comes down. The calf is ground to powder and dissolved in water and drunk. The wheel has completed its first turn inside the story of liberation itself — before the people have reached the promised land, before any of Ch 8's mechanisms have had time to operate.

The Judges cycle names the pattern across generations. The golden calf names it at the anthropological bottom. The self that builds the calf is the self that will inhabit any system, any liberation, any well-designed cage — and find, within forty days, what it needs to worship in the silence.

That self is the problem the rest of this book is about.

*Revelation's Babylon is the wheel named as principality — the beast that was, and is not, and is to come. Not an empire. A power that outlasts any particular empire, that keeps returning under new names and new costumes, that John watches from Patmos with the long view Qohelet had from Jerusalem. Come out of her, my people. Not reform her. Not improve her terms. Come out.*

*The kingdom is not of this world. That is not a concession. It is the only exit from the wheel.*

**On the opener — against starting with the mechanisms.**

The book has been earning its claims through scene before argument. The Wheel needs the same. And you have a genuinely strange empirical fact sitting right at the surface: this is the most liberated, most rights-expanded, most materially comfortable generation in human history — and it is the most anxious, most depressed, most lonely, most childless. The arc bent. This is where it arrived.

Open there. Don't open with mechanisms or with historical cases. Open with the anomaly. Let the reader feel the wrongness of it — the gap between what all that liberation was supposed to produce and what it actually produced. Then ask: what happened? The wheel is the answer to that question. That framing also threads directly back to Ch 4's Sunday hum, which is the same phenomenon seen from the interior rather than the exterior.

**On organization — three movements:**

**Ch 7: The Turn** — Empirical. The wheel in motion. Genuine victories first — don't underplay them. Abolition, suffrage, civil rights, legal equality. These were real. The arc did bend. Then: the modern condition data. Then: historical cases of capture. French Revolution → Napoleon (fifteen years). Russian Revolution → Stalin (a decade). The Civil Rights Movement → the DEI industry (here the Marx resurrection earns its place — Marx resurrected to analyze the PMC that inherited his vocabulary while serving its own class interest). The point is not that these movements failed — it's that something keeps happening to them. The wheel turns.

**Ch 8: The Engine** — Mechanistic. Why does it keep happening? Here's where the mechanisms get laid out:
- Power as attractor state — the amplifier theory, but translated from the essay's analytical register into the book's register. The salmon run. The granary. The algorithm. The credential. Different nouns, same grammar.
- The remainder — who is always left outside, and what they do with that
- Will as fuel — revolutions run on will, and will is capturable. The cage anticipated the rebel.

**Ch 9: The Fuel** — Anthropological bottom. The deepest claim, the one that makes The Ground necessary rather than optional. The problem is not the systems. The problem is us.
- Mimetic desire — we want what others want, which produces rivalry before it produces anything else
- Violence within — not installed by capitalism or any contingent system, present at the salmon run, present in the first amplifier
- And then Dostoevsky — this is the chapter's hinge. Even if you solved all of it. Even if the remainder were gone, power distributed, will uncaptured. Someone would kick it over. The underground man doesn't need deprivation. He needs only the Crystal Palace — the perfect system — and his own freedom, which he will exercise precisely by refusing it. This is the claim that closes the political solution permanently: not that we haven't found the right configuration, but that the self running any configuration is the problem.
- Therefore: transformation, not rearrangement. All political theories are elaborate cages. Which opens The Ground.

**On the essays as raw material:**

The Socialist's Convenient Blindness is polemical in a way the book isn't — the aphoristic, Nietzsche-pastiche register doesn't fit. But its core argument translates: revolutionaries locate domination in an institution young enough to be slain. They need a villain. The villain is a costume the will to power wears. Strip the costume; it finds another. That argument belongs in Ch 8 as part of the engine section, translated into the book's register.

The Marx resurrection is Ch 7's strongest historical case but needs the same translation — the PMC as institutional capture, not in Marx's voice but in the book's.

The Amplifier essay is the empirical backbone of Ch 8-9, but its conclusion ("design counter-amplifiers, it's a permanent tension to be managed") is actually the position the book is arguing *against* — or rather, the position the book argues is insufficient. The amplifier theory gets you to "design better institutions." Ch 9 argues you can't get there from here because the designer is the problem. That tension is productive and should be named explicitly.
