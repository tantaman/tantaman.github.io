# Chapter Eleven: The Unearned

*What remains when the account cannot be settled*

---

Job's friends need him to be guilty.

They have come to comfort a man whose life has been destroyed. For seven days they sit with him in silence. Then the explanations begin. There must be a connection between the suffering and the sufferer. A concealed fault. A forgotten obligation. Something that would restore the order in which a person can live safely by living rightly.

Job keeps refusing the explanation. The reader knows why he can refuse it: the story has already established his integrity. His friends are defending an account of the world against the person sitting in front of them. Their defense requires his confession. They cannot let him remain what the text says he is, because an innocent Job would mean that their own innocence does not protect them either.

The honest record preserves this. It preserves the friends' need to explain and the violence that need does to the afflicted. It also preserves the long silence before they began explaining — the part of their visit in which they were able to be with him.

The first thing this record takes away is the guarantee.

Ecclesiastes observes that speed does not always win the race, wisdom does not reliably produce bread, and righteousness does not secure the outcome righteousness deserves. These are observations about the failure of a guarantee, not the uselessness of wisdom. Training still matters to a runner. Honesty still matters to a friendship. A bridge built carefully is more likely to stand. The error begins when these particular connections are expanded into a total account: the world gives each person what that person has earned.

It does not. Competence meets circumstances. Choices meet other people's choices. Bodies meet events they cannot control. A life can be damaged by something that says nothing about the worth of the person living it.

That last sentence is easy to assent to when it concerns somebody else. Applied inwardly, it asks for the collapse of an entire court. The failure no longer gets to testify that you were always a failure. The loss cannot establish that you were unworthy of what was lost. Success, in turn, loses its authority to prove that you deserve more of the earth than those who did not succeed.

The cross drives this separation into the center of the Christian account. The one whom the narrative declares righteous is condemned by the cooperating authorities of his world. The verdict is legible. The execution is public. Neither makes the verdict true.

Here is a ground the cage cannot provide: a judgment of the person that can stand against what happened to the person. The question is whether that ground is real — and what would allow someone to stand on it when the verdict has entered the body.

---

## I. Before the Work

The older brother has a case.

He stayed. He worked. His brother took the inheritance, consumed it, and returned when it was gone. Now there is music in the house. The father has ordered a feast. The son whose labor kept the place running stands outside, hearing celebration for the one whose conduct should have disqualified him from it.

His anger is comprehensible because his account is comprehensible. Effort should count. Faithfulness should count. If they count for nothing, what has he been doing all these years?

The father's answer reaches for something the account has not measured. The younger son was lost and has returned. The older son has been with his father all along. Belonging was available before either son could convert it into a wage. One tried to spend it. The other tried to earn it. Both mistook what it was.

This does not abolish wages. A worker can still be cheated, a promise broken, a debt legitimately owed. The parable does not give an employer permission to replace payment with a speech about generosity. Its disturbance is more intimate: does all the goodness available to a person have to arrive through deserving? Must every welcome be the result of an examination?

Chapter Four followed the achievement subject into a vacation he could not endure. Without his tasks, he did not know what justified his presence. His problem was older than the unfinished work. Work had become the process through which he continuously requested permission to exist.

The claim of given worth interrupts that request. The person has a value that precedes usefulness, survives failure, and does not increase when someone else is diminished. The Christian name for its ground is the image of God. To call that worth infinite is to refuse a final exchange rate: no quantity of output exhausts what the person is, and no loss of output cancels the claim the person makes upon us.

This is a theological and moral commitment. The failure of merit to govern events does not logically prove it. An indifferent universe and a world of given worth are different interpretations of the same undeserved suffering. What the honest record offers is a reason to inhabit the second: it repeatedly places the condemned and abandoned person before the reader and asks whether the public verdict has told the truth.

Grace names the welcome that does not wait for the account to balance.

It reaches the confessing animal at a different wound. She has things to answer for. Some are grave. Grace does not make those things unreal or transfer the cost back to the people she hurt. An apology may still be owed. Restitution may still be possible. What changes is the relation between the act and the whole person. She can name what she did without having to make her entire existence equal to it. She can receive forgiveness without first completing the impossible work of becoming someone who never needed forgiveness.

There is no final insight she must extract from herself to become eligible. The naming can end for today. Tomorrow may ask something further. Tomorrow does not have to revoke the welcome that made today's honesty possible.

## II. The One Who Kept the Ledger

The account rebuilds itself quickly.

A person hears that grace cannot be earned and begins working at receptivity. She learns that worth precedes achievement and makes accepting her worth into the next achievement. She notices the contradiction, then takes pride in noticing it. The machinery has acquired a more delicate instrument. It can now measure how well she has stopped measuring.

Luke tells a story about two men praying. The Pharisee lists what distinguishes him: his fasts, his gifts, his distance from the failures of other people. The tax collector has no comparable list. He asks for mercy.

The story specifies its target before either man speaks: people confident in their own righteousness who regard others with contempt. The figure is a warning to the reader, not a description of the Pharisees as a whole or a license to make Jewish observance the opposite of Christian grace. The Christian who thanks God for being free of legalism can occupy the first man's place without changing a word of his theology. So can the secular critic who thanks no one for seeing through what the religious still believe.

The Pharisee need not be lying about his conduct. He may do every good thing he names. The injury occurs in the use to which the goodness is put. His neighbor has become the negative term required by his own positive result. Even prayer has become an occasion to make the result official.

Call him the optimizer in his spiritual register. He has a standard, a procedure, a record of compliance, and a self whose standing should follow from the record. The trouble is that anything which threatens the result now threatens him. He cannot afford to learn from the tax collector. He needs the tax collector to remain beneath him. The practice that could have opened him to another person has been recruited to keep that person at a distance.

Optimization is useful when the object is sufficiently clear. Count the meals a kitchen can serve. Reduce the errors in a process that harms people. Check whether promised aid arrives. Those measurements protect the people whom pious vagueness can abandon. The danger begins when the measure becomes a complete account of goodness, and a favorable account becomes the thing the agent serves.

Then an inconvenient person is experienced as a problem with the metric. Her need arrives at the wrong time. His complaint spoils the evidence. The donor wants gratitude; the caregiver wants improvement; the teacher wants a pupil whose progress demonstrates the teaching. What looks like generosity can contain an unspoken demand: become the evidence that I am good.

The person on the receiving end feels that demand. She has been given something, and another debt has quietly opened.

Moral formation requires that the person remain capable of interrupting the account. This does not require moral ignorance. Knowing that cruelty is wrong can strengthen a person who refuses it. Clear reasons matter. The uncertainty that cannot be eliminated is more particular: you cannot secure in advance what your action will mean in another life, whether you will be thanked, whether doing the good will preserve the image of yourself that helped you begin.

The hidden curriculum is this exposure. The answer you know has to meet the person you do not. Whatever goodness develops in the encounter has passed through a place your account did not control.

## III. Preparing Without Purchasing

Does this make practice another trap?

It can. A man begins sitting quietly each morning and discovers that he can occasionally let a thought pass without following it. He keeps sitting. Eventually he has a streak to protect, a vocabulary of attainment, a view of the people who have not done the work. The quiet has become part of his résumé. An interruption now threatens his spiritual life in exactly the way it once threatened his productivity.

Something real happened at the beginning. Something real may still happen. The capture does not prove that the practice was fraudulent. It shows what the accumulating self can do with a benefit, including a benefit that briefly loosens its hold.

The opposite error is to conclude that habits are inherently corrupt and only an event without preparation could be free. A habit of listening can help another person finish a difficult sentence. A dependable hour of silence can make an unexamined demand audible. Learning to restrain a cruel response can protect someone while the disposition behind it is still changing. These are gains. Calling every gain accumulation would make the theory incapable of recognizing the difference between care and capture.

The distinction concerns ownership. Training can change what you are able to do. It cannot make another person's trust your property. Practice can prepare attention. It cannot make whatever attention discovers obey the purposes for which you prepared it.

Consider listening to someone you love. You can set down the phone, make time, learn to notice when you are preparing your reply. All of this helps. But if the whole arrangement exists to produce a particular disclosure, a particular gratitude, a particular confirmation of closeness, the other person remains inside your project. Listening becomes an instrument for making the relationship deliver.

The release may come when you discover that this person has something to say which will not improve your position. You can still attend. The preparation has not been wasted. It has reached something it cannot purchase.

This is also why the wheel's history points toward contemplation without proving contemplation to be the only possible exit. Institutions can correct themselves. People can recognize motives they did not previously understand. Reforms can change incentives and protect the vulnerable. No theorem forbids a mind from examining its own habits. The specific difficulty is that a project organized entirely around securing the self keeps renewing the demand it proposes to satisfy. It offers the self another result on which to make its security depend.

The contemplative question reaches beneath that arrangement. Can the demand relax without first being paid?

John of the Cross describes practitioners who become attached to the sweetness of spiritual exercises, pursuing the pleasure of devotion and becoming irritable when it disappears. His account of the dark night includes the loss of those consolations and the exposure of the appetite that had made them indispensable. The possibility he describes is love continuing when the experience no longer certifies the lover. [His description of spiritual attachment is unusually concrete.](https://ccel.org/ccel/john_cross/dark_night.all.html)

Pain does not establish that such a transformation is occurring. Nor does comfort establish that it is false. To treat suffering as a spiritual credential would reopen the ledger with suffering as its currency. The force of John's account lies elsewhere: even the experience of divine nearness can become something the self insists on possessing.

Grace cannot be the reward for successfully undergoing this loss. Then the bargain would survive intact: relinquish enough, receive the highest good. The theological claim is that the movement toward receptivity is already held by something it did not originate. Even making room cannot become the last qualification you must earn.

The hands can be tired. The attention can wander. The welcome cannot depend on becoming an accomplished receiver.

## IV. What Can Be Observed

The language of release can become so elevated that nothing would count against it. A careful account has to come back to what can be observed.

There is empirical work relevant to one part of the claim: the familiar way of experiencing oneself is not the only way awareness can be organized. Three studies provide useful points of contact. They do not measure grace.

In 2007, Norman Farb and colleagues compared two forms of attention during brain imaging: thinking about oneself across time and attending to current thoughts, feelings, and bodily sensations. Participants who had completed an eight-week mindfulness course showed a more pronounced shift between the neural patterns associated with these modes than participants awaiting training. The finding suggests that sustained self-evaluation and immediate experience can be distinguished more than ordinary habit makes apparent. The study's “narrative” condition concerns personal self-description; it is not a test of the narrative knowing discussed in the previous chapter. [Farb et al., “Attending to the present.”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2566754/)

In 2011, Judson Brewer and colleagues compared twelve experienced meditators with twelve matched controls. During several meditation practices, the experienced group reported less mind-wandering and showed lower activity in key regions of the default mode network, a network associated with processes including self-reference. This small comparison cannot establish that meditation caused the group differences: people who sustain a practice for years can differ from people who do not before training begins. It does provide evidence that attention can operate with less of the activity accompanying the ordinary wandering, self-concerned stream. [Brewer et al., “Meditation experience is associated with differences in default mode network activity and connectivity.”](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1112029108)

Zoran Josipovic and colleagues asked a more specific question in a study published in 2012. They compared focused attention and nondual awareness meditation in experienced practitioners. Data from twenty-two people were analyzed; fourteen completed both forms. During nondual practice, networks associated with internally and externally oriented processing were less opposed in their activity than during the comparison condition; focused attention showed the opposite pattern. The authors explicitly called the connection between this result and experienced subject–object division speculative. They also lacked a reliable measure of the quality of the meditative states beyond rudimentary checks. [Josipovic et al., “Influence of meditation on anti-correlated networks in the brain.”](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2011.00183/full)

The evidence supports a possibility the achievement subject has difficulty imagining: some of the processing bound up with the ordinary experience of self can change while awareness continues. A person need not maintain the same stream of commentary in order to remain present.

It does not follow that the default mode network is the ego, that less activity means greater holiness, or that a changed relation between brain networks demonstrates union with God. A brain image cannot determine whether a person has become more generous. The studies are small, their tasks differ, and the passage from a measured neural pattern to the meaning of an experience remains an interpretation.

These limits matter because the evidence is already enough to unsettle one assumption. The self's familiar arrangement does not have to be treated as the permanent form of consciousness. The theological question begins where that modest finding stops: what, if anything, is encountered when the arrangement loosens?

---

## V. The Verdict Loosens

Return to the sentence the failed life keeps hearing: *this means I am worthless*.

There is an event. Then there is a total meaning attached to the event. Then the meaning is experienced as if it were one more fact the event contained. The job was lost. The relationship ended. The person is worthless. All three arrive with the same apparent authority.

But the third is doing something the first two cannot do. It is extending a particular loss into a judgment without remainder. It claims to have exhausted the person.

A different relation to the loss can become possible. The pain remains. Consequences remain. Yet the judgment ceases to occupy the whole field. This is one reason meaning must be understood through encounter: another person can meet the same failed life and refuse the conclusion that seemed inseparable from it. Someone stays. Someone speaks your name without making it an accusation. What happened acquires a meaning the verdict could not generate on its own.

Mystical language extends this possibility beyond the particular encounter. In Eckhart's account, the Ground is prior to the distinctions through which the self assigns and defends its position. To be received there would be to cease deriving existence from the account. The claim is more than a pleasant change of perspective. It concerns what a person most deeply is and what sustains that person before any judgment arrives.

That claim cannot be smuggled into the conclusion of a neuroscience paper. It has to be faced as a claim. The Christian contemplative gives it a theological name; a secular account may speak of attention, attachment, and a less rigid experience of self. Their descriptions can touch without their explanations becoming identical. The disagreement remains open to thought, experience, and the lives each account makes possible.

For the achievement subject, the immediate disturbance is already considerable. Rest no longer has to testify to tomorrow's productivity. For the confessing animal, honesty need not be an endless petition for admission. Both can continue the work that matters without requiring the work to manufacture the worker's worth.

The gift is not an exemption from the world. The people you owe are still there. The need that interrupted your plans is still there. What may be absent, for a moment, is the additional task you brought to every encounter: make this establish me.

There is a wanting left when that task falls quiet. The other person can still be hungry. You can still want her fed. The desire does not have to disappear with the account.

What would it look like to act from there?
