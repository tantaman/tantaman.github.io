# Epilogue: The Next Morning

*A fictional continuation of Nine Months, Two Men*

---

The baby is home. This has not made the house quiet.

One of the boys has lost a shoe inside the house and considers the house responsible. The other is standing in the kitchen holding a bowl at an angle that makes its contents a matter of time. His wife is sitting at the table in the clothes she slept in. Her tea has gone cold.

He has been awake since four. The baby made a sound he did not recognize. She made it again, then went back to sleep. He stayed awake.

His laptop is on the counter.

For nine months it held the future in columns. The figures were useful once. He had kept updating them long after they ceased to tell him anything he did not know. Changing a number provided a brief sense of movement. He could do something. He could prepare something. He could be somewhere other than the place where he had to wait.

Now there are new things to prepare for.

He puts his hand on the lid.

His wife says his name.

He looks at her.

“Can you take her?”

“Of course.”

He says it a little too quickly, as if the speed can establish that he was about to offer. She notices. He notices her noticing. An explanation begins to assemble.

Then she stands, and he has to take the baby.

She is heavier than she looks and lighter than he expects. He has held her many times already. Each time, his hands have to learn her again. A foot presses against his wrist. Her mouth moves in the elaborate preparation for a complaint she then decides not to make.

His wife goes upstairs.

He has something he wants to tell her. That he sees it now. That he understands what the preparations had done to the months they were meant to protect. He has imagined saying it in a way that would let her know the man who disappeared into the calculations has come back.

He does not call her back downstairs to hear it.

The bowl tips.

Milk crosses the table, finds the edge, and begins to fall. The boy watches with the calm interest of someone who has initiated an event without yet accepting a role in it.

“Why would you—”

He hears his voice. The boy's face changes.

For a second he wants to finish the sentence. It is not an unreasonable question. The bowl was visibly tipped. He is tired. Everyone is tired. There are things children need to learn.

“Get the towel,” he says.

The boy does not move.

“The one on the oven. Please.”

The boy brings it. It is too small. They use it anyway, pushing the milk around rather than absorbing it, and then the other boy appears with the missing shoe in one hand and a bath towel in the other.

“That one will work,” he says.

The baby starts crying.

Nothing about the morning has become easier.

---

At ten, a neighbor knocks with a pot and a bag of bread. He has forgotten she was coming. His wife had arranged it two days ago and written it on the calendar he had walked past several times.

He begins to apologize for the kitchen.

The neighbor asks where the pot should go.

He clears a space. She sets it down. She asks whether there is anything else she can do, and the answer he has ready is no, they are fine, this is already too much.

There are bottles beside the sink. Laundry fills a chair. His wife is asleep upstairs, which means he is trying to keep the boys downstairs, which means neither has been outside all morning.

“Could you take them around the block?”

“Sure.”

He had expected the request to cost more in the saying.

They find the shoes. One boy wants a jacket and the other refuses one. The neighbor waits while they negotiate. Then the front door closes and, for the first time that morning, he can hear the refrigerator.

He sits down with the baby.

There are things to do. He can see them from where he is sitting. The dishes, the counter, the pot that needs a lid. His mind begins to arrange them in an order that would justify sitting down afterward.

She is asleep against him.

He stays.

---

In the other version of the morning, he has learned this earlier. He has spent more of the pregnancy in the room. He has made the necessary plans and, more often, closed them. He has told his wife when he is frightened. He has allowed other people to help.

He is also awake at four. He also snaps at the boy with the bowl. For a moment he is angry because he had thought he was past this.

The thought makes him angrier.

Then he sees the boy holding the small towel, waiting for permission to move.

There is no finished man in either kitchen.

There is this man, this child, the milk reaching the edge of the table. There is the next thing he can say.

---

When his wife comes downstairs, he is still sitting with their daughter. The neighbor has brought the boys back. They are drawing on the floor. Someone has put a purple sun on a page and is defending it.

She stands beside the chair and touches the baby's head.

“Did you sleep?” he asks.

“A little.”

He wants a little to be enough. It is not his decision.

“There's food.”

“I saw.”

She sits beside him. For a while neither speaks.

The fear has not gone. There will be another night, another noise, another opening through which the future enters before it is needed. He will sometimes follow it. He will sometimes need someone to say his name.

His wife asks him to pass the bread.

He does.
