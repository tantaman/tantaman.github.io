# Chapter Six: How the Bars Are Installed

*The cage does not require guards. It requires parents.*

---

You have seen the bars.

The next question arrives quickly — almost automatically, because the formation that produced you is very good at converting perception into problem-solving: *so what do I do about it?*

Hold that question. It belongs to a later part of this book. Before it can be answered honestly, something harder needs to be seen: the cage has been visible, at least in outline, for a long time. Polanyi's *The Great Transformation* appeared in 1944. Heidegger's essay on technology appeared in 1954. The Frankfurt School, Marcuse, the critics and the prophets and the diagnosticians — across their differences, someone has long been naming the ways a social order enters the person.

And the cage is still standing.

Not because the analysis was wrong. Because the cage does not require your ignorance to function. It requires only reproduction. And reproduction does not need a plan, does not need guards, does not need anyone to know what they are doing.

It needs imitation.

This is what Chapter Five left to be examined: the cage reproduces itself through an ordinary human capacity. We learn much of what to want by watching what others want. We learn how to be by watching how others are. Before we can evaluate a world, we are already inhabiting one. Not chosen. Inherited. Copied, hungrily, the way children always have — because imitation is part of how a human being becomes human.

By the time she can evaluate anything, the cage is already interior. Not a set of ideas to be assessed. A set of reflexes that feel like the self.

That is one way the bars stay up. The already-formed raise the next generation, often passing on what they themselves dislike. They can also pass on interruptions: the neighbor fed without a calculation, the afternoon allowed to remain an afternoon. Formation is powerful because imitation is powerful. That power is not exclusive to the cage.

---

## I. Before Language

The deepest installation happens before you can remember it.

The father who cannot sit still on a Sunday — who reaches for the phone, who makes the list, who feels the low-grade anxiety of productivity interrupted — is not delivering a lecture. He is demonstrating a relationship to time. The child watching him is not receiving a lesson. She is receiving a world. The mother who speaks of her inner life as a project — who talks about her growth, her work, her journey — is not instructing anyone. She is modeling what a self is. The parents who evaluate the neighborhood by its school ratings, who track their children's activities for portfolio value, who speak of their own friendships in terms of what they offer — none of them are doing anything wrong by their own lights. They are doing what one does. They are being what one is.

The ideology does not require the child's assent. It requires only imitation. And the child is already imitating, before language, before evaluation, before the capacity to distinguish a model from the world.

This is why the adult who intellectually refuses the cage still lives as though she hasn't. She still monitors her productivity. Still evaluates her relationships for what they return. Still experiences rest as a problem to be solved rather than a state to be inhabited. The formation is not in the mind. It is in the hands. In the habits. In the thousand small daily practices that reproduce the cage without requiring awareness or consent.

She has been imitating since before she could speak. An argument cannot undo those years by being understood once.

## II. The School

What the family installs by imitation, the school formalizes.

Schools have many purposes, and teachers often protect possibilities that the institution neglects. But preparation for the labor market supplies a particularly legible mandate: its claims can be attached to test scores, placements, earnings, and budgets. Other purposes must defend their place beside it.

Follow an imagined child through the path on which that mandate becomes a self.

He is six. He sits in a circle and completes a worksheet about feelings. The worksheet has faces on it — happy, sad, angry, scared, frustrated, calm. He practices identifying which face matches what he felt when his friend didn't share the blocks. He practices the strategy that helps him regulate it. The teacher is warm. The exercise is called Social-Emotional Learning. Its stated goal is to help children understand themselves and relate well to others.

The exercise can give him words where he previously had only a surge of feeling. That is a real good. It also introduces a possible relationship to the interior life: feelings as items to be categorized and managed. Whether this becomes care or administration depends on what comes next. Can the anger tell the adults that something is wrong in the room, or is the task complete only when the child becomes easier to manage?

He is ten. He got a C. The teacher sits beside him: *you haven't learned this yet.* Better than telling him he is simply bad at math. The idea that a capacity can develop opens a possibility. The shadow appears when it becomes a universal explanation of outcomes. When the job doesn't come, when the degree doesn't pay, when an institution extracts more than it returns, the same encouragement can be used to send the failure back inside him: develop yourself further. A growth mindset does not logically require that conclusion. An institution can recruit its language to avoid examining the conditions under which growth is being asked to occur.

He is seventeen. He is filling out a college application. He has spent two years preparing for this moment — selecting activities, curating experiences, differentiating his profile. He chooses an essay prompt about a challenge he faced. He writes something true. He writes it in a grammar he did not choose. Years of preparation have taught him to make the episode legible as a qualification. He is already fluent in a language of self-commodification. He does not experience this as loss. He experiences it as accomplishment.

He is ready. Much of the formation has become ordinary.

The employable subject that emerges carries several premises so deeply installed they do not feel like premises. That his outcomes reflect his inputs. That self-development is never finished, that there is no arrival, no Sabbath, no day on which enough has been done. That precarity is landscape, not failure. That his inner life is raw material to be processed and leveraged. That he is, in the last analysis, a portfolio.

He will enter the world with this. The grammar that awaits him there will feel like home.

---

Follow another child through a different room in the same building.

She is fourteen. The unit is American history, or literature, or both — the boundary has been deliberately blurred. The question written on the board: *whose story is being told? Whose is missing?* The teacher is good. The question is real. The texts being examined were, in fact, organized around a center that excluded; the absences are genuine absences. The correction is not wrong.

What can be installed, alongside the correction, is a method made total. Any room, any text, any situation is read for the same variables: who is centered, who is marginal, who speaks, who is silenced, what arrangements benefit whom. These are indispensable questions in many circumstances. They become a closed grammar when they are the only questions a serious person is permitted to ask.

She is good at this. She gets better every year. By sixteen, the method has become automatic — a perceptual default that precedes conscious application. She does not decide to read the room for power. She looks, and this is already what she sees.

She, too, reaches the college application. She chooses to write about her identity — what it cost her, what it taught her, what she understands about the world because she had to navigate it from where she stood. The essay is true. It also converts the wound into a credential. Suffering has been reformatted as competitive advantage. She does not experience this as loss. She experiences it as finally being seen.

She will enter a world that has prepared a specific place for this formation. The grammar that awaits her there will feel like recognition.

## III. The Grammar Cascade

Above the school, generating the language the school delivers, is a structure most people will never see — not because it is hidden, but because it is too large to see from inside.

Picture him at thirty-two. He graduated from a good school, joined a consulting firm, works long hours, is technically capable and ambitious. He has undergone, in three years, a transformation so gradual and total he could not trace it if asked.

When he arrived, he spoke in relatively plain language. He used words like *inequality* and *workers* and *communities*. He described problems in terms of who benefited and who was harmed.

Now he speaks differently. *Stakeholder alignment. Value creation ecosystems. Multi-sector partnerships. Scalable solutions. Implementation gaps. Incentive structures.* He has absorbed, through proximity and status pressure, a grammar that was produced at the apex of elite networks and reached him through a series of steps — the panels, the papers, the partners he watched present, the vocabulary the clients used, the language that marked someone as someone who understood how things actually worked.

He does not experience this as loss. He experiences it as sophistication.

What he does not notice is what the new grammar makes difficult to say in the rooms that matter. *Stakeholder alignment* sounds practical. *Class interest* sounds ideological. *Evidence-based intervention* sounds serious. *Power masquerading as expertise* sounds hostile. *Inclusive growth* is a proposal. *Growth that concentrates wealth while advertising inclusion* is a difficulty for the proposal's author. The words have not vanished from the language. Their standing has changed. He learns, through the reception of his sentences, which questions advance the meeting and which end his invitation to the next one.

Eventually he need not bite his tongue. The acceptable formulation arrives first. He thinks in *stakeholder alignment* because the phrase has become the shortest path from a difficulty to an answer his world will recognize.

Picture her at thirty-two. She graduated from the same kind of school, entered a different world — a nonprofit, an advocacy organization, an institution with explicit commitments to equity and justice. The vocabulary she absorbed there traveled through status networks as surely as *stakeholder alignment* traveled through his. *Centering marginalized voices. Holding space. Doing the work. Decentering. Naming harm. Sitting with discomfort.* She absorbed it through proximity to people she respected, through the panels and trainings and organizational documents that marked someone as someone who understood what was actually at stake.

She does not experience this as loss. She experiences it as clarity.

What she does not notice is what her grammar makes difficult to say in her rooms. *Centering lived experience* affirms the mission. *Does our funding reward the continuation of the problem?* puts the mission's custodians under examination. *Naming harm* directs attention outward. *Who can safely name harm inside this organization?* directs it toward the people distributing authority. These questions are possible; people within such institutions do ask them. The pressure is to replace the costly question with the recognizable phrase before the cost becomes explicit.

The propagation mechanism need not be coordinated. No committee has to determine the professional class's entire range of thought. Both grammars travel through status networks — absorbed by proximity to prestigious people, reproduced by those who want to be seen as competent, amplified by media that adopt the vocabulary in covering the networks. Criticism can circulate in both worlds. The selection pressure bears on the criticism that threatens the institution carrying it: how far it travels, who gets promoted for making it, whether it can govern a decision.

By the time either grammar reaches them, it has passed through institutions that confer professional legitimacy: business schools and consulting firms, credentialing bodies and advocacy organizations. It arrives as a language of seriousness, complete with examples of the people whose use of it has been rewarded.

The family installed it in the hands. The school installed it in the mind. The professional world installs it in the mouth. By the third installation, the cage is not felt as a cage. It is felt as competence.

## IV. What the Sociology Cannot Reach

Here is the problem with everything said so far.

The formation survives contact with its own exposure. The sociological critique has been available for decades. Intelligent, motivated people have read it, believed it, organized their lives around refusing it — and found themselves, years later, running the same engine by different means. The commune that became a hierarchy. The movement that became an institution. The prophet who became a gatekeeper. This is not a pattern of insufficient effort. It is a pattern that suggests the thing being resisted has more coherence than the resistance.

The sociology has named a mechanism. The theological tradition gives this kind of struggle another name: a principality.

Before accepting that word, consider the pattern it is being asked to name. A formation can exceed the intentions of the people inside it. Remove an individual participant and it continues. It recruits. It adapts. It absorbs critique and reproduces itself through critics. People who dedicate their lives to opposing it can find it has followed them into their organizations, their vocabularies, their ways of distributing status. The person in front of them is responsible for particular actions. Yet defeating that person may leave the pattern free to acquire another occupant.

You have felt this, if you have tried seriously to refuse the cage. The refusal that became a brand. The community that quietly sorted itself by the same metrics it was founded to reject. The moment you noticed you were monitoring your own resistance for signs of insufficient rigor — performing the critique rather than living otherwise. The formation is in the hands. You carried it with you. It arrived before you did.

Ephesians names a struggle against principalities and powers, beyond flesh and blood. In its own register, this is a spiritual claim, not an early treatise on organizational incentives. To read an institution through it is an interpretation: the empire is more than its current emperor, and the struggle exceeds a contest with the person holding office. Remove the emperor and the office remains available. The old vocabulary warns against mistaking the occupant for the whole of what is being resisted.

The cage, named in this register, has the character of a principality. It shapes hands before those hands can name what they enact; it survives individual replacements; it recruits even the will to oppose it. This does not prove that an institution possesses a literal mind or a supernatural will. The sociological description cannot settle that ontological question. The theological reading asks us to take seriously the experience of confronting a power whose persistence exceeds every individual intention — without turning the individuals into demons.

A surviving Roman document brings the question down to a gesture. Writing to Trajan, Pliny describes a test for people accused of being Christians: invoke the gods, offer incense and wine before the images, curse Christ. Those who complied could be released. The administration required a public act.[^ch06-pliny]

The document does not tell us that everyone who complied gradually lost their private faith. Coercion does not make the interior legible that easily. What it shows is that a gesture could have institutional meaning independently of the explanation a frightened person gave herself. The hand offering incense enacted a public allegiance; refusal interrupted the enactment. Repetition can form the person who repeats, but it does not erase the difference between conviction and duress. The comparison with our daily habits concerns that formative power. It does not make reaching for a phone equivalent to a persecuted person's choice under threat of death.

You have been offering incense. So has everyone you know. Not from weakness or bad faith — from a formation so thorough the altar stopped being visible as an altar. It became the desk. The feed. The list. The vocabulary that makes certain questions costly. The reflex that reaches for the phone before the thought forms.

In Pliny's test, the public act supplied the proof the administration would accept. The larger lesson is not that belief never matters. It is that an order can persist through what people enact while privately withholding agreement. Small daily gestures tell the watching community what is normal. Once habituated, participation can function like assent even where no assent was deliberately given. The cage is not only imposed from outside. It is enacted from inside by people for whom many of the gestures have stopped feeling like choices.

## V. What Changes Anyway

There is a temptation, having seen the mechanism whole, to believe that seeing is enough.

It is not enough. The formation has reached the hands. Argument can help redirect attention, but the hand has its own memory. What has been practiced into a life has to meet that life again.

A change of grammar can begin with an argument. It becomes more than an argument through encounters that give the new words a world to name: living among people whose practices interrupt your reflexes, meeting a crisis the old account cannot contain, recognizing something you could not previously see. Some changes are gradual. Others resemble conversion — the same world reassembling around a different center. The cage's pressures make such changes costly. They do not make them impossible.

That leaves a harder question. The people who undergo a conversion still have to live together. Their new language can become a credential; their refusal can acquire leaders and property; their freedom can be passed on as another set of requirements. How does the institution founded to interrupt the cage become one of its rooms? To answer that, we have to follow the wheel.

---

*The cage could not have designed a better guard than the self that believes it is free. The self that knows it is a guard is not thereby free. But it is, for the first time, available to become so.*

[^ch06-pliny]: Pliny the Younger, [*Letters* 10.96–97, with Trajan's reply](https://faculty.georgetown.edu/jod/texts/pliny.html). Pliny describes public worship and cursing Christ as tests; his report does not document a gradual psychological transformation in those who complied.
