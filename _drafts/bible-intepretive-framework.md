# How to Read the Oldest Record

## A Companion to “Woe Is Me Who Has Discovered the Disinterest of the Universe”

-----

Here is the synthesis.

The Bible is an honest record of what happened to one civilization over roughly two thousand years. Because the record is honest and the time horizon is long enough, it reveals the structure of reality — how power works, how institutions capture liberation, how merit fails to connect to outcomes, how every reform becomes the thing it reformed.

That structure is what some people call God.

The proper posture for reading the text is to let it break your frame. The improper posture is to use it to confirm your frame. And the text predicts that most people will adopt the improper posture — which is itself part of the structure it reveals.

-----

But why read it at all? If reality has a structure, can’t you just observe reality directly?

You can. But you’ll die before you see the pattern.

You get eighty years, maybe ninety. In eighty years you might watch one institution rise and one decline. You might see one political cycle complete itself. You might notice that a reform movement you joined in your twenties has become bureaucratic by your fifties. But you’ll never know if that’s a general law or a local accident. You won’t live long enough to see the cycle repeat five, six, ten times across different centuries and different contexts and arrive at the structural insight underneath.

The Bible compresses millennia of observation into a single text. It shows you the golden calf and the prosperity gospel — the same pattern separated by three thousand years. It shows you Samuel’s warning about kings and every centralized power structure since. It shows you the prophets captured and the critique of capture itself captured — a recursive pattern you could never observe in a single lifetime.

This is why we read any great literature. Not for information. For *compressed experience*. You read Thucydides to see what happens to democracies at war — because you won’t live through enough wars to learn it yourself. You read Shakespeare to see what ambition does to people across dozens of lifetimes compressed into an evening. You read Dostoevsky to experience the interior consequences of ideas you’ll never have time to live out personally.

The Bible does this at a scale no other text matches — not because it’s divine (though it may be), but because no other text covers this much time with this much honesty. It is the longest-running dataset on human nature and institutional behavior in existence. To ignore it because you’ve classified it as “religion” is like ignoring the longest clinical trial ever conducted because you don’t like the528 hospital where it started.

-----

## The Problem of Reading

If the proper posture is letting the text break your frame, then most readings of the Bible are improper — including most Christian readings.

This is not an accusation. It’s a description of how human beings interact with texts. You don’t read a text. You read a text *through a framework*. You arrive with needs, fears, prior commitments, cultural assumptions, and a community that has already told you what the text means. The text is large enough and varied enough that whatever you’re looking for, you’ll find it.

Want a God who rewards the righteous? Deuteronomy 28 is right there. Want a God who doesn’t? Ecclesiastes. Want a mandate for war? Joshua. For peace? The Sermon on the Mount. For submission to authority? Romans 13. For resistance to authority? Exodus. For wealth as blessing? Proverbs. For wealth as danger? The camel through the needle’s eye.

The Bible contains all of this because it’s an honest record spanning centuries of different authors, contexts, and experiences. Its comprehensiveness is both its greatest strength and its greatest vulnerability. The strength: no single framework can claim total ownership, because there’s always a text that undermines your system. The vulnerability: you can always find *enough* texts to build your system and ignore the rest.

The prosperity gospel pastor isn’t ignorant of the Bible. He knows it well. He’s performing canon within the canon — building his theology on Malachi 3 and Deuteronomy 28 while treating Ecclesiastes as Solomon’s bad mood and Job as a story with a happy ending. He’s selecting.

The liberation theologian isn’t ignorant either. She builds on Exodus, the prophets, and the Magnificat while downplaying Romans 13 and the Pastoral epistles. She’s also selecting.

The Christian nationalist selects. The pacifist selects. The progressive selects. The traditionalist selects.

*Everyone selects.* This is the default human reading posture, and the Bible predicts it. The golden calf was selection — we want *this* god, the visible one, the one that confirms our need for control. The false prophets were selecting — speaking “visions of their own minds” while claiming divine authority. The Pharisees were selecting — elevating the purity system while neglecting “justice, mercy, and faithfulness.”

The question is not whether you will be tempted to select. You will. The question is whether you adopt a posture that resists it.

-----

## The Proper Posture

The posture the text seems to demand is this: *come expecting to be contradicted*.

If you’re reading and everything confirms what you already believe, you’re not reading. You’re looking in a mirror. The honest record — because it is honest, because it spans centuries, because it contains genuine internal disagreement — will inevitably challenge any framework you bring to it. If it’s not challenging you, you’re filtering.

This means the most important parts of the text for any given reader are the parts that make them uncomfortable. The progressive Christian who is comfortable with the prophetic social justice tradition needs to sit with the passages that seem to affirm traditional sexual ethics and ask: am I too quickly dismissing this? The traditional Christian who is comfortable with the moral law needs to sit with Ecclesiastes demolishing the moral ledger and ask: am I too quickly domesticating this?

The text is not a buffet where you take what you like. It is a diagnostic instrument. The parts that disturb you are the parts doing the work.

## Contingent or Structural: The Hard Question

If the Bible is a long record containing both timeless structural insights and culturally contingent material, then the reader must somehow distinguish between them. This is where things get genuinely difficult, and where most readers abandon the proper posture in favor of a system that resolves the difficulty for them.

The framework gives us some signals.

**Does the text itself overturn it?**

This is the strongest signal. The dietary laws appear in Leviticus as commands. Then Jesus says it’s not what goes into a person that defiles them but what comes out (Mark 7:15). Then Peter receives the vision of the unclean animals: “What God has made clean, do not call profane” (Acts 10:15). The tradition *overturns its own code from within*. The law was liberation technology for a people in formation. It became a system of priestly control over clean and unclean. The text itself recognized the capture and moved on.

When the text does its own housecleaning, that’s a clear signal the material was contingent — functional for a time, then superseded.

**Is it in the legal code only, or does it connect to the narrative structure?**

The structural insights we’ve identified — power capture, institutional corruption, the failure of merit — appear as *narrative patterns* observed across centuries. They’re not commanded. They emerge from honest recording. The legal codes — rules about mildew, skin diseases, agricultural practices, purification rituals — are commands for specific situations. They live in a different layer of the text.

Material that exists only in the legal code with no connection to the broader narrative arc is probably contingent. Material that threads from narrative through law into the New Testament is probably structural.

**Is the cultural distance doing the work?**

Some prohibitions were addressing practices that no longer exist in recognizable form. Temple prostitution, specific pagan fertility rituals, forms of economic exploitation particular to ancient Near Eastern contexts. When the cultural referent has vanished, the prohibition may be contingent — not because it was wrong, but because it was *aimed at something that no longer exists to be aimed at*.

But cultural distance alone isn’t enough to establish contingency. “Do not murder” is also ancient. The creation narrative is also culturally distant. Something can be old and culturally embedded and still structural. Age doesn’t settle it.

**Does the broader structural argument depend on it?**

The central insights of the text — that power corrupts, that merit doesn’t reliably connect to outcomes, that every liberation gets captured — do not depend on any particular sexual ethic or dietary law or civil regulation. These insights are independent of the legal codes. This suggests the legal codes are, at minimum, less central than the structural patterns.

But some elements of the legal and narrative tradition may be load-bearing for the text’s anthropology in ways that aren’t immediately obvious. The creation account’s framing of human nature, for instance, might be doing structural work even if it looks like it’s “just” narrative.

## A Test Case: Homosexuality

This is where the framework has to do real work, because this is where the stakes are highest and the temptation to select is strongest.

The progressive case: The Leviticus prohibitions (18:22, 20:13) sit alongside shellfish bans, fabric-mixing rules, and other clearly contingent material. The cultural context suggests the prohibitions address specific exploitative practices — temple prostitution, pederasty, dominance rituals — rather than committed same-sex relationships, a category that didn’t exist for the ancient writers. The text’s own trajectory of internalizing external law (dietary laws → Mark 7 → Acts 10) suggests a pattern of moving beyond such codes.

The traditional case: Unlike the dietary laws, the homosexuality prohibitions are reiterated in the New Testament (Romans 1, 1 Corinthians 6) — meaning they survived the same transition that explicitly overturned the dietary and purity codes. The creation account establishes male-female pairing as foundational (Genesis 1-2), and Jesus himself references this specific pattern when asked about marriage (Matthew 19:4-6). There’s a thread from narrative structure through legal code into the teaching of Jesus and Paul that the dietary laws simply don’t have.

Both cases have real weight. And here is where the proper posture matters more than the conclusion.

The progressive who says “obviously contingent, like shellfish” is too confident. They must deal with the New Testament reiteration and the creation-narrative thread. They are selecting the texts that support their framework and minimizing the ones that don’t.

The traditionalist who says “obviously structural, like creation order” is also too confident. They must deal with the cultural distance, the genuine ambiguity of Paul’s terminology (*arsenokoitai* remains a contested term), and the fact that the text’s prohibitions may have been addressing practices categorically different from what we now mean by committed same-sex relationships. They too are selecting.

The honest reading holds the tension. It says: *this is genuinely ambiguous. The text provides evidence on both sides. Any reader who is certain in either direction is probably reading through a frame rather than letting the text break one.*

This will satisfy no one. That is the point. A reading that satisfies everyone has been captured. A reading that produces a clean program — “the Bible clearly says X” — is a vending machine. The text, if read in the posture it demands, produces not certainty but *serious engagement*, the kind that holds real people’s lives in one hand and the weight of the tradition in the other and refuses to drop either.

A reader who holds the ambiguity honestly and treats the people affected with the gravity that deserves is closer to the proper posture than a reader who has a confident answer in either direction. Because confident answers are capturable. Ambiguity resists capture.

-----

## What Remains

The Bible is the longest, most honest record of human experience in existence. No other text spans this much time with this much self-critical honesty. To read it properly is not to extract a system from it but to let its centuries of compressed observation show you what you cannot see from the vantage point of a single lifetime.

You will never live long enough to watch the cycle of liberation and capture play out ten times across different millennia. You will never live long enough to see, from your own experience alone, that merit does not reliably connect to outcomes — you’ll always be tempted to think your case is the exception, that your hard work really did cause your success, that the system basically works. You will never live long enough to see every reform movement you believe in get captured by administrators and converted into a tool of power.

The text has seen it. Over and over. And it recorded it honestly.

That is why it’s worth reading — not as religion, not as divine command, not as moral program — but as the accumulated observation of a civilization that wrote down what happened and refused to look away. The same reason you read any great literature: because other people’s experience, compressed and honestly rendered, can show you the shape of things that your own brief life never will.

Come to it expecting to be contradicted.

That’s the posture.

That’s the whole thing.
