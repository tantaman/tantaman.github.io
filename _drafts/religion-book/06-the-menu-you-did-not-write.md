# Chapter Six: The Menu You Didn't Write — Grammar, Values, and the Unchosen Self

*You did not choose your current self. Your values are downstream of a grammar you didn't select. And the belief that you could choose differently is the grammar's most sophisticated product.*

---

You were six years old. The teacher held up a sentence.

"The Earth orbits the Sun." She wrote it on the board. *Fact*, she said. Objective. Verifiable. This is what science handles.

"Chocolate ice cream is the best flavor." She wrote that one too. *Opinion*, she said. Subjective. Personal. This is what you handle.

You had no theory of knowledge. You didn't know what a theory of knowledge was. But you had just been given one — a complete framework for sorting every claim in the world into two bins. And you had received it before you were old enough to evaluate it, which meant you never evaluated it. You absorbed it the way you absorbed grammar — not as a belief held consciously, but as the structure through which beliefs became possible.

That binary — fact or opinion, objective or subjective, science or preference — is the foundational epistemological education most people in the modern West will ever receive. Every science unit runs on it. Every essay rubric encodes it. Every standardized test presupposes it. It is never named as a framework. It is never examined as a theory. It is simply *how knowledge works*, installed early enough to feel like common sense.

This chapter is about what it cannot see.

---

## The Exercise

Take some data. Watch what you do with it.

Since 1970, women in the developed world have gained unprecedented access to education, careers, legal rights, reproductive autonomy, and financial independence. By every measurable metric, women's freedom has expanded dramatically.

Are women happier?

You had an expectation before reaching the end of that sentence. That expectation is not from the data — the data hasn't arrived yet. It is from a *story* about what freedom produces, a story you hold but did not choose, a story that determines what the data will appear to show before you see any.

Here is the data: women's self-reported happiness has declined since the 1970s, both in absolute terms and relative to men. This is documented across multiple countries and decades. Researchers have a name for it — the paradox of declining female happiness — and no settled explanation.

Now watch your mind move.

If your formation runs in one direction, you are already reaching for explanations: the revolution is incomplete, structural barriers remain, the mental load of domestic labor was never redistributed, the metrics measured the wrong things. If your formation runs in another direction, you are reaching for different ones: women were better fitted to a different arrangement, the natural structures carry their own logic, some goods don't trade well for freedom.

The data is identical. The explanations are opposite. And both feel, to the person generating them, like *what the data obviously means*.

Add one more data point. Women in traditional religious communities — dense communal structures, defined roles, high fertility — consistently report higher life satisfaction than the secular average.

If your formation treats individual autonomy as the baseline condition of human flourishing, this result requires explaining away. You will reach for "false consciousness" or "adaptive preferences" — the conclusion that this satisfaction doesn't count because it was produced under conditions that shouldn't produce it. Notice: that is not an empirical response. It is a *narrative commitment disguised as one*. It presupposes that satisfaction under constraint is illegitimate, that the only real satisfaction is satisfaction *after* liberation. That presupposition is a story. It arrived before the data. It determined what the data was allowed to mean.

You just watched yourself interpret evidence through a narrative ground you hold but didn't notice selecting. The interpretation preceded the reasoning. The story preceded the analysis.

The fact/opinion binary has no name for what just happened. It wasn't a fact — you can't verify a narrative ground by observation. It wasn't an opinion — it isn't a personal preference, it's a structural commitment about the nature of reality that determined what the evidence appeared to show. It was something else. Something the binary cannot see.

## What the Binary Cannot See

Philosophers have a name for the thing the binary cannot see: the hermeneutic circle. All understanding begins from a prior framework of understanding. You bring questions to the data, and your formation determines which questions you ask, which determines what the data appears to show. There is no unformed knower. There is no view from nowhere.

This is not a fringe position. It is not controversial among people who study how knowledge actually works. It is absent from the education system — not because anyone examined it and rejected it, but because the fact/opinion binary has no category for it. Narrative grounding is not a fact. It is not an opinion. It is the *precondition* for interpreting facts and forming opinions. The binary cannot argue against it. The binary structurally cannot see it.

The difference between a locked room and a room where the concept of "door" has been removed from your vocabulary.

This explains why more information never settles anything. Every public debate in the modern West takes place between people who share this binary and therefore cannot name what they are actually disagreeing about. Both sides think they are arguing about facts. Neither can see that they are arguing about *which narrative makes the facts legible*, because neither has a category for narrative as a mode of knowing. Both present evidence. Neither moves. They are standing on different ground, and the binary makes it impossible to say so. The closest it can produce is "that's just your opinion" — precisely the move that prevents the recognition from landing.

The postmodern response doesn't help either. It names the circle correctly — all knowledge is situated, all interpretation stands on prior ground — and then draws the wrong conclusion: that all grounds are therefore equal. This is the fact/opinion binary in disguise. If narrative grounding isn't a fact, it must be an opinion. And opinions are all equal. So no ground is better than any other.

But this is false. Narratives can be evaluated — not against some narrative-free reality, but against the coherence of the accounts they generate and the accuracy of their predictions. The economists' narrative held that fertility decline was a resource problem: children are expensive, subsidize childcare and births follow. Scandinavian countries ran the experiment. Generous subsidies, paid leave, universal childcare. Fertility kept falling. The narrative predicted something. The prediction failed. That failure is information — not about a fact alone, but about the story that was generating the expectations. A narrative that survives contact with its own predictions is not the same as one that doesn't. Grounds can be better or worse. Refusing to judge between them is not sophistication. It is the fact/opinion binary refusing to acknowledge that it has a third category.

## The Grammar Layer

Below the fact/opinion binary — installed through it, carried by it — is the grammar that determines which values appear available to you at all.

The conventional story about values runs like this: we have experiences, we reflect on those experiences, through reflection we arrive at values, then we find language to express them. Language is the vehicle. Values are the cargo. The person who values "authenticity" first felt something real about the importance of being genuine, then found the word as a useful label for a pre-existing commitment.

This story is backwards.

The sequence runs the other direction: grammar absorption → constrained perception → available values → the feeling of having chosen. You don't first develop values and then find words for them. You absorb a grammar — before you are old enough to evaluate it — and that grammar determines which values appear on your menu.

Consider someone formed in therapy grammar. What values are available to her? Authenticity. Boundaries. Self-care. Growth. Healing. Vulnerability. These feel like hard-won discoveries — the product of years of doing the work. But they are precisely the values therapy grammar makes speakable. They come pre-loaded. Someone formed in corporate grammar has a different menu available: growth, impact, excellence, leadership, innovation. Someone formed in traditional moral grammar has a different menu again: duty, honor, piety, sacrifice, obedience. These are not different words for the same underlying values. They are different values — grammatically available or unavailable depending on the language you were taught to think in.

Therapy grammar cannot express duty as a positive good without distortion. Corporate grammar cannot express sufficiency without it sounding like failure. Traditional moral grammar cannot express "following your authentic self" without it sounding like a vice. The grammars are not translating the same content into different vocabularies. They are generating different content, making different things visible and invisible, producing different human beings in the practitioners who inhabit them.

The mechanism has four stages. The grammar is acquired pre-reflectively, through imitation of high-status speakers, before you are in a position to evaluate it — the way a child acquires an accent. The grammar then shapes perception: you begin to see "opportunities" and "challenges" and "stakeholders" where another grammar would have produced different categories. From within shaped perception, you reflect on what you care about. The reflection is genuine. The agency is genuine. But the menu was fixed before the reflection began. At the end you experience your values as your own, chosen through honest self-examination. The freedom was real. The bars were made of the concepts you were using to see.

This explains something that the conventional story cannot. Why do people in similar social positions tend to hold similar values, apparently independently? Not because they've reasoned from shared experience to shared conclusions. Because they absorbed the same grammar. The corporate professional class shares values — growth, impact, excellence, innovation, leadership — not because each person independently discovered these goods through honest reflection, but because they absorbed the same grammar through the same MBA programs, the same management literature, the same professional formation. Ask someone steeped in that grammar whether "sufficiency" is a value worth organizing a life around and watch the category fail to parse. It isn't prohibited. It's grammatically unavailable. The convergence is not consensus. It is grammatical distribution.

## The Liberal Self's Foundational Error

The person who has read this far may feel, at this point, that the exit is still available. She has learned about her grammar. She can see the menu. She can, in principle, step back, survey the available grammars, and select a better one.

This feeling is the grammar's most sophisticated product.

To evaluate a grammar, you need criteria. Where do those criteria come from? From your current grammar. You are using the values your formation provides to evaluate whether to adopt a different formation. The progressive professional who evaluates traditional moral grammar will find it "oppressive" — because her grammar provides that category. The traditionalist who evaluates therapy grammar will find it "decadent" — because his grammar provides that category. Each grammar supplies the tools to reject the others. The selector stands inside a formation while believing she is standing outside all of them.

Grammar-switching is possible, but it does not happen through careful rational evaluation. It happens through immersion — living among people who speak differently until their grammar becomes yours. It happens through crisis — when your grammar fails to make sense of your experience and you are forced toward another. It happens through conversion — sudden gestalt shifts that feel more like revelation than reasoning. What it does not happen through is the sovereign individual surveying her options and choosing wisely.

This is the liberal self's foundational error. The belief that the individual stands prior to her formation — evaluating it from outside, capable of constructing herself through sovereign reflection — is not a philosophical conclusion she reasoned toward. It is the grammar of liberalism itself, transmitted through Enlightenment epistemology, Protestant inwardness, and Romantic authenticity, absorbed before she could evaluate it. She does not stand outside the cage and assess it. She *is* the formation, experiencing itself as free because freedom is the central value the grammar installed.

The individual exit is grammatically *available* — authentic self-discovery, doing the work, waking up, choosing better values — which is precisely what makes it a trap rather than an exit. An available exit that leads back to the interior of the cage is more effective than a locked door. The locked door at least tells you something true about your situation.

## Below the Grammar

There is a layer beneath the grammar that the grammar itself cannot account for.

Before any concept arrives, before any grammar is absorbed, there is a mode of perception — a way the world shows up — that modern technology installs in the self before language begins. The river doesn't first appear as a river and then get reinterpreted as a resource. It appears as a resource. The forest doesn't first appear as forest and then get calculated as board-feet of lumber. It appears as lumber. The person doesn't first appear as a person and then get evaluated as human capital. She appears as human capital. This is not metaphor. This is the structure of what shows up before reflection begins.

Pull it inward. The achievement subject cannot see herself except as a portfolio to be developed. The therapeutic self cannot see her relationships except as resources to be managed or costs to be cut. The colonization runs in three descending layers: perception is converted into standing-reserve before language arrives; grammar then constrains what values that perception can generate; and only then does the feeling of sovereign reflection begin — the "I" doing the choosing, three levels deep inside a formation it did not construct, experiencing itself as free.

Understanding the layers does not eliminate them. But it changes the character of your habitation.

---

## Colonized All the Way Down

The reader who has followed this argument to its present position may feel something like vertigo. The formation goes all the way down. There is no uncolonized remainder standing behind the grammar and the enframing, waiting to be liberated.

This is correct. And the vertigo is appropriate. But there is a distinction worth preserving before the chapter ends.

Recognizing that the formation runs deep is not the same as concluding that nothing can be said or done within it. You cannot step outside your grammar and choose a better one through rational evaluation — but you can learn to hold your grammar lightly enough to notice the values it cannot express, to ask who benefits from their absence, to recognize that the person who disagrees with you may not be wrong but grammatically equipped with different possibilities. Grammar-switching is possible. It happens. It is just not available through the individual sovereign will that the grammar of liberalism tells you is your most fundamental possession.

You cannot evaluate the fact/opinion binary using the fact/opinion binary — but you can recognize that narrative grounding is a mode of knowing, that you already have a narrative, that the question is whether you will examine it or whether it will continue examining you.

These are not nothing. They are just not exits.

The question that follows is whether the exterior is any different — whether collective political action, if not individual self-revision, can restructure the conditions that produce these selves. The next chapter is shorter than this one. Because every serious political philosophy, examined honestly, has already answered it.
