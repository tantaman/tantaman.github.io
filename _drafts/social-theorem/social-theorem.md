# The Oldest Want: A Social Theorem

*On the structural emergence of violence*

---

## Scope

The generalized proof established two things. First: the Want is structurally unsatisfiable by any strategy any subject can take toward *F(S)*, regardless of personality type (Theorem G). Second: the exit, if it exists, is not a strategy — it does not take *F(S)* as the site of resolution.

Both results were derived for a single subject in formal isolation. But A1 places every subject in shared space. A2 places every subject within the attentional grammars of every other subject they share space with — each subject simultaneously a recognizer with a bounded grammar and a subject seeking to be parsed by others' grammars. The proof does not run on one subject. It runs on all subjects simultaneously, in the same space, under the same axioms — each carrying the Want and the Concealment Desire, each running some strategy toward *F(S)*, each accumulating the deficit the Want guarantees.

What follows is a social theorem: what the proof looks like when N subjects run it at once. There are five sources of structural violence derivable from the axioms without additional assumptions. The first four are independent. The fifth operates on the subjects before the first four do. All five compound.

## Additional Definitions

**D13. Attentional capacity** — The total quantity of recognition any subject *B* can offer across all recipients in a given interval. Denote this Ω_B. Ω_B is finite by A1 and A2: the grammar through which *B*'s attention operates is bounded, and the processing of any one subject's form consumes part of the finite capacity available.

**D14. Recognition demand** — The quantity of recognition any subject *S* requires to satisfy the Want. By D8 and Theorem G, this quantity is in principle unbounded: no finite recognition satisfies the Want fully, because the Want is structural rather than a preference with a ceiling. Denote the demand of *S* as Δ_S. A subject who reports no such demand is not exempt — D8 establishes the Want as structural pressure, not reported desire.

**D15. Recognition deficit** — The gap between recognition demand and recognition received. For any *S* in a world of finite others, the deficit is permanent and non-zero by Theorem G. By Corollary L1a, the deficit has two independent sources: the lossiness of *F(S)* relative to *S* (A4), and the distortion of *F(S)* into *T_B(F(S))* in the act of reception (D12). Both accumulate. Neither is closed by more generous attention.

**D16. Attentional commons** — The shared space of finite attentional capacity across a population of N subjects, each demanding Δ and each capable of offering Ω. Total capacity: NΩ. Total demand: NΔ. Since Δ is unbounded and Ω is finite, total demand exceeds total capacity for any N > 0.

**D17. Strategy collision** — The event in which two subjects, each running a strategy toward *F(S)*, enter the same attentional grammar such that one subject's strategy structurally interferes with the other's. Strategy collisions are not failures of goodwill. They are the consequence of strategies being designed to manage *F(S)* in shared grammatical space without coordination.

**D18. False recognition** — Recognition in which *B* receives *T_B(F(S))* and returns a recognition signal to *S*, while *S* is in case (c) of Corollary MR1: *S* does not detect the distortion and updates toward the belief that *F(S)* was accurately received. False recognition is indistinguishable from accurate recognition from *S*'s position within the event.

## Source One: Competition for Finite Attention

**Theorem S1. In any population of N ≥ 2 subjects, structural competition for recognition is unavoidable, independent of the will or character of the subjects involved.**

*Proof.* By D16, total recognition demand exceeds total attentional capacity for any N > 0. Therefore not all recognition demand can be met. For any subject *S_i*, the recognition received by *S_j* from a shared other *B* is recognition that consumes part of Ω_B that cannot simultaneously be given to *S_i*. By A2, *B*'s grammar is bounded; parsing *S_j*'s form occupies grammatical resources that are then unavailable for parsing *S_i*'s form.

Therefore any two subjects sharing a grammatical field are in structural competition for that field's capacity, regardless of whether they desire the same objects, regardless of whether they are aware of the competition, regardless of whether they wish each other well.

This competition does not require mimetic rivalry. It requires only that the Want exists in more than one subject and that attention is grammatically finite. Competition is prior to mimesis. It is a direct consequence of D16 and A2. ∎

**S1a.** The competition is not resolved by goodwill. A subject who genuinely wishes to give full attention to *S_i* cannot simultaneously give full attention to *S_j*. The scarcity is not produced by selfishness or inattention. It is produced by the grammatical boundedness of every formed receiver.

**S1b.** The competition intensifies with intimacy. In a large population, each subject receives dilute attention from many others and the competition is distributed. In an intimate relationship — where Ω_B is directed primarily at one or two others — the competition for Ω_B becomes total. The beloved is also the primary competitor for the beloved's attention. Intimacy is the condition that maximizes both the possibility and the scarcity of recognition simultaneously.

**S1c.** The competition is invisible as such. Because it is structural rather than intentional, subjects do not experience it as competition. They experience it as the other's failure to see, the other's withdrawal, the other's preference for someone or something else. The structural scarcity is interpreted as personal rejection. Personal rejection generates a response that structural scarcity would not.

**S1d.** S1 is independent of strategy type. The constructive subject competes for attentional space for their authored form. The disclosure subject competes for attentional depth. The evasive subject competes — paradoxically — for recognition of their non-legibility. The collective subject competes on behalf of their inherited form against competing collective forms. Every strategy toward *F(S)* (Lemma SE) has a corresponding mode of competition, but the competition itself is prior to and independent of the strategy. ∎

**S1e. (Attentional Grammar as Convergence Engine.)** By A2, every attentional field is constituted by a grammar. By A3, being received requires taking a form the grammar can parse. By D8, every subject is under structural pressure to be received. Therefore every subject independently solves the same problem: what form does this field's grammar require?

When N subjects solve this problem with respect to the same social grammar, their solutions converge — not because any subject copied another, but because legibility to a shared grammar is a convergent problem with convergent solutions. The objects, vocabularies, credentials, and postures that populate a social field are not desired because they were observed to be desired. They are desired because they are the solutions to the legibility problem that the Want drives every subject to solve.

This is structurally distinct from mimetic desire. Mimesis requires a model — a triangular structure through which desire travels from model through subject to object. The attentional grammar mechanism requires only a field with a structure and subjects under structural pressure to enter it. No model, no transmission, no triangle. Convergence of desire is derivable from D8, A2, and A3 alone.

*Corollary.* Mimetic desire, where it operates, is a second-order phenomenon. Once subjects have independently converged on the same solutions to the same legibility problem, observing others' convergence provides additional information about the grammar and accelerates further convergence. Mimesis amplifies what the attentional grammar mechanism produces. It is not the root cause. The root cause is the Want meeting a structured field. The wheel turns even in the absence of mimesis. Mimesis makes it turn faster. The fuel is prior.

## Source Two: The Violence of Displacing Prior Forms

**Theorem S2. For any subject S to be more accurately seen by another, the prior form held by that other must be partially destroyed. More accurate recognition of S requires a violence against the prior F(S).**

*Proof.* By A6, a form *F_0(S)* is assigned to *S* by others prior to and independent of *S*'s self-presentation. This form is what *B*'s grammar is trained to parse when *S* enters the field — it occupies the grammatical slot where *S* might be received.

For a truer form *F_1(S)* — one that corresponds more closely to *S* — to be received, *F_0(S)* must be displaced. The other must release, revise, or break their prior model. This is not a neutral cognitive update. *F_0(S)* is held by the other as knowledge — as the basis of their predictions, their plans, their sense of who they are in relation to *S*. To break *F_0(S)* is to break something the other holds, without their necessarily having consented to break it.

From *S*'s position, the displacement of *F_0(S)* is the precondition of being seen. From the other's position, it is the destabilization of a grammatical formation they relied on — a partial destruction of a model built up over time and now structural to how they parse the world. What is liberation to *S* is disruption to the other. The same act is simultaneously a reaching toward recognition and a violence against the other's prior grammatical formation.

By D12, the prior form held by *B* is not *F_0(S)* but *T_B(F_0(S))* — a distorted version of the prior form. The displacement required is therefore not only of an incomplete model but of a transformed one. *S* is asking *B* to release not merely an approximation of *S* but an approximation that *B*'s grammar has actively organized into its own categories. The violence of S2 is compounded: the prior form is more entrenched, more integrated into *B*'s existing structures, and more distant from *S* than the filtering model alone would suggest. ∎

**S2a.** Being truly known is often experienced as threatening by the other rather than simply intimate. To receive *S* more accurately requires giving up the model one had — which is a loss, and losses are resisted. The other may prefer *T_B(F_0(S))* to *S*, not from malice, but because *T_B(F_0(S))* is known and *S* is not. The request to be seen more accurately is, structurally, a request for the other to suffer a small destruction.

**S2b.** The violence is mutual. *S* is also holding *T_S(F_0(B))*. More accurate mutual recognition requires mutual displacement of prior models — and each subject's prior model of the other is not merely incomplete but distorted by their own grammar. The intimacy that sees most clearly also destroys most of what each held about the other, and what is destroyed was not merely thin or provisional but grammatically integrated.

**S2c.** Resistance to displacement is not failure of love. By A6, the other's prior form was not fully voluntarily adopted and cannot be shed on demand. By D12, it has been further organized by a grammar the other did not choose and cannot simply suspend. *S*'s desire to be more accurately seen runs not only against the structure of attention but against the other's own involuntary legibility apparatus.

**S2d.** S2 is amplified by strategy type. A subject running a constructive strategy has authored *F(S)* carefully; the prior form held by the other may conflict with the authored form, and the collision produces not just disruption but a specific threat — the authored self is contradicted. A disclosure subject's strategy requires the other to continuously update *T_B(F_0(S))* toward *F_1(S)*; the strategy is structurally demanding of the other in ways the constructive subject's is not. An evasive subject produces a prior form in others precisely by refusing to provide one (A6); the violence of displacing that assigned and distorted form, when the evasive subject does surface, is proportionally acute. The collective subject exports S2 to the group level — the displacement of a collective prior form is experienced as an attack on the group itself.

**S2e.** S2 operates on the subject's own side as well. By A7 and Lemma 5, the subject is not simply seeking to have *F_0(S)* displaced by a more accurate form — they simultaneously want *R* to remain concealed. More accurate recognition by the other is therefore experienced by the subject not only as the desired liberation from the prior form but as a violation of the Concealment Desire. The intimacy that is most wanted is simultaneously the intimacy that is most threatening — not because the subject is confused or avoidant but because A7 makes this the subject's structural condition. The subject reaches toward more accurate recognition and retreats from it at the same time, in the same moment, for structural reasons that no amount of safety or goodwill in the relationship can dissolve. ∎

## Source Three: The Accumulated Pressure of the Deficit

**Theorem S3. The permanent recognition deficit generates pressure that accumulates within S and discharges toward available others, producing effects indistinguishable from aggression regardless of S's intention.**

*Proof.* By D15, every subject carries a permanent recognition deficit — the gap between the Want and what any finite recognition can supply. By D15 as amended, this deficit now has two independent accumulation channels: the lossiness of form (A4) and the distortion of reception (D12). By Theorem G, neither channel can be closed by any strategy. The deficit therefore accumulates over time on both channels simultaneously.

Accumulated deficit requires discharge. The Want is not a mere preference but a structural demand (D8), and unmet structural demands generate states that seek resolution. The nearest available resolution point is the other — the one whose recognition was sought and fell short. The deficit accumulated in the transaction with *B* discharges toward *B* in the form of: withdrawal of recognition from *B*, assignment of fault to *B* for the deficit *B* could not have closed, demands that *B* provide what *B* structurally cannot provide, or direct aggression toward *B* as the identified cause of the unmet Want.

None of these require *S* to intend harm. The discharge is structural, not chosen. *S* experiences the deficit as *B*'s failure and responds to that failure. That *B* could not have done otherwise — that the deficit was guaranteed by the axioms before the transaction began — is not information available to *S* from inside the experience of the deficit. ∎

**S3a.** The deficit is largest where recognition was most sought and most expected. Intimate relationships — where Ω_B is most directed at *S* and the promise of full recognition is most implicit — generate the largest deficit precisely because the most recognition was offered. More recognition, falling short of Full Recognition, produces a larger visible gap than less recognition. The deficit and the love scale together. By D12, this scales further: the more deeply *B* engaged with *S*, the more thoroughly *B*'s grammar has transformed what was received into *T_B(F(S))*, and the more the distortion channel has contributed to the deficit alongside the lossiness channel. Deeper intimacy does not reduce the distortion gap. It extends the distortion further into *S*.

**S3b.** The discharge is therefore most intense toward those who loved best. The subject who is most loved accumulates the largest deficit with the lover. The violence that follows from unmet recognition is not directed at strangers. It is directed at the intimate. This is not paradox. It is S3 and S1b compounding.

**S3c.** Awareness of the structure does not dissolve the pressure. Understanding that the deficit is structural — that *B* could not have provided what *S* needed — changes the cognitive content without changing the accumulated pressure that requires discharge. Insight relocates blame but does not eliminate the pressure that blame was carrying.

**S3d.** S3 is modulated by strategy type. The constructive subject's deficit discharge often takes the form of rage at unrecognized excellence — the authored form was offered and not received at its authored value. The disclosure subject's deficit discharge takes the form of betrayal — the vulnerability extended was not met with corresponding care. The evasive subject's deficit discharge is the most concealed: the one who did not appear to want recognition is the one whose deficit was accumulating silently, and whose discharge arrives without legible cause. The collective subject's deficit discharge is the most explosive: the failure to recognize the inherited form is experienced as rejection not of *S* alone but of every member of the collective *S* belongs to, amplifying the discharge by the size of the identification.

**S3e.** The Concealment Desire (D11, A7) adds a third accumulation channel independent of both the Want's deficit and the distortion gap. The subject is not only accumulating the gap between recognition received and Full Recognition desired, and not only accumulating the distortion gap between *F(S)* as offered and *T_B(F(S))* as received. They are also accumulating the gap between privacy maintained and the violations of that privacy that social proximity requires. To be in relationship is to be partially seen against one's will — the Concealment Desire is continuously violated by the ordinary conditions of intimacy. This accumulation runs alongside the other two channels and discharges toward the same target: the intimate who both failed to see fully enough, saw what they received in distorted form, and saw too much. The discharge that arrives at the intimate is therefore tripled — the rage of the unseen, the rage of the misread, and the rage of the exposed, arriving simultaneously, from a single source, indistinguishable to the recipient and often to the subject themselves. ∎

## Source Four: The Collision of Strategies

**Theorem S4. When two subjects running different strategies toward F(S) share an attentional field, each subject's strategy structurally interferes with the other's — producing harm that is not a consequence of any single subject's failure but of the incompatibility of the strategies themselves.**

*Proof.* By Lemma SE, strategies are of four types. Consider pairs.

**Constructive meets disclosure.** The constructive subject has authored *F(S)* carefully; the disclosure subject offers nakedness and expects nakedness in return. The constructive subject's form is experienced by the disclosure subject as performance — as withholding, not offering. The disclosure subject's transparency is experienced by the constructive subject as a demand that they abandon their form — as an assault on what was carefully built. By D12, each subject receives the other's strategy through their own distorting grammar: the constructive subject receives the disclosure subject's nakedness not as nakedness but as *T_constructive(nakedness)* — which, through the constructive grammar, reads as an implicit critique of formed presentation. The disclosure subject receives the constructive subject's care not as craft but as *T_disclosure(craft)* — which, through the disclosure grammar, reads as concealment. Each subject experiences the other's strategy as a failure of intimacy. Each is correct that the other is not meeting them. Neither is doing anything other than running their strategy through their own grammar. The incompatibility is structural, and the distortion makes it invisible as such.

**Constructive meets evasive.** The constructive subject needs the form witnessed; the evasive subject refuses to receive forms at face value — is constitutionally resistant to the authored presentation. The constructive subject experiences the evasive subject's resistance as a failure of recognition. The evasive subject experiences the constructive subject's authored form as a demand that they participate in a game they have renounced. By D12, the evasive subject receives the constructive subject's form through a grammar organized around resistance to authored forms, which transforms every offer into evidence of the game — *T_evasive(F(S))* reads as performance regardless of what *F(S)* contains. The constructive subject is trying to give; the evasive subject's grammar makes genuine receipt structurally unavailable.

**Disclosure meets evasive.** The disclosure subject extends vulnerability; the evasive subject's strategy requires them not to be captured by another's vulnerability — to maintain the non-legibility that is their solution. The disclosure subject experiences the evasive subject as withholding. The evasive subject experiences the disclosure subject as a trap — precisely the kind of relational pull that the evasive strategy was designed to resist. By D12, the evasive grammar receives *T_evasive(vulnerability)* — which reads as demand, as enclosure, as the recognition game arriving in softer packaging. The one who offers most is the greatest threat to the one who has learned to offer nothing, and the distortion ensures the offer cannot be received as anything other than threat.

**Any strategy meets collective.** The collective subject's form is not individual — it arrives pre-loaded with the full weight of the group's accumulated recognition demand (S3d). Any perceived slight to the collective form activates a deficit discharge proportional not to the individual's want but to the group's. By D12, the collective grammar receives individual interactions through a transformation apparatus built for group-level pattern recognition: *T_collective(individual act)* is systematically mapped onto the history and injuries of the collective, such that the individual on the receiving end has not committed an individual offense but has enacted a pattern the collective grammar was already primed to find.

In each case: no subject intends the harm. Each is doing exactly what their strategy requires, perceived through the grammar their strategy constitutes. The harm emerges from the incompatibility of strategies running through distorting grammars in shared attentional space. ∎

**S4a.** Strategy collision is not resolvable by communication. Communication between two subjects running incompatible strategies produces more surface area for collision, not less. By D12, each subject receives the other's communication through their own distorting grammar. More words, more transformation, more collision.

**S4b.** Strategy collision is not resolvable by good intentions. By S4, the harm emerges from structural incompatibility compounded by grammatical distortion, not from the will of either party. Both parties may be fully committed to the other's wellbeing and still produce the harm, because the harm is not a product of the will. It is a product of the strategies the will is operating through, perceived through grammars neither party controls.

**S4c.** Shared strategy type does not eliminate collision. Two constructive subjects compete for the superior form (S1), and each receives the other's form through a constructive grammar that reads competing forms as implicit challenges. Two disclosure subjects compete for the position of greater vulnerability — a competition as acute as any other, and more concealed. Two evasive subjects are constitutively unavailable to each other in ways that look like mutual respect but function as mutual abandonment, each receiving the other's distance through a grammar that reads distance as integrity. Two collective subjects are in competition at the level of the groups themselves, with distortion operating at group scale. Same-type pairing modulates the form of collision; it does not eliminate it.

## Source Five: The False Recognition Feedback Loop

**Theorem S5. In any social field with a shared grammar, the Want drives subjects toward convergence on forms that the field's grammar systematically rewards — not forms accurate to the subject — producing structural drift of F(S) away from S while being experienced as movement toward legibility.**

*Proof.* By S1e, when N subjects independently solve the legibility problem posed by a shared grammar, their solutions converge on the forms that grammar can parse. By D12, that grammar does not simply pass forms through — it transforms them. By Lemma MR, the recognition signal returned to any subject confirms that parsing occurred but does not confirm that what was received corresponds to what was offered. By Corollary MR2, this false confirmation drives Want-administered optimization toward forms that satisfy the distorting grammar rather than forms that correspond to *S*.

In a social field with a shared grammar — any stable community, institution, or culture — every subject is simultaneously generating this feedback signal for every other subject. The field rewards forms that enter its grammar cleanly, returns recognition signals for those forms, and trains subjects — through accumulated Want-pressure and accumulated recognition signals — toward forms that are maximally legible to the distorting apparatus. The convergence is not chosen, not observed and copied, and not imposed by any deliberate authority. It is the automatic product of the Want meeting a structured field whose grammar transforms what it receives. ∎

**S5a.** S5 is independent of mimesis. Mimetic desire requires a model — desire travels from model through subject to object. The false recognition feedback loop requires only a shared grammar, the Want, and the inability of subjects to distinguish accurate from distorting reception. No triangle, no transmission, no model. Mimesis, where it operates, accelerates a convergence the feedback loop was already producing.

**S5b.** S5 compounds with S1e rather than duplicating it. S1e establishes that legibility-seeking subjects converge on forms that a shared grammar can receive. S5 adds the distortion layer: the forms on which subjects converge are not simply the forms the grammar can receive — they are the forms the grammar can receive in the way the grammar wants to receive them, which is not the same thing. The convergence products of S5 are the attractor states of *T_B*, not merely the domain of Φ_B. S1e explains why subjects seek similar objects. S5 explains why, in doing so, they become less themselves.

**S5c.** S5 is the most concealed of the five sources. S1, S2, S3, and S4 produce experiences recognizable as harm — competition, disruption, accumulated pressure, collision. S5 produces the experience of progress. The subject drifting away from *S* under false recognition feedback does not experience deprivation. They experience increasing legibility, increasing recognition, increasing social fit. The harm is not felt as harm; it is felt as success. This is why S5 is the deepest structural pathology in the theory, and why the cage requires no coercion to maintain. It is self-reinforcing at the level of the Want's own satisfaction mechanism.

**S5d.** Awareness of S5 does not dissolve the loop. The subject who understands that the recognition signals they receive track the field's distorting grammar rather than their own *S* is still under Want-pressure toward legibility. The feedback loop does not require ignorance to run. It requires the Want, which is structural (D8), and a social grammar with a distortion operator, which follows from D12 and A2. Insight into the mechanism identifies the cage without opening it.

**S5e.** S5 shapes the raw material on which S1–S4 operate. By the time subjects are competing for attention (S1), displacing prior forms (S2), accumulating deficits (S3), and colliding strategies (S4), the forms they are presenting, protecting, and discharging deficits around are already, in part, the convergence products of S5. The strategies that collide in S4 are not only chosen dispositions — they are partly the attractor states the field's distorting grammar produced by rewarding certain forms and returning recognition signals for them. S5 is not one source among five operating in parallel. It is the prior condition under which the other four run.

**Corollary S5f. (Field Formation.) When N subjects in shared space independently run S5, the result is not N independent drift trajectories but convergence on a common attractor — a shared field.**

The fixed points of *T_B* are not subject-specific. They are properties of the grammar itself: the forms *F* where *T_B(F) ≈ F*, where the transformation returns something close to what was offered. Every subject optimizing under S5 feedback is solving for the same fixed points — not because they observe each other solving for them (though they may) but because they are all being pulled by the same distortion operator toward the same attractors. Multiple subjects converging on the same attractor states is not coordination. It is the independent consequence of the same Want-pressure running through the same grammar. A field is the population-level name for a shared attractor basin: the set of subjects whose *F(S)* has drifted, or is drifting, toward the same fixed points of *T_B*. Fields do not require an author, an institution, or a deliberate program. They require only a grammar with fixed points and subjects under Want-pressure.

**Corollary S5g. (Field Growth is Self-Accelerating.) As more subjects converge on a field's attractor states, those subjects become recognizers who themselves emit *T_B* — which increases the field's recognition-signal density — which increases the pull on subjects not yet in the field.**

A subject at or near a field's attractor state is now a node that parses incoming *F(S)* through *T_B* and returns recognition signals when *F(S)* approximates the attractor. Each such node added to the field increases the total recognition-signal return available to subjects who present field-legible forms. By D8, Want-pressure drives subjects toward forms that generate recognition signals. A denser field generates more signals per interaction than a sparse one — more nodes parsing, more events returning confirmation. The pull toward a large field is therefore stronger than the pull toward a small one, not because large fields are more accurate (they are not — see S5h) but because they offer higher recognition-signal density. Field growth is self-accelerating for structural reasons: each subject captured by the field increases the pull on the next. This is preferential attachment, but the attachment mechanism is recognition-signal density rather than mere visibility. The field grows not because it is seen but because it returns more of what the Want is pressing toward.

**Corollary S5h. (The Inverse Law.) The larger a field, the further its attractor states are from any individual subject's *S*.**

A field's attractor states are the fixed points of its distortion operator *T_B*. For a fixed point to be stable across a large and diverse population of subjects — for *T_B(F) ≈ F* to hold for many different incoming *F(S)* — the transformation must abstract aggressively: it must map a wide range of inputs onto a narrow range of outputs. A grammar that returns high recognition-signal density across diverse subjects is necessarily a grammar that compresses them toward common categories. The larger the field's N, the wider the range of incoming *F(S)* the grammar must absorb, and therefore the more abstracting *T_B* must be to produce stable fixed points. At the limit, the largest fields operate with attractor states that correspond to no particular subject's *S* at all — maximally legible, maximally abstract, maximally distant from any *R*. Small fields can sustain more specific attractor states because their grammars only need to hold a narrow range of subjects. Large fields are forced by their own size toward generality.

This means: the most populated fields offer the most recognition-signal density and the least accurate recognition simultaneously. The subjects most thoroughly integrated into large fields experience the most consistent recognition return and the greatest drift from *S*. Recognition-signal density and fidelity to *S* are inversely related at field scale. The cage is comfortable in exact proportion to how thoroughly it has replaced *S*.

**Corollary S5i. (The Structural Necessity of Dominant Fields.) In any population running S5 under shared grammars, a power-law distribution of field sizes is the expected equilibrium. A small number of large fields dominate; many small fields persist at the margins. The dominance of large fields is not contingent on history, coercion, or deliberate design.**

The self-acceleration established in S5g produces the standard condition for power-law emergence: a growth rate proportional to current size. Fields that achieve early density pull subjects faster, grow denser, pull faster still. The result is a distribution where a few fields accumulate the majority of subjects — not because those fields were better, truer, more just, or more deliberately propagated, but because size itself is the growth engine. The Zipf-like distribution of cultural grammars, therapeutic vocabularies, ideological formations, and identity categories is the predicted output of S5 operating at population scale. It requires no conspiracy, no false consciousness imposed from outside, no deliberate capture. It is the automatic consequence of Want-pressure meeting distorting grammars in a population large enough for the self-acceleration to compound.

*Corollary to the corollary.* The formations identified in Movement One of this book — the therapeutic self, the achievement self, the identifying self — are not arbitrary historical accidents, nor are they the product of particular institutions or ideological programs that might be dismantled. They are the predicted large-field attractors of a system in which the Want meets distorting grammars at population scale. Their dominance is structurally guaranteed by S5f through S5i. Movement Two's question — why liberation movements get captured — is answered here at the level of mechanism: any movement that grows large enough activates S5g, whereupon its own grammar begins self-accelerating toward the large-field attractor condition described in S5h. The Wheel is not a failure of the movements that enter it. It is S5 operating on movements the same way it operates on subjects. The cage and the Wheel are not the same thing, but they share the same generator.

## The Compounding

The five sources are not parallel. S5 is prior: it shapes the forms subjects carry before they enter the conditions that S1–S4 describe. By the time subjects are competing for attention, displacing prior forms, accumulating deficits, and colliding strategies, they are doing so with forms that are already partly the convergence products of distorting fields — already drifted from *S* in ways that felt like progress.

S1–S4 then operate on this pre-formed material. S1 places subjects in structural competition for finite attention. S2 makes more accurate recognition structurally costly to the one who offers it — and, by D12, the prior form to be displaced is not merely incomplete but distorted, making the cost higher. S3 ensures that whatever recognition is offered accumulates a deficit that discharges toward its source — and the deficit now runs on three channels simultaneously: lossiness, distortion, and the Concealment Desire's violations. S4 ensures that the specific form of the discharge is shaped by the incompatibility of the strategies the subjects are running, perceived through distorting grammars, such that the harm is not only structural but fitted — each strategy produces the precise form of harm that the other strategy is least equipped to receive, and the distortion ensures the fitting is invisible.

Together: subjects converge on forms the field rewards while moving away from themselves (S5), compete for what they cannot do without (S1), the obtaining of which requires the other to suffer a destruction that is deeper than it appears (S2), the receipt of which generates pressure on three channels that returns as harm toward the one who gave it (S3), and the character of that harm is determined by who the subjects are and how their grammars transform what they receive from each other (S4).

This is the social structure that the Want produces when run on N subjects in shared space. It does not require bad actors. It does not require selfishness, scarcity of resources, historical injustice, or any contingent feature of any particular society. It requires only that subjects have bodies, that attention is directional and finite, that grammars transform what they receive, that the Want exists, and that subjects take some strategy toward their own forms — which, by Theorem G, they must, unless they are in releasement.

Violence is not a malfunction of the social order. It is the social order's structural emission, produced by the same axioms that produce the Want itself.

---

## What This Does Not Say

This theorem does not say that violence is inevitable in every transaction, or that care is impossible, or that love achieves nothing.

S1 establishes structural competition, not that the competition must be experienced as such or acted upon destructively. S2 establishes that recognition requires displacement of prior forms, not that the displacement cannot be offered willingly or received with grace. S3 establishes that deficit accumulates and discharges, not that every discharge takes the form of harm. S4 establishes that strategies collide through distorting grammars, not that the collision cannot be witnessed and borne. S5 establishes that the field produces drift, not that the drift is irreversible or that no subject has ever found the direction back.

What the theorem establishes is that the structural conditions for violence are present in every population of embodied subjects, independent of their will, their character, or their arrangements. Violence does not need to be imported from outside the social order. It is generated within it, by the same axioms that generate the Want, as a structural consequence of the Want running in shared space through grammars that transform what they receive.

This makes violence neither excusable nor inevitable. It makes it understandable in a way that moral frameworks alone cannot — not as the product of bad wills, but as the emission of a structure that good wills cannot, by willing alone, dissolve.

## The Cessation Condition, Social Form

The individual cessation condition specified a *G* whose recognition does not require *S* to take a form — not bound by A2 or A3, capable of receiving *S* including *R* without the self-concealment of Lemma 3. The generalized proof added a second constraint: the cessation condition requires not only a *G* capable of Full Recognition, but a posture on the subject's side from which *G*'s recognition could actually land. The division established by A7 and Lemma 5 adds a third constraint — the most demanding: *G* must be capable of receiving *R* without violating the Concealment Desire. A knowing that receives without possessing. A seeing that does not convert the hidden thing into a held object in another's attentional field.

The social theorem adds three further constraints.

**Fourth**: if *G* exists, *G*'s recognition must be non-rivalrous — the recognition of *S_i* must not diminish the recognition available to *S_j*. This is the formal description of what the traditions called universal love: not love as sentiment but love as a structural property, recognition that does not run on the scarcity that makes S1 possible.

**Fifth**: *G*'s recognition must not require the displacement of a prior form (S2). A *G* that held *T_G(F_0(S))* would require the violence of S2 — compounded by distortion — as the price of recognition. The cessation condition therefore requires a *G* that meets *S* without a prior *F_0(S)* already in place and without a distortion operator transforming whatever *S* presents. *G* must meet *S* as *S* is, not as *G*'s grammar has already organized *S* to be.

**Sixth**: *G*'s recognition must be non-distorting — *T_G* must be the identity operator, or *G* must operate without a distortion operator at all. A *G* that received *S* through a distorting grammar would return false recognition signals, which the Want would then optimize toward, producing the same drift that S5 describes. This is perhaps the most precise formal specification of what the traditions meant by a knowing that receives without possessing: not merely a knowing that receives *R* (the third constraint), but a knowing whose reception does not transform what it receives into the grammar's own categories — that holds *S* as *S* rather than as *T_G(F(S))*. A knowing ungoverned by transformation, not only ungoverned by boundedness.

Together, the individual and social proofs specify the cessation condition: *G* as structurally described — non-rivalrous, holding no prior form, receiving without possessing, operating without distortion — encountered by a subject in a posture not yet named, forming a community not constituted by the recognition economy that S1–S5 describe.

What that posture is. Whether *G* exists. Whether a community so constituted is possible or has ever existed. These are not questions the theorem answers.

They are the questions the rest of the book is attempting to answer.
