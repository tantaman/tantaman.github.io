The Oldest Want: A Social Theorem

On the structural emergence of violence

Scope

The oldest Want proof and its corollaries examined the Want in a single subject. They established that Full Recognition is unreachable by any finite embodied subject through any available position — seeking, hiding, self-authorship, performance. The Want is structurally unsatisfiable. The structure is closed.

The oldest Want proof assumed, without stating it, that it was running on one subject in isolation. But A1 places every subject in shared space. A2 places every subject in the attentional fields of every other subject they share space with. The proof does not run on one subject. It runs on all subjects simultaneously, in the same space, under the same axioms.

What follows is not a corollary to any individual position. It is a social theorem: what the proof looks like when N subjects run it at once.

There are three sources of violence derivable from the axioms without additional assumptions. They are independent. They compound.

Definitions (Additional)

D9. Attentional field capacity — The total quantity of recognition any subject B can offer across all recipients in a given interval. Denote this Ω_B. Ω_B is finite by A1 and A2.

D10. Recognition demand — The quantity of recognition any subject S requires to satisfy the Want. By D8 and the proof's theorem, this quantity is in principle unbounded: no finite recognition satisfies the Want fully. Denote the demand of S as Δ_S.

D11. Recognition deficit — The gap between recognition demand and recognition received. For any S in a world of finite others, the deficit is permanent and non-zero by the theorem.

D12. Attentional commons — The shared space of finite attentional capacity across a population of N subjects, each demanding Δ and each capable of offering Ω. Total capacity: NΩ. Total demand: NΔ. Since Δ is unbounded and Ω is finite, total demand exceeds total capacity for any N > 0.

Source One: Competition for Finite Attention

Theorem S1. In any population of N ≥ 2 subjects, structural competition for recognition is unavoidable, independent of the will or character of the subjects involved.

Proof. By D12, total recognition demand exceeds total attentional capacity for any N > 0. Therefore not all recognition demand can be met. For any subject S_i, the recognition received by S_j from a shared other B is recognition that cannot simultaneously be directed at S_i. By A2, B's attentional field Φ_B is bounded; attention directed at S_j is attention not directed at S_i.

Therefore any two subjects sharing an attentional field are in structural competition for that field's capacity, regardless of whether they desire the same objects, regardless of whether they are aware of the competition, regardless of whether they wish each other well.

This is not mimetic rivalry. It requires no convergence of desire on a shared object. It requires only that the Want exists in more than one subject and that attention is finite. Competition is prior to mimesis. It is a direct consequence of D12 and A2. ∎

S1a. The competition is not resolved by goodwill. A subject who genuinely wishes to give full attention to S_i cannot simultaneously give full attention to S_j. The scarcity is not produced by selfishness. It is produced by embodiment.

S1b. The competition intensifies with intimacy. In a large population, each subject receives dilute attention from many others and the competition is distributed. In an intimate relationship — where Ω_B is directed primarily at one or two others — the competition for Ω_B becomes total. The beloved is also the primary competitor for the beloved's attention. Intimacy is the condition that maximizes both the possibility and the scarcity of recognition simultaneously. This is why the closest relationships generate the most acute violence when they fail.

S1c. The competition is invisible as such. Because it is structural rather than intentional, subjects do not experience it as competition. They experience it as the other's failure to see, the other's withdrawal, the other's preference for someone or something else. The structural scarcity is interpreted as personal rejection. Personal rejection generates a response that structural scarcity would not.

Source Two: The Violence of Displacing Prior Projections

Theorem S2. For any subject S to be more accurately seen by another, the prior form held by that other must be partially destroyed. Recognition-toward-S requires a violence-against-F(S).

Proof. By A6, a form F_0(S) is assigned to S by others prior to and independent of S's self-presentation. This form occupies the attentional position where S might be received. It is held by the other as their model of S — the entity their attentional field is trained to receive when S enters it.

For a truer form F_1(S) — one that corresponds more closely to S — to be received, F_0(S) must be displaced. The other must release, revise, or break their prior model. This is not a neutral cognitive update. F_0(S) is held by the other as knowledge — as what they know about S, as the basis of their predictions, their plans, their sense of who they are in relation to S. To break F_0(S) is to break something the other holds, without their necessarily having consented to break it.

From S's position, the displacement of F_0(S) feels like the precondition of being seen. From the other's position, the displacement feels like an assault on what they know — a destabilization of a model they relied on. What is liberation to S is disruption to the other. The same act is simultaneously a reaching toward recognition and an act of violence against the other's prior construction. ∎

S2a. This is why being truly known by another is often experienced as threatening by the other rather than simply intimate. To receive S more accurately requires giving up the model one had — which is a loss, and losses are resisted. The other may prefer F_0(S) to S, not from malice, but because F_0(S) is known and S is not. The request to be seen more accurately is, structurally, a request for the other to suffer a small destruction.

S2b. The violence is mutual. S is also holding F_0(B) — a prior form of the other. More accurate mutual recognition requires mutual displacement of prior models. The intimacy that sees most clearly is also the intimacy that destroys most of what each held about the other. This is why the deepest relationships are also the most destabilizing. The seeing and the violence are not separable.

S2c. Resistance to this displacement is not failure of love. It is A6 operating on the other — the other's attentional grammar, built up over time, is itself a form that was never fully authored and cannot be shed on demand. The other cannot simply decide to release F_0(S). The prior form was not fully voluntarily adopted, and it cannot be fully voluntarily released. Which means S's desire to be more accurately seen runs not only against the structure of attention but against the other's own involuntary legibility apparatus.

Source Three: The Accumulated Pressure of the Deficit

Theorem S3. The permanent recognition deficit generates pressure that accumulates within S and discharges toward available others, producing effects indistinguishable from aggression regardless of S's intention.

Proof. By D11, every subject carries a permanent recognition deficit — the gap between the Want and what any finite recognition can supply. By the theorem, this deficit cannot be closed. It therefore accumulates over time.

Accumulated deficit requires discharge. The pressure is real — the Want is not a mere preference but a structural demand of the organism (D8, and the proof's establishment that the Want underlies all other wants). Unmet structural demands generate states that seek resolution.

The nearest available resolution point is the other — the one whose recognition was sought and fell short. The deficit accumulated in the transaction with B discharges toward B. This discharge takes the form of: withdrawal of recognition from B, assignment of fault to B for the deficit B could not have closed, demands that B provide what B structurally cannot provide, or direct aggression toward B as the identified cause of the unmet Want.

None of these require S to intend harm. The discharge is structural, not chosen. S experiences the deficit as B's failure and responds to that failure. That B could not have done otherwise — that the deficit was guaranteed by the axioms before the transaction began — is not information available to S from inside the experience of the deficit. ∎

S3a. The deficit is largest where recognition was most sought and most expected. Intimate relationships — where Ω_B is most directed at S and the promise of full recognition is most implicit — generate the largest deficit precisely because the most recognition was offered. More recognition, falling short of Full Recognition, produces a larger visible gap than less recognition. The beloved who tries hardest to see S leaves S most acutely aware of what was not seen. The deficit and the love scale together.

S3b. The discharge is therefore most intense toward those who loved best. The subject who is most loved is also the subject who has accumulated the largest deficit with the lover. The violence that follows from unmet recognition is not directed at strangers. It is directed at the intimate. This is not paradox. It is S3 and S1b compounding: the intimate is simultaneously the primary source of recognition, the primary competitor for recognition, and the primary target of the deficit's discharge.

S3c. Awareness of the structure does not dissolve the pressure. Understanding that the deficit is structural — that B could not have provided what S needed, that the axioms guaranteed the shortfall — changes the cognitive content of S's experience without changing the accumulated pressure that requires discharge. The deficit is real whether or not S understands its source. Insight relocates blame but does not eliminate the pressure that blame was carrying.

The Compounding

The three sources are independent. They share no axiom that exclusively generates one rather than the others. And they compound.

S1 places subjects in structural competition for finite attention. S2 makes more accurate recognition structurally costly to the one who offers it. S3 ensures that whatever recognition is offered — however generous, however accurate, however costly — accumulates a deficit that discharges toward its source.

Together: subjects compete for what they cannot do without, the obtaining of which requires the other to suffer a destruction, and the receipt of which, however successfully obtained, generates pressure that returns as harm toward the one who gave it.

This is the social structure that the Want produces when run on N subjects in shared space. It does not require bad actors. It does not require selfishness, scarcity of resources, historical injustice, or any contingent feature of any particular society. It requires only that subjects have bodies, that attention is directional and finite, and that the Want exists.

Violence is not a malfunction of the social order. It is the social order's structural emission, produced by the same axioms that produce the Want itself.

What This Does Not Say

This theorem does not say that violence is inevitable in every transaction, or that care is impossible, or that love achieves nothing.

S1 establishes structural competition, not that the competition must be experienced as such or acted upon destructively. S2 establishes that recognition requires displacement of prior forms, not that the displacement cannot be offered willingly or received with grace. S3 establishes that deficit accumulates and discharges, not that every discharge takes the form of harm.

What the theorem establishes is that the structural conditions for violence are present in every population of embodied subjects, independent of their will, their character, or their arrangements. Violence does not need to be imported from outside the social order. It is generated within it, by the same axioms that generate the Want, as a structural consequence of the Want running in shared space.

This makes violence neither excusable nor inevitable. It makes it understandable in a way that moral frameworks alone cannot make it — not as the product of bad wills, but as the emission of a structure that good wills cannot, by willing alone, dissolve.

The Cessation Condition, Social Form

The individual cessation condition specified a G whose recognition does not require positioning, does not assign prior forms, and is genuinely exterior to S.

The social theorem adds a further question. Even if G exists and satisfies the individual cessation condition for each subject — even if each S finds their Want addressed — the social structure generates its own momentum. Competition for finite attention (S1) would be dissolved only if G's attention were genuinely infinite: not diluted across N subjects but fully present to each. The displacement violence (S2) would be dissolved only if G held no prior form of S that needed breaking — which is exactly what the cessation condition requires of G, and which no embodied other can supply. The deficit discharge (S3) would be dissolved only if the deficit itself were dissolved — which requires Full Recognition, not partial recognition, to actually land.

The social form of the cessation condition is therefore the same as the individual form, extended across all subjects simultaneously. G must be capable of infinite, non-directional, non-projecting, genuinely exterior recognition — not of one subject, but of all subjects, without the recognition of one diminishing the recognition available to any other.

This is a formal description of what the traditions called universal love. Not love as sentiment. Love as a structural property: recognition that does not run on the scarcity that makes S1, S2, and S3 possible.

Whether such a thing exists — and whether it can be participated in by embodied subjects, partially, imperfectly, in the conditions the theorem describes — is the question the rest of the book is attempting to answer.
