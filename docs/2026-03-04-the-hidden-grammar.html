The Grammar That Teaches Grammar

Published 2026-03-04 · T
philosophyculturegroundknowledgepoweressay

    We teach children three ways of knowing: scientific method, direct experience, reason and logic. These feel complete. They feel like the whole map.

    There's a fourth. It's been hidden — not deliberately, but structurally. Hidden by the very framework we use to recognize what counts as knowledge.

    You learned that framework in first grade. The teacher held up a sentence and asked: is this a fact or an opinion?

    "The Earth orbits the Sun." Fact. "Chocolate ice cream is the best flavor." Opinion.

    You were six. You had no theory of knowledge. You didn't know what a theory of knowledge was. But you had just been given one — a complete framework for sorting all claims into two bins:

    Facts. Objective. Verifiable. The domain of science. Opinions. Subjective. Personal. The domain of you.

    That's the entire epistemological education most people will ever receive. Everything that follows — every science unit, every history class, every essay rubric, every standardized test — runs on this binary. It is never examined. It is never named as a framework. It is simply how knowledge works, taught so early and so universally that it becomes the air.

    Try something. I'll give you data. You interpret it.

    Since 1970, women in the developed world have gained unprecedented access to education, careers, legal rights, and financial independence. By every measurable metric, women's freedom has expanded dramatically.

    Are women happier?

    You have an expectation. That expectation is not from the data — I haven't given you happiness data yet. It's from a story about what freedom produces.

    Here's the data: women's self-reported happiness has declined since the 1970s, both in absolute terms and relative to men. Documented across multiple countries and decades.

    Now watch your mind. If you lean progressive, you're already reaching for explanations: the revolution is incomplete, structural barriers remain, the wrong things were measured. If you lean conservative, you're reaching for different ones: the costs of disrupting traditional structures are real, nature has its own logic, some things don't trade well for freedom.

    The data is identical. The explanations are opposite. And both feel, to the person generating them, like what the data obviously means.

    Now consider: women in traditional religious communities — dense communal structures, defined roles, high fertility — consistently report higher life satisfaction than the secular average.

    If your instinct is to reach for "false consciousness" or "they don't know any better" — notice: that's not an empirical response. It's a narrative commitment disguised as one. It presupposes that satisfaction under constraint is illegitimate. But that presupposition is a story. Your story. The one you didn't notice selecting.

    You just watched yourself interpret data through a narrative you hold but didn't choose. The interpretation preceded the reasoning. The story preceded the analysis.

    The fact/opinion binary has no name for what just happened. It wasn't a fact — you can't verify a narrative ground by observation. It wasn't an opinion — it's not a personal preference, it's a structural commitment about the nature of reality that determined what the data appeared to show. It was something else. Something the binary cannot see.

    Philosophers have a name for this: the hermeneutic circle. All understanding begins from a prior framework of understanding. You bring questions to the data, and your formation determines which questions you ask, which determines what the data appears to show. There is no unformed knower. There is no view from nowhere. Every act of interpretation stands on ground it did not create.

    This is not a fringe position. It is not controversial among people who study how knowledge works.

    It is completely absent from the education system.

    Not because someone examined it and rejected it. But because the fact/opinion binary has no category for it. Narrative grounding is not a fact. It is not an opinion. It is the precondition for interpreting facts and forming opinions. The binary doesn't argue against it. The binary simply can't see it. Like asking a color-blind person to evaluate a painting's palette. The limitation isn't stubbornness. It's structural.

    Education constrains what you can think about thinking. It doesn't hide a thought. It hides a type of thought — the type that would reveal all the other hiding. It excludes narrative knowing from the inventory of real knowledge, making the tool of recognition permanently unavailable.

    The difference between a locked room and a room where the concept of "door" has been removed from your vocabulary.

    How does a room like that get built? Not through conspiracy. Through a pipeline that has no mechanism for questioning its own foundation.

    The pipeline is self-sealing. Ed schools train teachers within the Enlightenment settlement — science produces knowledge, values are personal. Those teachers teach the binary. The students absorb it before they're old enough to evaluate it. Some of those students become ed school professors. They carry the binary with them, not as a belief but as the structure of belief. They design the next curriculum framework. The framework encodes the binary. The circle closes.

    Nobody conspires. Nobody decides. The binary reproduces itself through omission — through the absence of a category that would make the absence visible. A teacher couldn't teach narrative epistemology if she wanted to. There is no standard it maps to. No assessment. No rubric. It doesn't exist inside the framework, and the framework is all she has.

    This explains why more information never resolves anything.

    Every public debate in the modern West takes place between people who share this binary. Both sides think they're arguing about facts. Neither side can see that they're arguing about which narrative makes the facts legible — because neither side has a category for narrative as a mode of knowing. Both sides present evidence. Neither side moves. They are standing on different ground. But the binary makes it impossible to say this. The closest it can produce is "that's just your opinion" — which is precisely the dismissal that prevents the insight from landing.

    And the postmodern move doesn't help either. "All knowledge is situated, all narratives serve power, no ground is neutral" — this names the circle correctly but draws the wrong conclusion. From "there is no view from nowhere" it concludes "all views are equally groundless." This is the fact/opinion binary in disguise. If narrative grounding isn't a fact, it must be an opinion. And opinions are all equal. So all grounds are equal. So no ground is better than any other.

    But this is false. Narratives can be tested — not against some narrative-free reality, but against the coherence of the accounts they generate and the accuracy of their predictions.

    The economists' narrative held that fertility decline was a resource problem: children are expensive, women lack childcare support, fix the economics and births follow. Scandinavian countries ran the experiment. Generous subsidies, paid leave, universal childcare. Fertility kept falling. The narrative predicted something. The prediction failed. That failure is information — not about a fact, but about the story that was generating the expectations.

    This is what narrative accountability looks like. Not refutation by data alone, but a story making a bet about the shape of the world and losing it. A narrative that survives contact with its own predictions is not the same as one that doesn't. The postmodern refusal to judge between them isn't sophistication. It's the fact/opinion binary in disguise — if narrative grounding isn't a fact, it must be mere opinion, and opinions can't be ranked. But that conclusion doesn't follow. Grounds can be better or worse. Some stories are more honest about what they're predicting, more willing to be tested, more capable of updating.

    A scientist will recognize this process. Form a hypothesis, make predictions, test them, update. Yes — but the scientific method requires you to know you have a hypothesis. You write it down. You declare it before the experiment. Narrative grounding doesn't work that way. It operates below the level of declared hypotheses. The economist who designed the childcare subsidy didn't write at the top of his paper: "I am assuming that human flourishing is primarily a resource allocation problem." That assumption was invisible to him. It was the lens, not the object. The method can't test what it can't see — and it can't see what was never named as a hypothesis in the first place.

    Scientific method is a tool for testing visible hypotheses. Narrative grounding is the layer underneath that determines which hypotheses feel worth forming at all. Same structure, different depth.

    You have a narrative. You've always had one. The education system didn't give you the tools to see it, because seeing it would require a category the system doesn't have.
