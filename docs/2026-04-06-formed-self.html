The Formed Self: Rome, Modernity, and the Grammar of Stagnation

An essay on the soul as output

I. A Deep Grammar

There is a way of understanding the human person that is so old, so pervasive, and so quietly assumed that it rarely announces itself as a position. It simply presents itself as reality. The position is this: the self is downstream of forces it did not choose and cannot fully master. It is shaped by fate, by class, by social structure, by the mimetic currents of desire it absorbs from those around it. It is, in the most precise sense, an output—the residue of causes that preceded it.

This grammar appears in very different vocabularies across the centuries, but its structure remains constant. It appears in Virgil's fatum, in the Stoic logos that runs through all things, in Marx's base and superstructure, in Freud's unconscious determinism, in Bourdieu's habitus, and in Girard's mimetic desire. Each is a distinct theory, often hostile to the others. Yet they share something more fundamental than their surface disagreements: they share a picture of the person as a site where larger forces settle and solidify into what we call a self.

What is philosophically remarkable is that this picture—despite feeling modern in its sociological and psychological inflections—is recognizably ancient. The Roman world thought in this grammar with extraordinary sophistication, and the modern world did not so much invent it as rediscover it, translate it into secular idioms, and present it as science. What Rome called fatum, we call structure. What Rome called pietas, we call socialization. The wheel turns; but it is the same wheel.

Against this grammar, Dante's Commedia stands as a radical counterproposal. Dante's damned are not victims of structure. They are eternally themselves, which is to say: eternally choosing. This essay does not reach Dante except at the end, as a contrast rather than a resolution. Its task is narrower: to trace the deep continuities between the Roman and modern imagination of the formed self, and to ask what it means that we keep returning to this grammar, century after century, as though it were simply the truth about persons.

II. Rome: The Architecture of Fate

The Roman universe is a universe of fatum—of what has been spoken, which is also what will be. The word itself preserves the structure: fatum is the past participle of fari, to speak. Fate is not mere destiny but utterance already accomplished, a word already spoken by the gods before the human actor arrives on the scene. In the Aeneid, Jupiter declares to Venus:

Parce metu, Cytherea, manent immota tuorum / fata tibi.

Fear not, Cytherea; the fates of your people remain unmoved for you. (Aeneid I.257–258)

Unmoved. The word immota is doing everything here. The fates are not responsive to pleading, to virtue, to excellence of any kind. They are structural facts. Aeneas does not achieve the founding of Rome; he fulfills it. The distinction is crucial. To achieve something is to bring into existence what would not otherwise exist. To fulfill a fate is to occupy the position that was always yours to occupy. The self, in this model, is not an agent but a vessel.

This is why the famous opening of the Aeneid—arma virumque cano, I sing of arms and the man—is, on reflection, slightly misleading. We expect the epic of a man. What we get is the epic of a mission. Aeneas himself is notably thin as a character; he weeps constantly, doubts often, and would rather stay with Dido than cross the sea. His famous pietas is not a virtue he has cultivated so much as a role he has been assigned. When he leaves Carthage, he does not do so from moral conviction but from divine compulsion: Mercury appears and repeats the command. The text is frank:

At pius Aeneas, quamquam lenire dolentem / solando cupit et dictis avertere curas, / multa gemens magnoque animum labefactus amore / iussa tamen divum exsequitur.

But pious Aeneas, though he longed to soothe her grief with comfort and to turn away her cares with words, groaning deeply, his heart shaken by his great love, nonetheless carried out the commands of the gods. (Aeneid IV.393–396)

Nonetheless. The word (tamen) confesses what the poem otherwise suppresses: that Aeneas's pietas is not freely chosen but structurally enforced. He is obedient to a role that was assigned before his birth, carried in the phrase dum conderet urbem (until he should found a city) that appears in the poem's third line. The city is, in a sense, the subject of the poem, and Aeneas merely its instrument.

III. The Stoic Self as Cosmic Output

If Virgil embeds the formed self in myth, the Stoics embed it in physics. For Stoicism, the universe is a single rational substance—pneuma, the divine breath—that unfolds according to its own logos. The individual human being is not a free agent standing over against this logos but an expression of it, a temporary local configuration of the universal rational fire.

Marcus Aurelius, in the Meditations, is explicit and almost brutal about this:

Of human life the time is a point, and the substance is in a flux, and the perception dull, and the composition of the whole body subject to putrefaction, and the soul a whirl, and fortune hard to divine, and fame a thing devoid of judgment. (Meditations II.17)

The soul itself is a whirl—στρόβος in the Greek, a spinning thing, a vortex. The self for Marcus is not solid but in flux, not autonomous but subject to composition and decomposition by forces beyond it. What remains, then? Only the rational hegemonikon, the governing faculty, which has the limited freedom of assent or refusal. But even here, the Stoic position is carefully constrained. Epictetus draws the famous line:

Some things are in our control and others not. Things in our control are opinion, pursuit, desire, aversion, and, in a word, whatever are our own actions. Things not in our control are body, reputation, command, and, in one word, whatever are not our own actions. (Discourses I.1)

The freedom Epictetus carves out is real but radically reduced. It is the freedom of response, not of initiation. You cannot choose what happens to you; you can only choose how to interpret what happens. The external world—including your body, your social position, your reputation, the entire theater of historical life—is simply given. It is the domain of indifferents (adiaphora). The sage withdraws from all of this into the inner citadel, which is the only space the self actually owns.

This is a sophisticated position, but notice its shape: the self as refuge from the world, not as actor in it. The Stoic subject survives by contraction. Seneca, writing to Lucilius, puts it memorably:

Recede in te ipse quantum potes.

Withdraw into yourself as much as you can. (Epistulae Morales VII.8)

The motion is always inward and backward. The world is a torrent of fortune, mimetic opinion, and social noise. The wise man does not reform it—he insulates himself from it. What looks like freedom is actually a sophisticated form of passivity: the acceptance of the structure of things, combined with the refusal to let it touch the inner self. The outer self remains entirely formed by forces beyond its control.

IV. The Wheel Turns: Modern Structure

When Marx writes, in the 1859 Preface, that "it is not the consciousness of men that determines their existence, but their social existence that determines their consciousness," he is not inventing a new grammar. He is translating the old one. Fatum has become the mode of production. Pietas—fidelity to one's assigned role—has become false consciousness. The gods have been replaced by historical forces, but the logic of the formed self is identical.

The 18th Brumaire contains the most famous articulation:

Men make their own history, but they do not make it as they please; they do not make it under self-selected circumstances, but under circumstances existing already, given and transmitted from the past.

The grudging concession—men make their own history—is swallowed immediately by the structural qualification. History is made, but not by the persons who nominally act within it. It is made by the weight of everything that preceded those persons: economic relations, class positions, the ghosts of dead generations who come back wearing borrowed costumes to haunt the present. The human actor is a kind of relay station for forces that use him and discard him.

Freud's contribution to this grammar arrives from a different direction—inward rather than outward—but the structure is the same. The unconscious is a formation, a sediment of experiences, traumas, and drives that the conscious self did not choose and cannot fully access. In the Introductory Lectures, Freud tells his audience with studied calm:

The ego is not master in its own house.

This sentence has the force of a theorem. The self you experience as yourself—the deliberate, choosing, narrating self—is not the primary phenomenon. It is a secondary formation, a surface effect produced by deeper processes. Your desires, your fears, your loves, your repetitions: these were formed by forces that preceded your awareness of them. Therapy does not liberate you from this; it simply makes the formation legible so you can, at best, make a slightly more conscious peace with it.

Bourdieu's habitus closes the circle. In The Logic of Practice, he describes the habitus as

systems of durable, transposable dispositions, structured structures predisposed to function as structuring structures, that is, as principles which generate and organize practices and representations that can be objectively adapted to their outcomes without presupposing a conscious aiming at ends.

The habitus is what you are before you decide anything—the posture, the taste, the intuitive sense of what is possible and impossible that your class position and upbringing have deposited in your body. It operates beneath conscious deliberation. It is, in Bourdieu's phrase, history turned into nature: the accumulated forces of social formation so deeply internalized that they feel like personality, like selfhood, like simply what you are.

V. Mimesis and the Caught Self

René Girard's mimetic theory arrives last in this genealogy, and in some ways most acutely, because it describes not the social formation of the self but the interpersonal one: the way desire itself is borrowed, caught, transmitted like a contagion before the self has any chance to deliberate.

In Deceit, Desire, and the Novel, Girard traces what he calls triangular desire: we do not desire objects directly but through the mediation of a model, a rival, a third party whose desire discloses the object's value to us. The subject of mimetic desire is always already secondhand:

Romantic literature, as well as all literature derived from it, attributes to the individual an absolute originality which it contrasts with the servility of others. But this is precisely the illusion that the great novelists—Cervantes, Stendhal, Flaubert, Dostoevsky, Proust—systematically expose.

Don Quixote does not choose chivalric adventure from some original well of personality; he catches it from Amadis of Gaul. Emma Bovary does not desire romance; she absorbs the template of desire from cheap novels. The human subject, on this account, is formed by imitation all the way down, and what we experience as our most intimate and personal desires are in fact the most socially mediated.

In Things Hidden Since the Foundation of the World, Girard connects this to the deeper logic of the scapegoat mechanism—the way communities discharge their mimetic crises onto victims—but the anthropological point follows from the same premise: the self is not an origin but a relay. Desire travels through persons, not from them.

What is remarkable is how precisely this maps onto the Roman structures we have already traced. Fatum is the cosmic version of what habitus is in the social domain and what mimetic contagion is in the interpersonal domain. They are the same grammar at different scales: the self as product of a force it did not originate.

VI. The Therapeutic Residue

The modern era has produced one native response to this grammar: the therapeutic self. If the self is formed by forces—economic, psychological, mimetic—then the task of the self is to process those forces, to become aware of them, to achieve what Philip Rieff called the "analytic attitude." This is the dominant mode of selfhood in the contemporary West: the self that has been in therapy, that has done the work, that has unpacked its childhood, deconstructed its class assumptions, become aware of its mimetic models.

But notice what the therapeutic self does not claim to do. It does not claim to escape the formation. It does not assert that the self can stand free of fate, structure, or mimesis and choose from some unconditioned ground. It asserts only that consciousness of the formation produces a slightly more livable relationship to it. The formation remains; the self simply learns to narrate it differently.

This is, in structure, identical to Epictetan Stoicism. What the Stoic called prosoche—watchful attention to one's inner states—the therapist calls mindfulness or self-awareness. What the Stoic called the inner citadel, the therapist calls the regulated self. What the Stoic called adiaphora—the indifferents beyond one's control—the therapist calls "what happened to you." The motion is the same: inward withdrawal, narrative management of a self formed by external forces, and the modest claim that consciousness of one's condition is as close to freedom as one can reasonably get.

Seneca, again, could be a therapist writing to a patient:

Dum differtur vita transcurrit.

While we are postponing, life speeds by. (Epistulae Morales I.1)

The urgency is the same. The horizon is the same. The self is in the grip of something—time, fortune, the accumulated weight of what has been done to it—and the best it can do is become aware of this grip and refuse to waste the remaining freedom of attention.

What neither Rome nor modernity achieves, within this grammar, is a self that genuinely acts—that introduces something new into the world rather than processing and relaying what was already there. The formed self can be refined, can become more conscious, can achieve a certain dignity of resignation. But it cannot, from within its own logic, become a different kind of thing. It cannot choose its fate; it can only come to terms with it. And coming to terms with one's fate is, of course, the Roman virtue par excellence.

VII. Francesca, or The Damned Are Eternally Themselves

Against all of this, Dante places a counterimage so startling that it has troubled readers since the 14th century. In Inferno V, Dante encounters Francesca da Rimini in the circle of the lustful—those blown about forever by an infernal wind that mirrors their earthly surrender to passion. The souls in Hell, in the standard reading, are victims of love, of fate, of forces that overwhelmed them.

But Dante is more severe than his commentators. Francesca speaks beautifully, eloquently, with the same self-deceiving rhetoric she used in life:

Amor, ch'al cor gentil ratto s'apprende, / prese costui de la bella persona / che mi fu tolta; e 'l modo ancor m'offende.

Love, which is quickly kindled in the noble heart, seized this one for the fair body that was taken from me; and the manner still offends me. (Inferno V.100–102)

She is still offended. She is still telling the story in the same way—love seized us, love compelled us, we were carried away. She speaks of herself and Paolo in the passive voice of the formed self: they were acted upon, not acting. And Dante the character—not Dante the poet—swoons. He faints from sympathy, from the beauty of the rhetoric, from the seductiveness of the grammar she is deploying.

Dante the poet, however, has placed her in Hell. The damnation is not the punishment of a self formed by forces beyond its control. It is the eternal self-revelation of a self that chose, and kept choosing, and in Hell is still choosing: still telling the story that excuses, still narrating herself as victim of Love rather than as the woman who betrayed her husband, who seduced her husband's brother, who mistook narrative for permission and rhetoric for innocence.

The damned in Dante are not victims of structure. They are eternally themselves—which is to say, eternally their choices. This is the theological precision that modernity finds most difficult to absorb: not that the formed self is impossible, but that appealing to formation as exculpation is itself a moral act, and a culpable one. Francesca is not in Hell because she loved; she is in Hell because she narrated her love in a way that foreclosed repentance. The narrative of the formed self—love seized me, fate decreed, structure determined—is, in the Commedia, the narrative of damnation.

VIII. A Grammar and Its Ghosts

The grammar of the formed self is not wrong. We are shaped by forces we did not choose: by birth, by language, by class, by the mimetic currents of our particular historical moment. To deny this is a different kind of naivety—the naivety of the Romantic subject who imagines himself self-made, self-originating, beholden to nothing prior to his own will.

But there is a question that neither Rome nor modernity presses with sufficient urgency: what is the formed self for? Formation is not destiny; it is equipment. The Stoic sage is formed by his training in order to achieve apatheia—not passivity, but the freedom from reactive passion that enables genuine action. Aeneas is formed by fatum in order to found something: a city, a civilization, a new order that was not there before. Even Marx's proletariat, formed by its class position, is formed in order to act—to make the revolution that will end the rule of structure.

But in each case, the modern versions of this grammar tend to foreclose the second act. The therapeutic self processes its formation without any clear telos beyond processing. The socialized subject becomes aware of its habitus without any obvious account of what genuine social action looks like from within a habitus. The mimetic subject becomes aware of its models, perhaps, but awareness of mimesis does not itself liberate from it—it may only produce a more refined and self-conscious form of secondhand desire.

What the grammar of the formed self most conspicuously lacks is an account of ground—of the unconditioned from which something genuinely new might emerge. Dante finds that ground in a specific theological claim: the soul is not exhausted by its formation. It has a depth that antecedes and survives all historical shaping. The Commedia is not, ultimately, an argument about free will in the philosophical sense; it is a phenomenology of that depth, traced through Hell, Purgatory, and Paradise.

Whether one accepts the theological claim or not, the phenomenological observation deserves its own attention: there is something in human experience that the grammar of the formed self does not capture—something in the encounter with genuine beauty, genuine sacrifice, genuine love, genuine understanding, that interrupts the wheel of formation and speaks from elsewhere. The Romans felt it; they called it numen. The mystics cultivated it; they called it Grunt, the Ground. The modern world suppresses the question, calling it mystification.

But the question keeps returning. The formed self keeps finding, in its most extreme moments, that it was not quite what the grammar said it was—that formation goes all the way down only until, suddenly, it doesn't.
