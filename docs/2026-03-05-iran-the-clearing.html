Iran: The Board Was Already Clear

The war began on February 28, 2026. The preparation began years earlier.

Not as conspiracy. Not as a master plan coordinated across capitals and briefing rooms. Something more interesting than that. A convergence of self-interested moves, each rational on its own terms, each removing one more obstacle between the present order and the sacrifice it was building toward. Nobody planned the scapegoat. The board cleared itself.

The Proxies

Iran's regional power was never its own military. It was the network — Hamas, Hezbollah, the Houthis, the Iraqi Shia militias — that allowed Tehran to project force without deploying it. Every proxy that fell was a wall removed from around the target.

Hamas was gutted in Gaza beginning in late 2023. Eighteen months of Israeli operations did not just degrade its military capacity. They made the political cost of the next war cheaper by establishing a precedent: sustained urban warfare against an Iranian proxy, with American diplomatic cover, and no red line crossed that triggered wider escalation.

Hezbollah was decapitated through 2024 and 2025. The pager attacks, the targeted assassinations of senior commanders, the Lebanon operations — by the time the Iran strikes began, Iran's most capable proxy had already lost its leadership cadre. But the deeper blow was the fall of Assad in late 2024. Syria was the land bridge. Weapons, money, and fighters flowed from Tehran through Iraq through Syria to southern Lebanon. When Assad fell, the corridor died. Hezbollah was not just weakened. It was severed from its supply.

The Houthis served a different function. The Red Sea campaign from late 2023 through 2025 was not primarily about shipping lanes. It was a laboratory. Two years of Houthi drone and missile attacks gave the US Navy a live-fire testing ground for layered air defense — sensor fusion, radar tuning, cost-effective intercept solutions. The Navy learned that $35,000 laser-guided rockets could kill drones that $4 million missiles had been wasted on. It learned reaction times against anti-ship ballistic missiles. It learned how swarm tactics work and how they fail. Every engagement in the Red Sea was a rehearsal for the Strait of Hormuz.

The Iraqi Shia militias were quietly degraded through targeted strikes across 2024 and 2025, each one framed as a response to a specific provocation, none of them large enough to trigger a wider conflict. By the time Iran needed its second line of regional retaliation, it was already threadbare.

Strip the proxies. Then strike the principal.

The Economy

The sanctions architecture did something more precise than weakening Iran. It produced the conditions for the moral justification the war required.

US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent admitted it openly: the United States had engineered a dollar shortage inside Iran. The country's largest bank collapsed. The rial entered free fall. Inflation passed 50 percent. Cooking oil doubled in price. The economic crisis was not an accident and it was not merely punitive. It was designed to produce internal revolt.

It worked. The protests that erupted in late December 2025 were the largest since 1979. Millions in the streets across every province. And when the regime massacred its own people — 7,000 confirmed dead, possibly 30,000 — the moral case for intervention assembled itself. Trump's "help is on the way" was not an improvisation. It was the rhetorical capture of a crisis the sanctions had been engineering for years.

The sequence matters. Sanctions collapse the economy. The economy produces protests. The protests produce massacres. The massacres produce the justification. Each step generates the next. No single actor needs to have planned the full chain. The logic is self-reinforcing.

The Reconnaissance

The June 2025 Twelve-Day War looked, at the time, like a contained exchange. Israel and the United States struck Iranian nuclear facilities. Iran retaliated. A ceasefire held. The conventional reading was that both sides tested limits and pulled back.

The retrospective reading is different. June 2025 was a reconnaissance in force. It mapped Iranian air defenses. It tested command-and-control response times. It gauged the quality of Iranian retaliation — which targets they chose, which systems they used, how quickly they adapted. It revealed Russia's response (nothing) and China's response (rhetoric). It tested whether the Gulf states would absorb retaliatory strikes without breaking from the coalition. They did.

Every lesson from June was applied in February. The eight months between the two operations were not a pause. They were preparation.

The Restrainer

Russia was the one power that might have altered the calculus. It had the UN Security Council veto, the diplomatic relationship with Tehran, the arms pipeline, and the rhetorical framework — the Katechon, the civilizational restrainer — that positioned it as the counterweight to American eschatological momentum.

Ukraine consumed all of it. Three years of grinding attrition hollowed out Russia's capacity to project power beyond its borders. Moscow had already internalized the production of Iranian Shahed drones — it no longer needed Tehran even for its own war. When the strikes came, Putin did not speak publicly. The Foreign Ministry issued a statement. On the same day, Russia launched 106 missiles at Ukrainian cities, because the war it chose to fight had consumed the capacity it would have needed to restrain the war it claimed to oppose.

The Katechon did not fail. It was removed — not by frontal assault but by attrition in a different theater at exactly the moment its restraining function was needed.

The Silent Partner

Then there is China. And China is where the pattern shifts from preparation against obstacles to preparation for the aftermath.

The conventional framing treats China as a passive bystander — an ally that failed to help Iran. This misreads the situation almost completely.

China buys 80 percent of Iran's oil exports. It signed a $400 billion, 25-year investment deal with Tehran. It brokered the Saudi-Iran detente in 2023. On paper, it is Iran's most important external partner. In practice, it positioned itself over the past several years to survive — and profit from — Iran's destruction.

The reserves tell the story. China accumulated 1.2 billion barrels of strategic petroleum reserves — enough to cover roughly eight months of a full Strait of Hormuz closure, far longer than any plausible disruption from a four-to-five-week air campaign. This was not improvisation. It was years of deliberate stockpiling by a country that had done the math on what was coming.

The diversification reinforces the point. Chinese renewable energy capacity surpassed oil as its second-largest energy source in 2025. Iranian crude, for all the attention it receives, accounts for only 13 percent of China's seaborne imports. China does not depend on Iran. It benefits from Iranian oil. There is a difference, and the difference is what makes the loss survivable.

The diplomatic positioning is the most revealing move. China brokered the Saudi-Iran detente — not to save Iran, but to establish itself as the indispensable mediator between the two sides. When the detente collapses because one side is bombed, who has relationships with both? Who can talk to Riyadh and to whatever comes after Tehran? Who is positioned to fund reconstruction, build infrastructure, and integrate the new Persia into Belt and Road without the sanctions complications that made every barrel of Iranian oil a covert operation?

China did not plan the sacrifice. But it positioned itself to inherit the material aftermath while the West inherits the symbolic victory. The relationship with Washington reinforces this. A Trump-Xi summit was scheduled for March 31, 2026 — one month after the strikes began. The trade truce that makes confrontation over Iran irrational was already in place. Beijing calculated that its relationship with Washington was worth more than its relationship with a theocratic regime whose proxies had been dismantled, whose military had been mapped, and whose population had already tried to overthrow it.

The essay calls this role "the silent partner." The West owns the myth. China owns the ground. But there is a deeper reading available.

China is not merely positioning for reconstruction contracts. It is positioning itself to become the next node in the Abrahamic sacrificial cycle — pulled in through infrastructure, entangled through commerce, synchronized through shared economic crises with its trading partners. The very integration that makes it the primary beneficiary of the post-Iranian order is the mechanism by which it enters the cycle it thinks it is merely observing.

The board was cleared by many hands, each pursuing its own interest, none of them coordinating with all the others, and yet the result is a convergence so complete it looks, in retrospect, like choreography. That appearance is not evidence of conspiracy. It is evidence of the mechanism — the scapegoat logic producing its own preconditions, assembling unanimity through the accumulation of self-interested moves that happen to point in the same direction.

The sacrifice requires that every obstacle be removed. The obstacles were removed. The sacrifice proceeded.

Whether anyone intended this or whether the mechanism intended itself is a question that cannot be answered from inside the convergence. You can only see the pattern after the victim has been chosen and the peace has begun. By then, the answer no longer matters. The peace is real. The preparation is renarrated as providence. And the silent partner begins building roads across the ruins, unaware that the roads lead somewhere it did not choose.
