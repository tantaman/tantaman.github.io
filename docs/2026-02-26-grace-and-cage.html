Grace and the Cage

Is religion just a way of having a good subjective experience while in the cage? Or truly oriented towards something real?

If the ratchet described in the preceding essay is real — if life is a value-capture engine, technology its externalized form, capital its stored optionality, and power the name for all of it compounding — then the strongest materialist case writes itself. Any experience of transcendence arising within this system is just another product of the system. The monk's bliss is a neurochemical state. Gelassenheit is a coping strategy for powerlessness. The apophatic "not this, not this" is a sophisticated way of making peace with a game you cannot win. Religion, on this account, is the system's most elegant pressure-release valve.

There is a version of this darker than Marx. At least Marx thought you could break the cage. If the ratchet is truly cosmic — operative from thermodynamics to politics — then religion isn't even false consciousness, because there's no true consciousness to recover. There's just gravity, and various ways of narrating your fall.

That case is not easily dismissed. But there are structural problems with it that emerge from the argument itself, not from sentiment.

The Problem of Self-Reference

If everything is gravity — if every movement of the soul follows the same accumulative, power-seeking logic — then the recognition of gravity is inexplicable. The ratchet doesn't produce self-awareness of the ratchet. A system that is nothing but value-capture has no reason to generate an agent that says "I see that I am nothing but value-capture, and something in me refuses it."

The demand sharer who exploits the system for social capital is doing what gravity predicts. The person who sees the demand sharer doing this, and sees themselves doing it too, and is troubled by it — that's a different kind of event.

This is essentially the argument Augustine makes against the skeptics, and that Gödel makes in a formal register: a system cannot fully account for itself from within. If the ratchet is the total system, it cannot generate the standpoint from which the ratchet is seen as the ratchet. The fact that you can ask "is this all just the cage?" is itself evidence of something not fully of the cage.

The Phenomenological Problem

The mystical traditions don't describe transcendence as a better subjective experience. That's the popular version — meditation makes you calm, prayer gives you peace. But the actual reports describe something much stranger. Eckhart describes the annihilation of the experiencing self. The Cloud of Unknowing describes a darkness in which all knowledge and consolation are withdrawn. John of the Cross calls it the dark night of the soul — the systematic stripping away of every pleasant experience, every comforting image, every felt sense of God's presence.

If religion were a coping mechanism, it would produce comfort. The serious mystical traditions produce the opposite. They demolish every consolation, every psychological support, every sense of meaning. Weil: "We must leave on one side the beliefs which fill up voids and sweeten what is bitter." If this is a cage, it's one that demands you give up every comfort of being caged.

The Behavioral Problem

The cage theory predicts that religious experience should be functionally inert — it should help you cope but change nothing about the system. And for much of institutional religion, this is exactly true. But at the margins, in the people who seem to have encountered something real, the behavior doesn't fit. Francis of Assisi's renunciation of wealth is not an optimizing strategy in any framework. The Desert Fathers walking into the wilderness aren't finding a more pleasant experience. They are doing something the ratchet cannot account for — voluntarily relinquishing optionality. This is thermodynamically absurd. The squirrel does not give away its acorns.

You can always explain this as pathology, or as a different kind of status-seeking — spiritual prestige — and in many cases that's exactly what it is. The monk who is proud of his humility is back inside the ratchet. But the traditions themselves are ruthless about this. They identify spiritual pride as the most dangerous form of gravity precisely because it disguises itself as its opposite.

The Asymmetry of the Argument

The materialist position — that everything is gravity and transcendence is a neural event — is unfalsifiable from within. If every piece of evidence for transcendence can be explained as "just what the cage produces," the hypothesis is immune to counter-evidence, which means it's not an empirical claim. It's a metaphysical commitment wearing the costume of clear-eyed realism.

The reverse is also true: the believer can always say "grace is real" without satisfying the materialist. Both positions are interpretive frameworks applied to the same data. But one of them can account for the other's existence and the other cannot. The framework that includes both gravity and grace can explain why the pure-gravity framework is attractive and partially true — it describes the mechanical operation of the world accurately. The pure-gravity framework cannot explain why the perception of something beyond gravity arises, why it persists across every culture and era, and why its most serious practitioners behave in ways the framework predicts should not occur.

Consciousness Is Not Automatic

Here the argument takes a turn that matters. The seeing of the system — the Gödelian recognition of the cage as cage — has a specific structural property: it cannot be habituated. The moment it becomes automatic, it ceases to be the recognition. It has become reflex, pattern-matching, technique. And technique is gravity.

This maps onto a distinction that gets collapsed in modern discourse: thinking versus consciousness. Thinking — problem-solving, inference, pattern recognition — is mechanical. It operates by gravity. It follows algorithms, even complex and adaptive ones. Thinking can and should become habitual; the expert doesn't consciously reason about fundamentals. That sinking into automaticity is a feature.

But the seeing — the recognition of the system as a system — is categorically different. It can't be routinized because routinization is what it sees through. The moment you develop a technique for seeing through technique, you've built a new cage. The moment your spiritual practice becomes something you do on autopilot, it has become mechanical. It's still operating, but it's no longer that.

This is why every serious contemplative tradition includes mechanisms for disrupting its own practice. Zen koans aren't puzzles to be solved. They're designed to break the pattern-matching mind so something else has to show up. The Desert Fathers deliberately disrupted their routines. The Cloud of Unknowing instructs you to forget everything you've learned about prayer. These aren't pedagogical tricks. They're acknowledgments that practice itself is subject to gravity and must be constantly de-automated.

The implication is startling: if consciousness in the strong sense is the non-automatic, then most of what we do, most of the time, is not conscious. We're thinking, processing, reacting, optimizing — and all of that is the machine. Genuine awareness — the seeing of the system from a standpoint the system does not generate — might be rare. Not something we possess continuously but something that happens to us, or that we momentarily participate in, and that we spend most of our lives falling away from.

The traditions report exactly this. Zen talks about satori as a flash, not a state. Eckhart describes the birth of the Word in the soul as occurring in the Nunc stans — the eternal now — which by definition cannot be sustained as a temporal sequence. Even Paul: "I knew a man in Christ who fourteen years ago was caught up to the third heaven." Fourteen years ago. Once.

What Can Be Said

The spiritual life, then, is not about achieving a permanent state of awareness. That would be making consciousness habitual, which is a contradiction. It is about increasing the frequency of the interruption — creating conditions where the seeing is more likely to break through the automaticity. The practices — prayer, meditation, asceticism, study — aren't producing consciousness. They're thinning the walls. They're reducing noise so the signal has a chance. But the signal itself comes from outside the system. You can't make it come. You can only stop preventing it.

This is Weil's formulation: grace fills empty spaces, but it can only enter where there's a void. The practice doesn't create grace. The practice creates the void. And creating a void is specifically a matter of not doing — of interrupting the automatic, the habitual, the mechanical. The effort is the effort of cessation, which is a very strange kind of effort.

Voegelin's answer is probably the most careful: we exist in the metaxy, the in-between, and the experience of transcendence is constitutive of human consciousness rather than an addition to it. Consciousness isn't a thing that exists and then optionally has religious experiences. Consciousness is the tension between immanence and transcendence. The question "is it real?" presupposes that awareness could exist without the tension — that you could have a fully gravitational mind that then asks whether there's anything more. Voegelin says no. The asking is the evidence.

This doesn't prove that God exists in the way a theorem is proved. It says something more modest and more radical: the structure of human awareness already contains both gravity and the perception of something that is not gravity. Any account of reality that eliminates either is less complete than the experience it's trying to explain.

And here it loops back to the ratchet. If consciousness — real consciousness, the non-automatic kind — is what allows you to see the cage, and if consciousness is inherently non-mechanical, then consciousness is genuinely outside the gravitational field in a way nothing else is. Not because it's supernatural in some gaudy sense, but because it's structurally the one thing that can't be reduced to the system it observes.

And it has a property that makes it immune to the ratchet: it can't be stored. You can't accumulate it. You can't build capital from it. The moment you try, you've converted it into something mechanical and it's gone. It is the one form of value that resists the ratchet — because the ratchet depends on storability, and this can't be stored. Only re-encountered. Every single time.

The cage is real. The gravity is real. But the thing that looks at the cage and says "this is a cage" is not entirely of the cage. And that may be enough.
