The Universe Is Under No Obligation

Published 2026-02-27 · T
philosophyreligionculturegroundknowledgeessayoriginal

    A bit, in the style of Stewart Lee

    So Neil deGrasse Tyson — and you already know where you are with this, don't you, because I said the name and some of you did a little internal nod, a little "ah yes, I like him, he's the science one," and that nod, that little nod, is going to be a problem for you later, but we'll get to that.

    Neil deGrasse Tyson has this line. He says, "The universe is under no obligation to make sense to you." And the audience — it's always an audience, isn't it, it's never just one person in a room thinking, it's always a theatre full of people who've paid forty dollars to be told what they already believe — the audience applauds. They applaud this.

    And I want to take that seriously. I want to take the applause seriously. Because what are they applauding? They're applauding the idea that reality doesn't owe you an explanation. Which — yes. Fine. Correct. But the applause isn't for the idea. The applause is because they feel, in that moment, that they are on the right team. The universe doesn't make sense — and they're okay with that. They have made their peace with the vast indifference of the cosmos, in a conference centre, on a Saturday, for forty dollars. Plus booking fee.

    And Tyson stands there, and he does the thing with his hands, the thing where he sort of — he opens his hands like he's releasing a bird. The bird of knowledge. The knowledge bird. He releases it into the auditorium and everyone watches it go and thinks, "I am the sort of person who is comfortable with not knowing." And that feeling — that warm, specific feeling — is the exact same feeling that a megachurch pastor produces when he says "God has a plan." It is structurally identical. One group is being told "it's okay, the universe is vast and beautiful and meaningless," and the other is being told "it's okay, the universe is vast and beautiful and meaningful," and both groups leave feeling settled. Both groups have been settled. And settling people is not — it's not what the serious version of either project is supposed to do.

    But fine. Fine. Tyson's a populariser, he's doing a job, the job is to make science accessible, and he does it well. I'm not — I'm not here to say he's stupid. He's not stupid. He's a very intelligent man who has made a career out of performing a specific affect of intelligence, which is different from intelligence, but which, in a visual medium, is more useful than intelligence, because intelligence is slow and boring and frequently wrong and says "I don't know" a lot, and the affect of intelligence is fast and exciting and says things like "the universe is under no obligation to make sense to you" and then does the bird hands.

    Now. Bertrand Russell. Different problem.

    Russell was actually rigorous. Russell sat down and said, "Right, the First Cause argument, let's have a look at it," and he took it apart, and he was right. He was correct. The First Cause argument doesn't work. The argument from design doesn't work. The moral argument, as typically formulated, doesn't work. Russell demonstrated this clearly, and he was right, and it does not matter at all, because the people he was arguing against were not the people who had the serious position.

    It's as if — imagine someone writes a devastating, analytically flawless, peer-reviewed refutation of the claim that the moon is made of cheese. Unassailable paper. Published in Nature. Destroys the cheese thesis from every angle. And you read it and you think, "Yes, but who exactly were you arguing with?" And the answer is: mostly people he met at Cambridge dinner parties who said grace before meals and believed God had opinions about the age of rocks, and who were, from the perspective of, say, Meister Eckhart, already so far from the serious version of the thing that refuting them is like — it's like going to a fancy dress party, meeting a man in a Batman costume, punching him, and claiming you've defeated Batman.

    Russell punched a man in a Batman costume. And he punched him very well. The technique was impeccable. Devastating left hook. The man went down. But Batman, the real Batman — if there is a real Batman, which is precisely what we can't determine — Batman was never in the building.

    And here's what's strange. Here's what none of them seem to notice.

    The actually serious religious thinkers — and I know, I know, "actually serious religious thinkers" sounds like something you say at a dinner party to make everyone wish they'd sat somewhere else — but the serious ones, the Eckharts, the Weils, the anonymous lunatic who wrote the Cloud of Unknowing in the fourteenth century in — in what, Middle English, in a room somewhere, by candlelight, writing "forget everything you know about God, forget prayer itself, enter the darkness and want nothing" — these people agree with Russell. That's the thing. They got there first. They got there centuries first.

    Meister Eckhart, thirteenth century, says — and I'm paraphrasing, obviously, but the gist is — "if you think you understand God, whatever you've understood, that isn't God." That's — Russell hasn't improved on that! Russell has added footnotes to a position the mystics staked out in the thirteenth century and then went beyond, into territory Russell apparently didn't know existed, which is — I mean, it's like turning up to a mountain and refuting the base camp and not realising there's a mountain.

    Carl Sagan is the one that actually makes me sad, because Sagan had the thing. He had the thing. The pale blue dot passage — "a mote of dust suspended in a sunbeam" — that is not science communication. That is a man standing at the edge of something that has no name and trying to say what he sees. That's — in the phenomenological sense, that's much closer to what the contemplatives are describing than it is to a physics lecture. Sagan is having the experience, he's just narrating it in a vocabulary that doesn't include the word "God," which, fine, the word "God" has been so badly abused that avoiding it might be a kindness.

    But then Sagan turns around and dismisses the entire tradition of people who've had the same experience he's having and developed a vocabulary for it over three thousand years. Because their vocabulary includes metaphysical commitments he doesn't share. He's — it's like a man who is clearly, obviously, undeniably in love, writing a book about how love doesn't exist because he's checked the periodic table and there's no element for it.

    Tyson has the wonder without the awe. That's the distinction. Wonder is "wow, look at that, isn't that amazing, let me tell you about it." Awe is "I am not sure I survive this." Sagan had awe. You can hear it in his voice. Tyson has wonder, which is — wonder is the gift shop version of awe. Wonder is awe with a lanyard. Wonder is what you experience when you feel briefly small and then immediately recover because there's a meet-and-greet afterwards.

    And now here's where it gets uncomfortable, and I did warn you, I did say the nod would be a problem.

    The reason Tyson works — the reason he fills theatres and sells books and gets the applause — is not because he's saying something true, although some of what he says is true. It's because he's performing a resolution. He's taking the genuinely terrifying question — "is this all there is, is the cage the whole story, does the weight I feel when I make a moral choice correspond to anything real or am I a sophisticated animal performing significance in an empty room" — he's taking that question, which should keep you up at night, which kept Pascal up at night, which is the question on which your entire life either does or does not mean anything — and he's resolving it. He's going, "Nah, it's fine. Stars. You're made of stars. Isn't that enough?"

    And the audience goes: yes. Yes, that's enough. That is enough.

    And the audience is you. That's the — some of you, right now, are thinking, "Well, I mean, it is enough, isn't it, the stars thing, the stardust thing, that does seem like enough," and what I'm telling you is that that feeling, that satisfaction, that sense of "ah, I have arrived at a sensible and grounded and adult position" — that is the exact feeling that the serious contemplative traditions identify as the most dangerous obstacle to genuine seeking. It's not doubt that stops you from looking further. It's premature comfort. It's the moment you go, "Right, I've got a satisfying answer, I can stop now." And the answer doesn't even need to be wrong. It just needs to be satisfying. Satisfaction is the cage. Satisfaction is always the cage.

    Tyson is doing for scientific materialism what the prosperity gospel does for Christianity. He's taking something with a genuinely difficult, genuinely demanding, genuinely uncomfortable core — in this case, the position that the universe is mechanistic and indifferent and you must find a way to live inside that — and he's making it pleasant. He's making it fun. He's making it a thing you can enjoy on a Saturday afternoon and then go to brunch.

    The prosperity gospel pastor says, "God wants you to be rich." Tyson says, "The universe is awesome and you're made of stars." Both of these are — as theology and as philosophy respectively — they are exactly as shallow as each other. They are the same depth. You could swap the audiences and neither would notice for twenty minutes.

    And now — now — here is the worst part, and I'm sorry, I am genuinely sorry, but here is the worst part.

    I have just done the same thing.

    I have stood here and given you a critique of premature comfort, and you have found the critique comfortable. Some of you are feeling quite good right now. Quite pleased. "Yes," you're thinking, "I always suspected Tyson was a bit shallow, and now a comedian has confirmed it in an entertaining way, and I am the sort of person who sees through things." And that — that feeling — the feeling of seeing through things —

    That's the cage too.

    I'm sorry. That's the cage too. Seeing through things is the cage. Feeling clever about seeing through things is the cage. The cage goes all the way up. And a comedian who tells you the cage goes all the way up and makes you feel something about hearing it — he is also the cage. I am the cage. This — all of this — is the cage.

    The mystics would say: the only thing that isn't the cage is the moment you stop finding any of this satisfying. The moment every framework, including this one, collapses, and you're left with nothing — no team to be on, no nod to do, no position to feel good about — that's where the interesting thing starts. And it's not fun. It's not a Saturday. There is no brunch.

    Anyway. That's — I think that's probably — yeah. That's the end. Thank you.

    [pause]

    The universe is under no obligation to make sense to you. But you — you — are under some obligation to at least not pretend that it does.

    [Walks off. No bird hands.]
