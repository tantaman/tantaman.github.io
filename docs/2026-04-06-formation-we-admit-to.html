How corporations proved that texts shape souls — and what we did with the proof

We have, in the modern era, conducted an accidental experiment on one of the oldest questions in education: does what you read change who you are? The experiment has been running for roughly seventy years, its results are unambiguous, and we have used those results to sell streaming subscriptions and two-day shipping while treating the original question as a quaint philosophical speculation.

The irony is almost too neat to be believed.

The Experiment

In August 2009, Reed Hastings and Patty McCord released a 125-slide PowerPoint deck describing Netflix's culture. It was not a policy document. It was not a handbook. It was, in their own framing, an attempt to describe "the actual company we want to be" — the values, the behaviors, the kind of person who would thrive, the kind who would not. Sheryl Sandberg called it "the most important document ever to come out of Silicon Valley." It was downloaded more than 20 million times.

The deck opens with a claim: "The actual company values, as opposed to the nice-sounding values, are shown by who gets rewarded, promoted, or let go." This is a canonical argument. It is saying: ignore the stated text; the operative text is what happens to people. Judge the canon by its fruit.

What followed at Netflix was precisely what a careful student of canonical formation would predict. The document circulated. It was internalized. It was argued about at the dinner tables of a generation of tech founders who built their own companies around its logic. The concepts it introduced — "talent density," "adequate performance gets a generous severance package," "the keeper test" — became the vocabulary of an industry. People who had never worked at Netflix were shaped by the document. It propagated through a class of workers the way the Nicene Creed propagated through a class of Christians: not always believed completely, not always understood fully, but structuring the imagination of everyone who encountered it.

This is canonical transmission. It happened in a decade. We watched it happen. We have the download statistics.

What the Data Says

The evidence that organizational texts form people is not anecdotal. It is among the most replicated findings in organizational psychology.

James Baron and Michael Hannan's Stanford Project on Emerging Companies tracked 200 Silicon Valley startups from the late 1990s through their development, specifically examining what they called "blueprints" — the founding assumptions about how to select, coordinate, and attach employees. Their findings, published across multiple papers and summarized in Business Week in 2002, were stark: the founding blueprint persisted. Companies that started with a "commitment model" — hiring for values fit, treating employment as a long-term mutual obligation — remained commitment-model companies even after IPOs, leadership changes, and market upheavals. The founding text, encoded in early hires and early decisions, produced a durable culture that resisted later attempts to change it. Baron and Hannan found that commitment-model startups had significantly lower failure rates and higher IPO valuations than engineering-model or star-model startups — a result so counterintuitive to the market that it was largely ignored by the investors who funded those studies.

James Heskett at Harvard spent decades studying what he called the "culture cycle," ultimately arguing in his 2011 book that culture accounts for 20 to 30 percent of the differential in corporate performance compared to culturally unremarkable competitors. He is not talking about ping-pong tables. He is talking about the slow, deep shaping of how employees understand their work, their obligations, and their judgment calls when no policy covers the situation. The moral intuition that fires when something feels wrong — that is culture. That is formation.

Edgar Schein, whose Organizational Culture and Leadership (1985, now in its fifth edition) is the foundational text of the field, defined culture as "a pattern of shared basic assumptions that was learned by a group as it solved its problems of external adaptation and internal integration, that has worked well enough to be considered valid and, therefore, to be taught to new members as the correct way to perceive, think, and feel." Read that slowly. The correct way to perceive, think, and feel. Schein is describing paideia. He is describing what Plato thought Homer was doing to the Greeks. The vocabulary is different; the claim is identical.

The Formation Apparatus

Corporations did not stumble into this. The apparatus of formation was built deliberately, and it is sophisticated.

Consider onboarding. The average American company spends 1,000to1,000 to 1,000to1,400 per employee on onboarding, according to the Society for Human Resource Management — and that figure understates the real investment, which includes months of reduced productivity, manager time, and culture transmission that doesn't show up in training budgets. What is onboarding? It is initiation. It is the controlled introduction of a new person to the community's stories, language, heroes, and taboos. Every functioning onboarding program has all four. The heroes are the founding story and the people who exemplify the values. The stories are the canonical anecdotes about how the company behaved under pressure. The language is the internal vocabulary — every company above a certain size has it, and fluency in it marks belonging. The taboos are what you learn you cannot say.

This is the structure of every initiation ritual in human history. The corporation did not invent it. It rediscovered it, gave it a learning management system, and started measuring completion rates.

Amazon's Leadership Principles — fourteen statements, now sixteen, memorized and recited in interviews, deployed in performance reviews, invoked in meetings — function as a catechism. "Customer obsession," "bias for action," "frugality," "have backbone, disagree and commit." These are not descriptions of behavior; they are prescriptions for character. The word "backbone" is doing moral work. It is telling you what kind of person you should be, what you should feel when you capitulate, what you should feel when you push back. Former Amazon employees report that the principles become genuinely internalized — that they find themselves invoking them in situations outside work, that the moral vocabulary migrates. That is formation. That is the text remaking the self.

McKinsey's formation is perhaps the most thoroughgoing in the corporate world, which is why McKinsey alumni are so instantly recognizable. The firm does not just train analysts; it produces a type. The "McKinsey way" — documented partly by Ethan Rasiel in his book of that title, visible in the habits of anyone who has spent three years at the firm — includes specific rhetorical structures (the MECE framework, the pyramid principle), specific social registers (confident, data-deferring, politically agnostic in presentation), and a specific moral epistemology in which client value is the supreme good and the partner relationship is the structuring hierarchy. Ron Daniel, McKinsey's managing director in the 1970s, described the firm's ambition as building "an institution that is able to attract, develop, excite, and retain outstanding people." The word "develop" is doing the work. McKinsey is not a staffing agency. It is a formation institution that happens to sell consulting.

Duff McDonald, in The Firm (2013), quotes a former McKinsey partner: "We're in the business of creating a certain kind of person." The partner meant it as a compliment. It is also the most honest sentence anyone in the corporate world has said about what corporations actually do.

The Irony, Stated Plainly

Here is the situation we are in.

In universities, the question of whether reading Homer changes who you are is treated as either unanswerable or politically fraught. The mechanisms by which canonical texts formed Greek citizens, medieval Christians, Confucian scholars, or early Muslim jurists are studied as historical curiosities — interesting, perhaps, but not replicable, not measurable, not relevant to the contemporary curriculum. The very idea that a text could reshape the soul is considered naive, or worse, authoritarian. We assign the texts anyway, in case they convey useful information or perspectives, and then we are puzzled when nothing much happens.

Meanwhile, in the same society, the same graduates walk into organizations that have operationalized the soul-reshaping claim, built measurement systems around it, hired Chief People Officers to manage it, and generate billions of dollars in differential value from doing it well. The corporation does not wonder whether formation is possible. It has quarterly OKRs around it.

The academy studies the Confucian examination system and marvels that the Chinese state was able to reproduce a governing class through textual formation across thirteen centuries. The venture capital industry funds the next generation of companies whose culture decks will do the same thing in thirteen years, and nobody connects these facts.

Plato wanted to expel Homer from the republic because he believed Homer was forming people below the level of conscious reasoning — that the mimetic experience of epic poetry was reshaping the soul before the soul could evaluate what was happening to it. He was right to worry. He was also right about the mechanism. The Netflix culture deck works the same way. You do not read it and consciously decide to become a person who believes that adequate performance deserves a generous severance package. You absorb it, you work in an environment structured by it, you make decisions inside it, and then one day you are the person it described.

This is not a metaphor. This is what formation is.

What We Refuse to Ask

The question the corporate evidence forces — and that we refuse to follow into its implications — is: if formation is real, if texts and institutional practices genuinely remake the self below the level of conscious deliberation, then what kind of self is being produced at scale?

The answer is visible in the aggregate. The corporation forms people who are excellent at navigating complex social hierarchies while appearing not to. Who are fluent in the language of metrics and outcomes and have difficulty sustaining conversations that resist quantification. Who experience their deepest obligations — to employers, to institutions, to communities — as chosen and therefore revocable. The last point is the most consequential. The corporate formation is built on the premise of voluntary association: you chose this job, you can leave, your loyalty is contingent and professional rather than given and unconditional.

That is not simply an employment fact. It becomes a moral epistemology. The corporate-formed self extends the logic of voluntary association outward — to marriages, to neighborhoods, to political communities, to friendships. All commitments become provisional. All belonging becomes exit-optional. The question "what do I owe this?" gets answered by "what did I sign up for?" rather than "what is this?"

We are not describing bad people. We are describing people who have been formed by a specific canon, in the same way that Greek citizens educated on Homer were formed by a specific canon, and Confucian scholars memorizing the Five Classics were formed by a specific canon. The difference is that the Greeks and the Confucians knew they were being formed. They argued about it. Plato wrote the Republic in response to it. The Confucian debates about the curriculum were politically serious. We have outsourced our formation to institutions whose explicit goal is shareholder value, and we have called the resulting self autonomous.

The corporation proved that texts make people. We used the proof to make better employees. We are living in the society that decision produced, and we are puzzled by what we find there.
