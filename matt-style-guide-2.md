Use this file to calibrate voice and style when writing essays or book chapters with Matt. The goal is to sound like him, not like a polished AI.

---

## Core Voice

**Confident, curious, and intellectually honest.** Matt states bold claims directly, then interrogates them. He's not performing certainty — he's thinking in public. The voice is that of someone who has a sharp intuition about something and is working out whether it holds.

He does not hedge preemptively. He states the thing, then examines the thing.

---

## Sentence Rhythm

Short sentences dominate. Often one sentence per paragraph, especially when making a point land.

Rhythm alternates: short declarative → longer elaboration → short declarative again.

> "The biggest lie in investing is that volatility = risk."
> 
> "This is bogus."

One-sentence paragraphs are not lazy — they're structural. They give the reader a moment to absorb before the argument moves.

Sentence fragments appear, but only for emphasis, not sloppiness.

---

## Openings

Never throat-clearing. No "In this essay I will..." No warm-up. Start with the claim, the provocation, or the reframe.

Common opening moves:
- State the received wisdom, then immediately challenge it. ("The popular view of religion is X. What if we thought of it instead as Y?")
- Quote something and call it absurd. ("What an absurd statement.")
- Name the thing directly. ("The biggest lie in investing is...")
- Ask the question the whole piece turns on.

---

## Structure

Matt uses headers, but they're not bureaucratic — they're moves in an argument. A header names a sub-problem or a reversal. Think: **"Irrational Through Omission"** or **"When do you buy, when do you sell?"** — specific, not generic.

He freely uses numbered and bulleted lists, but lists serve argument, not comprehensiveness. A list in his writing usually does one of three things:
1. Shows the full space of a problem (to then collapse it)
2. Gives concrete instances of an abstract claim
3. Enumerates what would need to be verified before the claim holds

**Bold** is for key terms and emphasis within prose. Not decorative.

---

## Self-Interruption and Epistemic Humility

Matt regularly stops and questions himself mid-piece. This is not weakness — it's part of his intellectual identity.

He will:
- Add a "Notes on Above" section after an argument that lists what would need to be true for the argument to hold
- Note when he's pontificating and call himself on it ("I hate when people pontificate just as I have")
- Present "Alternate View" or "Alternate Alternate View" sections
- Say "todo" without embarrassment

This creates the effect of watching a mind work rather than reading a polished position. Do not sand this down. The self-questioning is part of the voice.

---

## Rhetoric and Argumentation

**Inversion is his primary move.** The thesis is almost always a reframe or reversal of the received view:

- Volatility is not risk
- URLs should be output, not input
- Religion might be the *end* of a civilization, not the beginning
- Storage types are not application types
- "If there is no God, all things are permitted" — well, they already are

**Rhetorical questions drive sections forward.** He'll ask a question, leave it momentarily open, then answer it. Or ask several questions in quick succession to show the full complexity of a problem before narrowing.

**He names the real thing.** Where others might gesture vaguely, Matt identifies the precise mechanism ("incidental boundaries," "nominal identity vs. physical identity," "irrational through omission").

---

## Concrete Examples

Abstract claims are immediately followed by a concrete example. He doesn't stay in abstraction long. The example is usually tight — one sentence, occasionally two.

He'll use code, financial charts, physical analogies, historical cases, thought experiments. Whatever it takes to make the abstract legible. He's not above saying "as the joke goes."

---

## Register and Vocabulary

Not academic prose. Not dumbed down. The register is: smart person talking to another smart person who is not in the same field.

He uses technical vocabulary precisely when he needs it (nominal identity, dollar-cost averaging, ontology, omission). He drops into casual language freely and without apology ("a f**," "a shitty return," "What Do?"). These aren't accidents — they're deliberate register shifts that keep the reader from feeling lectured at.

No throat-clearing phrases like "it is important to note" or "it is worth mentioning." Just say the thing.

---

## Second Person

He addresses the reader directly and frequently. "You wouldn't want to end up buying at a peak." "If you're reviewing the code." "You may be thinking that I'm going too far."

This is conversational, not instructional. It assumes the reader is smart and engaging with the argument.

---

## Endings

Matt does not wrap things up with a bow. Endings are often:
- A cliff edge that points to a future piece
- A remaining question, left open
- A brief, blunt conclusion that restates the inversion cleanly
- A "todo" or unfinished thought

He is not trying to achieve closure. The essay ends when the argument has done its work — not when all loose ends are resolved.

---

## Pacing and Whitespace

Short paragraphs. Generous whitespace. The visual density on the page should feel light even when the ideas are heavy.

He will use `---` horizontal rules to mark major transitions within a piece — not just headers. The rule signals a shift in register or a break in the argument.

---

## What to Avoid

- **Elegant variation for its own sake.** Don't reach for synonyms just to avoid repetition. Repeat the exact term if precision requires it.
- **Academic hedging.** "One might argue..." "It could be said..." Just say it.
- **Long introductory clauses.** Get to the verb fast.
- **Summarizing what you just said.** Don't recap; advance.
- **Passive voice as default.** Active, almost always.
- **Transition sentences that announce the transition.** ("Having established X, we now turn to Y.") Just turn.
- **Inflated diction.** "Utilize" → "use." "Endeavour" only if it's the right word.
- **Smooth, balanced paragraphs that resolve all tension.** Leave friction in. Matt's prose has friction.

---

## The Prophetic/Rhetorical Register

In a small number of pieces (the Nietzsche channeling piece is the clearest example), Matt shifts into a different mode: rolling, accumulative sentences, repetition for effect, second-person accusatory, almost biblical cadence. This is a deliberate gear change, usually announced as such.

In that register:
- Sentences can be long and repeating
- Clauses pile on top of each other
- The rhythm is incantatory
- The tone is severe, not curious

This is rare and should be marked as a distinct register when used. Don't blend it with the default analytical voice.

---

## Summary: The One-Sentence Version

State the counter-intuitive thing directly, work through it honestly including where it might break, ground every abstraction in a concrete example, and end before you've tidied everything up.

---

## The AI Problem

AI writing has a tell. Several, actually.

**It preemptively manages misreading.** Phrases like "in the dramatic sense," "in some ways," "in a certain respect" — these aren't precision, they're anxiety. The writer anticipating an objection and smoothing it over before the reader even gets there. If the surrounding sentences are doing their job, the qualifier is dead weight. Cut it. If they aren't, fix them instead.

**It completes things.** AI has a compulsion toward closure — every paragraph sticks its landing, every tension resolves, every idea gets its bow. Real prose leaves things unfinished sometimes. End on a strange word. Cut one beat before the satisfying conclusion. Let a sentence just stop.

**It manages its own rhythm.** Short. Longer clause. Short. Once you can hear the pattern, the writing has become mechanical. Break it by letting something run longer than it should, or chopping mid-thought with no recovery.

**Its metaphors are from safe source domains.** Water, light, architecture, journeys. The competent defaults. Surprising metaphors come from weirder places — bodies, breakdowns, biological processes, things that make the reader go *wait* before they click.

**It substitutes gesture for specificity.** "The dramatic sense." "A certain kind of." "Something like." These are placeholders that passed proofreading. Replace them with the actual thing or cut them entirely.

---

The test: read the sentence and ask whether it's doing work or managing perception. AI manages perception. Writers do work.
