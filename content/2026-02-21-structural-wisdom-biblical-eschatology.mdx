### *In the manner of Walter Benjamin's Theses*

---

## Prefatory Note

There is a well-known story about an automaton constructed to play chess. A puppet in Turkish attire, a hookah in its mouth, sat before the board and won every game. The truth was that a hunchbacked dwarf, a master of chess, sat inside and guided the puppet's hand. One can imagine a philosophical counterpart to this device. The puppet called "critical theory" is supposed to win all the time. It can easily be a match for anyone if it enlists the services of theology, which today, as we know, is small and ugly and has to keep out of sight.

The puppet is secular critical theory — Marx, Foucault, the whole apparatus of modern power analysis. The dwarf is theology. Benjamin's claim, and the claim of this essay, is that the puppet has never moved on its own.

I wrote that in 1940, about historical materialism. I write now to make the case more explicit: the biblical tradition contains what may be the most sophisticated structural analysis of power ever produced — an analysis that anticipates Marx by two millennia and surpasses him on the decisive question, which is not *how* systems of domination operate but *why liberation movements invariably reproduce them.* Marx diagnosed the disease. The prophets diagnosed the diagnosis.

<PullQuote>
"Marx diagnosed the disease. The prophets diagnosed the diagnosis."
</PullQuote>

To make this case, I must first explain what is at stake in the theological term most abused and least understood by both its defenders and its critics.

## I. What Eschatology Actually Is (And Why It Matters If You Have Never Opened a Bible)

*Eschatology* — from the Greek *eschaton,* meaning "last thing" — is the study of endings: the end of history, the end of the present age, the consummation toward which time moves. Every civilization that has reflected on its own existence has produced an eschatology, whether it calls it that or not.

The Marxist has one: the withering away of the state and the arrival of classless society. The liberal progressive has one: the gradual expansion of rights and reason until humanity reaches perpetual peace. The Silicon Valley futurist has one: the technological singularity. The nationalist has one: the restoration of a golden age. Even the nihilist has one, if only the heat death of the universe.

Eschatology is not a religious peculiarity. It is the *structure of hope* — the way any community imagines the relationship between the suffering of the present and the possibility of redemption. To have an eschatology is to believe that the way things are is not the way things must remain. Every political movement, every revolution, every reform campaign operates on an eschatological engine, whether or not it acknowledges the theology.

The question is not *whether* you have an eschatology. The question is *whether your eschatology is any good.*

The biblical tradition's eschatology is very good — not as a set of predictions about future events, but as a *structural analysis* of how power, time, and liberation interact. Its central claim, refined across roughly a thousand years of textual development, can be stated with a precision that should arrest the attention of anyone who has read Marx, Foucault, or the morning news:

**Every system designed to liberate will be captured by the very forces it was designed to overcome, and the genuine interruption of this cycle cannot come from within any system — including the one that names the problem.**

This is the insight encoded in the doctrine of the Second Coming. Not a prediction about a man descending on clouds. A *diagnosis* of a permanent condition, expressed in the only language its authors had — and, I will argue, expressed better in that language than in any subsequent secular translation.

<PullQuote>
"Every system designed to liberate will be captured by the very forces it was designed to overcome."
</PullQuote>

## II. The Engine: A Pattern Marx Discovered in One Century That Scripture Traced Across Ten

Marx's great insight was that the bourgeois revolution, which had liberated Europe from feudal bondage, had produced its own form of bondage — the domination of capital. The instrument of liberation became the instrument of oppression. He called this dialectical, and he was right. But he believed he had discovered the *mechanism,* and here he was wrong, because the mechanism was already ancient.

The Hebrew Bible tells the story of this mechanism recurring across every domain of human organization — political, legal, religious, prophetic — with a relentlessness that makes *Das Kapital* look like a case study.

**The Law.** God delivers Israel from Egypt — from slavery — and gives the Torah at Sinai as the architecture of a free society. "I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery" (Exodus 20:2). The very first words of the Law define it as *liberation technology.* The commandments, the festivals, the sabbatical and jubilee years that cancel debts and free slaves — this is a system explicitly designed to prevent the re-emergence of the conditions of Egypt.

Within a few generations, the Law has become the new Egypt.

Isaiah delivers the verdict: "What to me is the multitude of your sacrifices?… Wash yourselves; make yourselves clean; remove the evil of your deeds from before my eyes; cease to do evil, learn to do good; seek justice, correct oppression" (Isaiah 1:11, 16–17). The sacrificial system — the beating heart of Torah observance — has become the thing God rejects. The operating system designed for liberation has been captured by the very forces it was meant to neutralize: the accumulation of power by those who control the system's administration.

Marx saw this in the bourgeois state. The prophets saw it in the Temple. The prophets saw it first, and they saw it more clearly, because they saw it *as a universal law* rather than as a feature of one economic mode.

**The Temple.** Jeremiah sharpens the analysis to a point that any student of ideology will recognize. The Temple — the physical center of Israelite worship, the institution built to house God's presence among the people — has become what we would now call a *legitimation apparatus.* The people chant "The temple of the LORD, the temple of the LORD, the temple of the LORD" (Jeremiah 7:4), and Jeremiah mocks the threefold repetition because he recognizes what it is: an incantation. The sacred institution has become a spell — a mechanism by which the powerful assure themselves that the existing order is divinely sanctioned regardless of its actual justice. The Temple has become *ideology* in precisely the Marxian sense: a system of belief that obscures the real conditions of domination and presents them as natural, necessary, sacred.

But Jeremiah goes further than Marx. His solution is not the construction of a better institution but something far more radical: "I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts" (Jeremiah 31:33). The external system must be *internalized* or it will inevitably be captured. This is not pietism; it is a structural claim about the nature of institutional power. Any liberation encoded in external rules and administered by a professional class will be converted into a tool of that class. The only law that resists capture is the one that has no administrators.

Ezekiel sees the same reality in a vision that should haunt anyone who has watched a revolutionary institution become a bureaucracy. The *kavod* — the glory of God, the visible divine presence in the Temple — *departs.* It leaves the inner sanctuary, moves to the threshold, then to the east gate, then out of the city altogether (Ezekiel 10–11). God abandons God's own institution. This is not a punishment narrative. It is a *theorem:* an institution can continue to exist — performing all the correct rituals, maintaining all the formal structures, collecting all the usual revenues — while the thing that actually gave it authority has quietly departed. The Temple still stands. The priests still sacrifice. But the presence is gone. Anyone who has watched a university that still confers degrees but has lost free inquiry, a party that still claims to represent the working class but serves its professional administrators, a newspaper that still publishes but has lost the reader's trust — anyone who has seen the machinery continue to operate after the animating purpose has left the building — has seen Ezekiel's vision in secular dress. The institution built to house the divine presence has become inhospitable to it. The presence must leave in order to remain what it is.

<PullQuote>
"An institution can continue to exist — performing all the correct rituals, maintaining all the formal structures — while the thing that actually gave it authority has quietly departed."
</PullQuote>

No Marxist has ever stated the problem of institutional capture with this economy or this force. The means of spiritual production, we might say, have been seized by a priestly class that uses them to reproduce the conditions of spiritual domination. But the biblical framing is superior to the Marxist one for a reason I will return to: *it includes the critique within its own scope.* The prophets know that the prophetic institution will itself be captured. Jeremiah denounces false prophets who "speak visions of their own minds, not from the mouth of the LORD" (Jeremiah 23:16). The system designed to critique captured systems gets captured. Marx never solved this problem — who critiques the critics? — because his framework could not accommodate it. The biblical tradition *begins* from it.

**The Monarchy.** When Israel demands a king, God tells Samuel to warn them what will happen: "He will take your sons and appoint them to his chariots… He will take your daughters to be perfumers and cooks and bakers. He will take the best of your fields and vineyards" (1 Samuel 8:11–14). The monarchy is established *with a structural diagnosis built in.* God does not say this particular king will be corrupt. He describes what the *office* will do by its nature — extraction, conscription, the conversion of public authority into private accumulation. This is a theory of the state as such: political power concentrated in an institution will become an apparatus serving those who control it, regardless of its stated purpose. Engels arrived at the same conclusion in *The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State,* working through Hegel and Morgan across hundreds of pages. Samuel gets there in seven verses.

## III. Jesus as Structural Critic

To the secular reader who has been told that Jesus was a moral teacher — a Galilean Socrates dispensing ethical maxims — I must say plainly that this is like calling Marx a journalist. It is technically true and profoundly misleading.

Jesus operated as a *structural critic of institutional capture,* and every act of his public ministry is intelligible as such.

He arrived into a religious system that had performed the cycle once more. The Pharisees — and I must insist on this against both Christian anti-Semitism and liberal sentimentality — were not hypocrites in the cheap sense. They were *good-faith systematizers.* They loved the Torah. They built interpretive structures to protect it. And those structures became the new bondage. Jesus names this with the precision of a diagnostician: "You leave the commandment of God and hold to the tradition of men" (Mark 7:8). The liberation technology has been captured by its own maintenance staff.

His response is *systematically anti-systemic.* He heals on the Sabbath — not because rest is unimportant but because the Sabbath has been converted from gift to regulation (Mark 3:1–6). He touches lepers, eats with tax collectors, speaks with Samaritans — each act a deliberate violation of purity codes that were originally designed to preserve holiness but have become mechanisms of social exclusion. When asked why his disciples don't observe the ritual hand-washing, he responds with what amounts to a *structural inversion* of the entire purity system: "There is nothing outside a person that by going in can defile, but the things that come out are what defile" (Mark 7:15). The locus of the sacred moves from external compliance to internal reality. This is the same move Jeremiah made — the law written on the heart rather than administered by institutions.

Most critically for our purposes, Jesus refuses every attempt to *capture his movement within an existing eschatological framework.* After the feeding of the five thousand, the crowd wants to make him king — to slot him into the existing template of political messianism. He withdraws (John 6:15). Peter confesses him as the Messiah and immediately interprets this in terms of territorial power; Jesus responds with the most violent language in the Gospels: "Get behind me, Satan! For you are setting your mind not on divine things but on human things" (Mark 8:33). When Pilate — Rome itself, the imperial system — asks if he is a king, his answer is not denial but *relocation*: "My kingdom is not from this world. If my kingdom were from this world, my followers would be fighting" (John 18:36). He is not rejecting power; he is rejecting the *operating system* that converts every form of power into domination.

## IV. The Unknowable Day: An Epistemological Fortress

The passage in which the structural reading becomes most urgent is the Olivet Discourse (Mark 13), where Jesus predicts the Temple's destruction and then describes the coming of the Son of Man "in clouds with great power and glory." His disciples ask the question every political operative asks: *When? What is the timeline? Give us a schedule we can plan around.*

Jesus's response is the most important sentence in the eschatological tradition: "About that day or hour no one knows, neither the angels in heaven, nor the Son, but only the Father" (Mark 13:32).

This is not epistemic humility. It is an *epistemological fortress* built around the insight that matters most. The genuine rupture — the real interruption of the cycle of capture — *cannot be known in advance* because the moment it is known, scheduled, systematized, it has been absorbed into the operating system. It becomes one more item in the institutional calendar, one more lever of control. A known revolution is a captured revolution. A predicted interruption is no interruption at all.

<PullQuote>
"A known revolution is a captured revolution. A predicted interruption is no interruption at all."
</PullQuote>

Every failed revolutionary movement confirms this. The Bolsheviks knew exactly what the future held — the dictatorship of the proletariat, the withering of the state — and this knowledge *became the instrument of domination.* Because they knew the destination, they could justify any means of arrival. Because they possessed the eschatological schedule, they could declare anyone who deviated from it a counter-revolutionary. The known future became the most powerful tool of present control.

Karl Popper, writing in the shadow of both fascism and Stalinism, arrived at the same diagnosis in *The Open Society and Its Enemies*: **historicism** — the claim to know where history is heading — is the root of totalitarianism, because whoever possesses the destination controls the route. But Jesus had stated the anti-historicist position two millennia earlier, in the grammar of theology rather than philosophy: *no one knows the day or the hour.* Popper needed two volumes. Jesus needed one sentence.

The biblical tradition encodes the *opposite* principle. The future that truly liberates is the future that *cannot be possessed.* "The day of the Lord will come like a thief in the night" (1 Thessalonians 5:2). Not because God is capricious, but because any future that can be possessed by an institution will be *used* by that institution to legitimate its present power. The unknowability of the eschaton is not a limitation on human knowledge. It is a *structural defense* against the capture of hope itself.

## V. Paul: The Theorist of Systemic Capture

Paul of Tarsus is typically read as a theologian of personal salvation. He is in fact the most rigorous theorist of systemic capture in the pre-modern world.

Romans 7 is not a confessional aside about personal moral failure. It is a *diagnosis of how systems operate:*

> "I do not do the good I want to do, but the evil I do not want to do — this I keep on doing. Now if I do what I do not want to do, it is no longer I who do it, but it is sin living in me." (Romans 7:19–20)

Read this not as autobiography but as systems analysis. "Sin living in me" is not a moral stain; it is an *operating system.* It is the structural tendency by which any agent — individual, institutional, civilizational — is colonized by the very patterns it sets out to overcome. The Law — the liberation technology — has become the vector of the disease: "The very commandment that promised life proved to be death to me. For sin, seizing an opportunity through the commandment, deceived me and through it killed me" (Romans 7:10–11).

This is Marx's insight about ideology — the ruling ideas serving the ruling class — but stated with a universality Marx never achieved. Marx located the mechanism in a specific economic structure (capitalism) and predicted its resolution through a specific historical agent (the proletariat). Paul locates it in *the structure of law as such* — any law, any system, any institution, including the ones built to overthrow the previous ones. There is no privileged revolutionary class that escapes the cycle, because the cycle operates through the very act of systematization.

Paul's proposed resolution is correspondingly more radical than Marx's. He does not propose a better system. He proposes an *interruption from outside all systems* — something he calls *grace,* which he defines with the precision of a logician: "If it is by grace, it is no longer on the basis of works, otherwise grace would no longer be grace" (Romans 11:6). Grace is, by definition, that which cannot be earned, produced, or systematized. The moment you convert it into a set of conditions — a checklist, a program, a five-year plan — it ceases to be what it is. It has been captured.

<PullQuote>
"The moment you convert it into a set of conditions — a checklist, a program, a five-year plan — it ceases to be what it is."
</PullQuote>

This is why Paul reacts with such fury when the Galatian Christians return to Torah observance. The issue is not that circumcision is wrong. The issue is that it represents the *re-systematization of what was meant to be unsystematizable.* "You who want to be justified by the law have cut yourselves off from Christ; you have fallen away from grace" (Galatians 5:4). To "fall from grace" is not to lose a transaction. It is to *re-enter the cycle that grace interrupted.* The engine has recaptured them.

## VI. Revelation: The Architecture of the Uncontrollable

The Apocalypse of John is the text most abused by eschatological entrepreneurs and, properly read, the text most hostile to their enterprise.

It begins not with predictions about the end of the world but with letters to seven churches — each one a diagnosis of institutional capture *already in progress within the first generation.* Ephesus has "abandoned the love you had at first" (2:4). Sardis has "a name of being alive, but you are dead" (3:1). Laodicea is "neither cold nor hot" (3:16). The system Jesus established is already becoming the thing it was meant to oppose. The prophetic critique applies *to the institution that carries the prophecy.* The text includes its own recipients in the scope of the diagnosis.

The visions that follow are structured to *resist* systematic decoding. The seals, trumpets, and bowls do not proceed in linear sequence; they overlap, recapitulate, and interrupt each other. Nineteen centuries of interpreters have tried to extract a timeline from Revelation and have produced only competing timelines — which is itself the strongest evidence that the text was *designed* to defeat the scheduling impulse. Every attempt to map Revelation onto a prophetic calendar — from Joachim of Fiore to Hal Lindsey to the Left Behind novels — reproduces the very error the text diagnoses: the conversion of the uncontrollable into a system of knowledge that the interpreter controls.

The climactic vision is not a battle but a *descent.* The New Jerusalem comes *down* from heaven (21:2). It is not built by human hands, not engineered by political programs, not constructed by institutional effort. It *arrives* from outside. The human contribution is not construction but *reception.* "The Spirit and the bride say, 'Come'" (22:17). The entire apocalyptic tradition culminates not in a prediction but in a *prayer* — not in knowledge but in *desire,* not in a timeline but in an *orientation.*

"Come, Lord Jesus" (22:20). This is the last sentence of the Christian Bible. Not: *we will build it.* Not: *it arrives on this date.* But: *come.* The posture of invocation rather than engineering. The open hand rather than the blueprint.

## VII. The Delay That Teaches

The early church expected the end within the first generation. Paul clearly expected it in his own lifetime: "We who are alive, who are left until the coming of the Lord…" (1 Thessalonians 4:15). Jesus's words seemed to promise it: "This generation will not pass away until all these things have taken place" (Mark 13:30).

The generation passed away. The end did not come.

This should have destroyed the movement. It did not. And *why* it did not is the most important datum in the history of eschatology.

The author of 2 Peter addresses the crisis directly: "Scoffers will come… saying, 'Where is the promise of his coming?'" The answer given is typically read as an apologetic evasion: "With the Lord one day is like a thousand years, and a thousand years are like one day" (3:8). But this is not a dodge. It is a *removal of the Second Coming from chronological time altogether.* If a day is a thousand years and a thousand years a day, then "soon" and "delayed" lose their meaning. The event has been relocated outside the temporal framework within which prediction, scheduling, and institutional control operate.

The Gospel of John — likely the latest of the four canonical accounts — confirms the relocation. John's Jesus speaks of eternal life not as an afterlife but as a present-tense quality of existence: "This is eternal life, that they may know you, the only true God" (17:3). The decisive event is not deferred to a future date but *available now* to anyone who enters the posture of reception. "Now is the judgment of this world" (12:31). Not *then.* Now.

The Second Coming "failed" as a temporal prediction and *succeeded* as a structural insight. The event that cannot be scheduled, cannot be predicted, cannot be captured by any system that claims to await it — this is the event that every system needs and no system can produce. The non-arrival does not disprove the doctrine. The non-arrival *is* the doctrine.

## VIII. The Contemporary Capture — Left and Right

If this reading holds, then the two dominant eschatologies of the present moment stand equally condemned.

**The religious right** maps Revelation onto a geopolitical timeline and converts the Second Coming into a lobbying agenda. When organizations like Christians United for Israel host their annual Washington summits to promote policies that "hasten" the prophetic timeline, they perform the precise error that Jesus diagnosed in Mark 13:32. The moment you believe you can hasten the day, you have replaced the rupture with a program. You have converted the uncontrollable into a policy platform. You have done to the Second Coming what the Pharisees did to Torah and the Temple establishment did to the sacrificial system: captured the outside and put it to work for the institution.

**The secular progressive tradition** has performed the same capture in different language. When Martin Luther King Jr. quoted Theodore Parker's declaration that "the arc of the moral universe is long, but it bends toward justice," he was using the phrase as a *confession of faith* — a statement of hope against the evidence, spoken from a Birmingham jail, with no guarantee attached. But in the hands of the institutional progressive class, it has become something else entirely: a *guarantee.* The arc bends toward justice, therefore those who define justice are on the winning side of history, therefore resistance to their program is not merely wrong but historically futile. This is an eschatological claim disguised as historical observation. It converts the uncontrollable rupture into a secular *telos* — a guaranteed destination that legitimates the institutions claiming to be its custodians.

And here the reader should notice: *this is the essay's own pattern, operating in real time on an example within living memory.* A prophetic utterance — King's, born from suffering, directed toward a liberation no institution could guarantee — has been captured by the very institutional class it was meant to challenge, and converted into a legitimation formula for their authority. The same cycle Isaiah diagnosed in the sacrificial system, Jeremiah diagnosed in the Temple, and Paul diagnosed in the Law, completed within a single generation. The engine does not require millennia. It only requires administrators.

The progressive who is certain that history moves toward decolonization has the same structural relationship to their eschaton as the dispensationalist who is certain that history moves toward the rapture. Both have converted the open future into a known destination. Both have captured the outside. Both use their eschatology to *legitimate present institutional power* — which is precisely the function Isaiah, Jeremiah, and Jesus diagnosed and condemned.

The biblical tradition, read as structural analysis, says the same thing to both: *the rupture you await will not come in the form you expect, cannot be produced by the methods you employ, and will disrupt your institution no less than the ones you oppose.* "The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone" (Psalm 118:22).

<PullQuote>
"The rupture you await will not come in the form you expect, cannot be produced by the methods you employ, and will disrupt your institution no less than the ones you oppose."
</PullQuote>

The cornerstone is, by definition, the stone that *no existing building program selected.* It comes from outside the architect's plan — every architect's plan, including yours.

## IX. What Remains

What remains, if you take this reading seriously, is not a program but a *posture.*

The modern critic of institutions — Marxist, post-structuralist, decolonial — has inherited the prophetic tradition's diagnostic power without its most crucial element: the acknowledgment that *the critic's own position is subject to the same capture.* Foucault could analyze how power operates through every institution except the one from which he spoke. The university that teaches critical theory becomes the institution most resistant to criticism of itself. The revolutionary party that abolishes the ruling class becomes the new ruling class. The liberation movement that overthrows the oppressor adopts the oppressor's methods. The cycle continues, because the cycle is *structural,* not contingent — not a feature of capitalism or colonialism or any particular system, but a feature of *systematization as such.*

The biblical tradition knew this. It knew it in its bones, in the grain of its text, in the recursive pattern of its narrative. And it proposed the only coherent response: *an orientation toward something that cannot be captured because it cannot be produced.*

Paul called this *wakefulness*: "Let us not fall asleep as others do, but let us keep awake and be sober" (1 Thessalonians 5:6). Not knowledge. Not a program. Not certainty about the destination. Wakefulness — the permanent readiness to be interrupted by something no system can generate and no institution can contain.

John's Apocalypse called it the bride's cry: *Come.* Not: *we will build it.* Not: *it arrives according to our calculations.* But the open invocation directed toward the outside, the acknowledgment that what is most needed is precisely what cannot be engineered.

Jesus called it not knowing the day or the hour — and insisted that this *not-knowing* is not a deficiency but the *condition of genuine openness.* The one who knows the schedule has already been captured by it. The one who possesses the future has already converted it into an instrument of present power.

The tradition's name for this posture is *faith.* Not belief in a set of propositions. Not confidence in a timeline. Not membership in an institution that claims to administer the future. Faith as the willingness to be interrupted — to have your operating system, however sophisticated, however righteous, however critical, broken open by something you did not produce and cannot control.

"Behold, I am making all things new" (Revelation 21:5).

Not: *I have made.* Not: *I will, at a date you can calculate, make.* But: **I am making.** Present tense. Continuous aspect. The rupture is not behind us or ahead of us. It is the permanent pressure of the outside against every system that claims to be complete — every system, including the ones that critique completeness.

The only appropriate response is the one the text gives in its final breath:

*Come.*
