A note on method, prompted by the experience of writing an [essay about the fertility crisis](./2026-03-02-fertility-is-not-economics.html).

## The Sequence

The standard explanation for falling birth rates runs like this: children are expensive, women are educated, careers demand time, subsidize the costs and the problem eases. This explanation feels like it comes from the data. It doesn't. It comes from a formation — one that sees human behavior as cost-benefit optimization and therefore can only ask cost-benefit questions. "What are the barriers?" is the question the optimization framework knows how to pose. The data, thus questioned, answers obediently.

I saw the same data and asked a different question: why does the income-fertility correlation reverse within education levels? Why do people with the same degree who earn more have *more* children, not fewer? That question didn't emerge from the data. It emerged from a prior suspicion that the optimization framework was a thing to be examined rather than a transparent lens through which to examine everything else. The data didn't announce that it contained a reversal. I had to go looking, and I had to know what to look for, and knowing what to look for required a philosophical orientation I already had before I opened a single dataset.

Then I looked at satisfaction data. Women in traditional communities with dense communal structures report high life satisfaction. A standard social scientist, formed to treat individual autonomy as the baseline human condition, reaches for "adaptive preferences" or "false consciousness" — the subject reports satisfaction but doesn't know she's constrained. The debunking move feels like rigor. It is actually a prior commitment to a specific ontology: that the real self is the unconstrained self, and any satisfaction reported under constraint is suspect. I looked at the same data and asked whether the satisfaction might be real — whether the self that reports it was *built* by the communal formation, not constrained by it. That question is available only from within something like a MacIntyrean framework, where the self is constituted by practices and traditions rather than existing prior to them. The data didn't tell me to ask it. Alasdair MacIntyre told me to ask it. Or rather, the formation I'd undergone by reading MacIntyre, Heidegger, and Eckhart had built a self capable of seeing the question.

Then Israel. A demographer sees that Israeli women — highly educated, employed at rates matching the US and Europe, delaying first births like everyone else — have far more children than their OECD counterparts, and writes "pronatalist culture" or "nationalism." These are labels. They name the phenomenon without explaining it. My question was different: what kind of formation produces a self that has children despite every condition that suppresses fertility elsewhere? That question requires already having the ontological claim about community-constituted selfhood in hand. Without it, you'd never think to ask. With it, it's the obvious question.

At every level, the same data supported multiple conclusions. The economist's reading, the sociologist's reading, and mine all survived contact with the evidence. What differed was not the data but the question brought to it. And the question came from the questioner's formation.

## The Circle

This is not a new observation. Gadamer called it the hermeneutic circle: you cannot interpret without a pre-understanding, and the pre-understanding determines what the evidence is allowed to say. Kuhn called it a paradigm: normal science doesn't test its framework, it solves puzzles *within* the framework, and the framework determines what counts as a puzzle. Polanyi called it tacit knowledge: the researcher knows more than they can articulate, and what they tacitly know shapes what they explicitly find. The point is old. But it cuts deeper than most people let it.

"Just follow the data" is the epistemological equivalent of "just be yourself." It assumes a neutral agent who stands outside all frameworks and evaluates evidence from nowhere. That agent doesn't exist. The researcher who believes they are just following the data is the researcher most fully captured by their formation, because they can't see it as a formation. They think it's just how reality is. They think their questions are *the* questions.

This is the optimization framework's epistemological move, and it is the same move the fertility essay identifies at the level of ontology. The framework presents itself not as a way of seeing but as seeing itself. The economist who models children as a consumption good subject to budget constraints doesn't think they're applying a framework. They think they're being rigorous. The sociologist who treats religious satisfaction reports as evidence of false consciousness doesn't think they're importing an ideology. They think they're being critical. The framework has made itself invisible to the person formed by it — which is exactly what Heidegger means by *Gestell*. Technological enframing doesn't just enframe the objects of inquiry. It enframes the inquiry itself, so that the enframing disappears from view. You can't see the lens while you're looking through it.

## The Uncomfortable Implication

The fertility essay cannot claim to be "what the data shows" in any neutral sense. It is what the data shows to someone formed by Eckhart, MacIntyre, Heidegger, and Aristotle — someone whose pre-understanding includes the possibility that the self is constituted through community, that formation is prior to choice, that reception is a capacity built by discipline rather than a preference exercised by an autonomous agent. Someone formed differently — an economist, a liberal political theorist, a rational choice sociologist — would look at the same data and write a different essay, and they would believe just as sincerely that they were following the evidence. The data does not adjudicate between us. The formation does.

This is not relativism. The claim is not that all interpretations are equally valid, or that evidence doesn't matter, or that you can make the data say whatever you want. The Israeli fertility data really does break the delay hypothesis. The income-fertility reversal within education levels really does complicate the standard economic story. The satisfaction data really does show that women in dense communal structures report higher well-being than women in the dissolved middle. These are empirical findings that resist certain interpretations and support others. The data *constrains*. It just doesn't *determine*.

What it cannot do is generate the questions that unlock its meaning. The question "what kind of self does this institution produce?" is not inside the data. It has to come from somewhere. It comes from the researcher, and it comes from the researcher's formation — the books they've read, the communities they've inhabited, the practices that built their capacity to notice certain patterns and not others. The economist's formation builds eyes that see costs. The sociologist's formation builds eyes that see power. The phenomenologist's formation builds eyes that see the structure of experience itself. None of these is wrong. Each is partial. And each believes itself to be complete.

## The Demonstration

The deepest irony is that the fertility essay is a demonstration of its own thesis. It argues that the optimization framework produces selves incapable of receiving what lies outside the framework. It argues that formation determines perception — that what you can see depends on what you've been formed to see. And it was itself only possible because of a specific formation that built the capacity to see the question. A different formation would have produced a different essay — one about subsidies, or gender equity scores, or the price of childcare in OECD countries. That essay would be supported by data. It would feel rigorous. It would be wrong in a way that only someone formed differently could perceive.

This is the hermeneutic circle applied to its own application. You cannot escape it by trying harder or by being more rigorous or by gathering more data. You can only enter it honestly — by acknowledging that the questions you bring to the evidence are not neutral, that your formation is not invisible, and that the conclusions you draw are not what the data *says* but what the data says *to you*. The person who cannot make this acknowledgment is not more objective. They are more captured. Their formation has succeeded so completely that it has erased itself from their awareness.

The data doesn't speak. It answers. And the answer depends entirely on the question, and the question depends entirely on the questioner, and the questioner is not a free-floating rational agent but a formed self, built by specific practices, within a specific community, carrying a specific tradition's way of seeing. All the way down.
