# The Choosing: Civilizational Texts and the Societies They Made

*A study in canonization, power, and formation*

---

> "A people without a book has no history." — *attributed variously, but the sentiment is universal*

The question of which texts become civilizational is never innocent. Behind every "great book" lies a specific act — a commissioning, a festival, an editorial council, a burning of alternatives, or a slow organic elevation through use. The text and the choosing are inseparable: a society that commissions an epic of imperial founding is not the same as a society that preserves a record of defeat and exile. The text crystallizes what the society wants to be, or what it fears it is, or what it needs to forget. And then the text begins to make the society in return.

What follows is an attempt to trace the major acts of civilizational canonization from the ancient Near East through early modernity — attending closely to the mechanisms of choosing, the political conditions that shaped them, and the kinds of human beings and institutions those texts then produced.

---

## I. Mesopotamia: The Scribal Canon and the *Edubba* (~1800–600 BCE)

### The Text

The *Epic of Gilgamesh* in its Standard Babylonian version (~1300–1000 BCE), compiled and edited by the scholar-priest Sîn-lēqi-unninni. Earlier: the Sumerian *Instructions of Shuruppak*, the *Hymns to Inanna*, the *Descent of Inanna*.

### The Choosing

Mesopotamian canonization operated through the *edubba* — the "tablet house," or scribal school. The most important primary evidence is the corpus of *edubba* texts themselves, a genre of school literature describing scribal education from the inside. In one famous text (known to scholars as *Schooldays*, ETCSL 5.1.1), a student describes his daily curriculum: copying proverbs, hymns to gods, and administrative formulas. The curriculum was not chosen by kings in any direct sense; it was conserved by the scribal guilds themselves, families of *ummânu* (scholars) who controlled access to cuneiform literacy.

The Gilgamesh tradition illuminates this. The Sumerian Gilgamesh poems (five separate compositions, c. 2100–2000 BCE) circulated independently. Their consolidation into the Akkadian *Epic* — with the crucial addition of the Flood narrative from Utnapishtim, making mortality the poem's central problem — took centuries. Sîn-lēqi-unninni's Standard Babylonian version, named after him in colophon traditions, represents an act of authorial compilation unprecedented in the ancient Near East. The colophon of the Nineveh library tablets (preserved in the British Museum: K.3375+) names him as *ummânu*, master scholar. This is not state commissioning; it is guild authority shaping the tradition.

The great archive at Nineveh under Ashurbanipal (669–631 BCE) represents a second moment of canonization: the king sent agents across Mesopotamia explicitly to collect and copy tablets. The "Ashurbanipal letters" (published in Parpola's *Letters from Assyrian Scholars*) record instructions to gather "any tablet that is good for the palace." This is state collection, but it depended entirely on the prior scribal canonization. The state archived; the *ummânu* had already chosen.

### The Society Produced

The Mesopotamian scribal canon produced a culture of extraordinary textual conservatism and technical sophistication existing in profound tension. Scribes copied texts they may not have fully understood because copying *was* the piety. The *edubba* system created a literate administrative class bound to dead languages (Sumerian survived as a prestige tongue a millennium after it ceased to be spoken), obsessed with correct transmission, and deeply invested in the idea that wisdom was *old*. Gilgamesh's message — "see the walls of Uruk, read the lapis lazuli tablet, learn from the one who saw the abyss" — encodes this epistemology: wisdom is preserved record, not living inquiry.

The shadow side: this conservatism made Mesopotamian intellectual culture remarkably resistant to paradigm change. The omen literature (*enuma anu enlil*, celestial omens; *šumma ālu*, terrestrial omens) accumulated for centuries without developing into a predictive astronomy until very late, precisely because the scribal impulse was to add observations to tradition rather than revise frameworks.

## II. Egypt: Royal Theology and the Pyramid Texts (~2400–1200 BCE)

### The Texts

The *Pyramid Texts* (c. 2400 BCE, Unis's pyramid at Saqqara), the *Coffin Texts* (c. 2000 BCE, Middle Kingdom), the *Book of Coming Forth by Day* (mistranslated as *Book of the Dead*, c. 1550 BCE onward).

### The Choosing

Egyptian canonization was among the most explicitly royal of any civilization. The *Pyramid Texts* — the oldest religious corpus in the world — appear first in the burial chamber of Pharaoh Unis. The texts are spells, utterances, and hymns designed to secure the king's resurrection and celestial journey. There was no "public" version; these were royal property. Erik Hornung (*The Ancient Egyptian Books of the Afterlife*) documents how the democratization of these texts occurred gradually: the *Coffin Texts* spread them to the noble class, and the *Book of the Dead* to anyone who could pay for a papyrus scroll with their name inserted into the blanks.

The *Book of the Dead* canonization is thus a market phenomenon as much as a royal one: scribal workshops produced template papyri with gaps for the deceased's name, meaning the "choosing" was a commercial act driven by the anxiety of individual families. Yet the content remained theologically controlled by the priesthood of Amun, whose authority at Karnak rivaled the pharaoh's.

The primary texts themselves record the political tensions: the *Dispute of a Man Who Contemplates Suicide with His Ba* (Middle Kingdom, Papyrus Berlin 3024) and the *Admonitions of Ipuwer* (Papyrus Leiden I 344) suggest an alternate Egypt, one struggling with social collapse and questioning whether the cosmic order the royal texts proclaimed was real. These were preserved but never became structurally canonical — they survive as *marginal* literature, witnesses to what the official canon suppressed.

### The Society Produced

Egyptian civilization was perhaps the most successful at encoding its values in permanent form. The canon of divine kingship — Pharaoh as *Horus* incarnate, *Maat* (cosmic order/justice) as the structuring principle of reality — produced a society of extraordinary durability. Three thousand years of recognizable Egyptian culture is partly a product of this canonical stability. But it also produced a society almost structurally incapable of reform: the texts that legitimated power were also the texts that taught cosmology, ethics, and craft. To question the king was to question the Nile's flooding.

## III. The Hebrew Bible: Catastrophe as Canon (~620–400 BCE)

### The Texts

The Torah (Pentateuch), the Deuteronomistic History (Deuteronomy through Kings), the prophetic corpus, and the Writings — assembled across multiple crises: the Assyrian conquest of the North (722 BCE), Josiah's reform (621 BCE), the Babylonian exile (597–539 BCE), and the post-exilic restoration under Ezra.

### The Choosing

The canonization of the Hebrew Bible is one of the most documented and contested in scholarship, and uniquely among major canons, it records its own history of choosing — imperfectly and polemically, but visibly.

**The Deuteronomic reform (621 BCE):** 2 Kings 22 recounts that workmen repairing the Temple under King Josiah discovered a "book of the law" — almost certainly an early version of Deuteronomy. The high priest Hilkiah presents it to the king; the prophetess Huldah authenticates it; Josiah convenes an assembly and enacts sweeping religious reforms: centralizing worship in Jerusalem, abolishing rural shrines, reinstating Passover. This is a canonical event encoded in the canon itself. Scholars from de Wette (1805) onward have recognized that Deuteronomy was almost certainly *composed* (not merely *found*) in this period — the "discovery" was a legitimation device. The text was chosen by being invented in the moment of its finding.

**The Babylonian exile and the "P" source:** The Priestly source (P), which gives us the creation narrative of Genesis 1 and the Levitical law code, is now widely dated to the exile period (597–539 BCE). The priests, stripped of their Temple and their institutional base, responded by writing. The entire elaborate system of purity laws, sacrificial codes, and genealogies in Leviticus and Numbers becomes comprehensible as a response to catastrophe: if you cannot *perform* the cult, you can *memorize* it, carry it in your bodies, inscribe it on your children. The choosing here was driven by survival anxiety — the text became the portable homeland.

**Ezra and the public reading (c. 458–398 BCE):** Nehemiah 8 describes Ezra reading the "book of the law of Moses" publicly before the assembled people in Jerusalem while Levites provided interpretation. This is the first documented public canonical event in Israelite history — the text read aloud to a people who are constituted by hearing it. The Greek translation (Septuagint, c. 3rd century BCE) further stabilizes the canon for Diaspora communities, though with significant variations from the Hebrew.

**What was excluded:** The *Book of Enoch*, *Jubilees*, *Ben Sira*, and the *Wisdom of Solomon* — all circulating as authoritative texts at Qumran (Dead Sea Scrolls, c. 200 BCE–70 CE) — were eventually excluded from the rabbinic canon formalized at Yavneh after the Temple's destruction (70 CE). The Yavneh discussions (recorded in the Mishnah, Tractate Yadayim) debate which books "defile the hands" (the rabbinic formula for canonical authority). The exclusion of apocalyptic literature was almost certainly partly political: after the catastrophic revolts of 66–70 and 132–135 CE, the rabbis were suspicious of messianic texts that energized further violence.

### The Society Produced

The Hebrew Bible produced the most consequential canonization act in human history — not because of its content alone but because of the *mode* it established: the book as portable nation, the people constituted by textual interpretation rather than territory. After 70 CE, the rabbis completed the process: the Temple destroyed, Israel survived as a community of interpretation, the Talmud as the living extension of the canon.

This produced an extraordinarily resilient civilization — two millennia of coherent identity without a state — but at a specific cost: the ossification of the interpretive apparatus. The rabbinic hermeneutic (midrash, halakha, the tradition that "Torah has seventy faces") was designed to keep the text infinitely productive; but it also made certain questions *structurally unaskable* for long periods. The text's productivity was its survival mechanism and its limitation.

## IV. Athens and the Homeric Canon (~550–300 BCE)

### The Texts

The *Iliad* and *Odyssey*, attributed to Homer; Hesiod's *Theogony* and *Works and Days*; the lyric poets (Pindar, Sappho, Alcman) as educational rather than civilizational texts.

### The Choosing

The critical ancient source is a scholion on Pindar (Nemean 2.1, scholia) which records that the Peisistratids — the Athenian tyrant dynasty, particularly Peisistratos and his sons, 561–510 BCE — standardized the Homeric texts for recitation at the Panathenaic festival. Diogenes Laertius (*Lives of the Philosophers* 1.57) credits Solon with requiring rhapsodes to perform Homer "in sequence" (*eph' heirmos*), and Plato's *Hipparchus* (228b) attributes to Hipparchus, son of Peisistratos, the specific reform of sequential recitation.

The mechanisms: (1) rhapsodes competed at the Panathenaia every four years, performing assigned sections; (2) this required a stable, shared text; (3) the Peisistratid court attracted scholars (*grammatikoi*) who almost certainly worked on textual standardization. Cicero (*De Oratore* 3.137) and Josephus (*Against Apion* 1.12) both preserve versions of the tradition of Peisistratid compilation.

But the Panathenaic institutionalization built on something organic: Homer was already functioning as *paideia* before the tyrants. Plato's dialogues are the richest evidence here. In the *Ion*, Socrates interviews a rhapsode who has won at the Epidaurian festival — Homer-performance was already a pan-Hellenic practice before Athens standardized it. The *Republic* (376e, 377a) — which famously proposes to expel Homer — is only scandalous because Homer was already the educational bedrock. You cannot propose to exile what is not already enthroned.

The post-Alexandrian Alexandrian scholars (Zenodotus, Aristophanes of Byzantium, Aristarchus, c. 280–145 BCE) constituted the second major act of Homeric canonization: the Library of Alexandria collected manuscript variants, and Aristarchus produced critical texts with *obelos* (deletion marks) and *diplai* (markings of authenticity) for suspected interpolations. His commentaries (*hypomnèmata*) shaped the textual tradition that descended through Byzantium to the Renaissance.

### The Society Produced

The Homeric canon produced Greek *paideia* — the educational ideal that held Greek civilization together across enormous political fragmentation. Werner Jaeger's *Paideia* (1933) remains the best account of how the Iliad and Odyssey functioned not as entertainment but as moral instruction: Achilles as the model of *aristeia* (heroic excellence), Odysseus as the model of *mètis* (cunning intelligence), the gods as both exemplary and cautionary.

The critical tension: the Homeric gods are not moral exemplars. They are petty, jealous, adulterous, capricious. The philosophers — beginning with Xenophanes (DK 21 B11-12, c. 525 BCE), who attacked Homer for attributing to the gods "whatever is shameful and blameworthy among humans: stealing, committing adultery, and deceiving each other" — were in perpetual conflict with the canonical text. This produced Greek allegorism: the Stoics especially reread Homer as disguised philosophy, Odysseus's journey as the journey of the rational soul. The text was too central to abandon; it had to be reinterpreted. Greek philosophy grew partly in the space of tension between Homeric religion and reflective ethics.

## V. The Confucian Canon: State Philosophy as State Structure (~136 BCE–)

### The Texts

The *Five Classics* (*Wujing*): *Classic of Poetry* (*Shijing*), *Classic of History* (*Shujing*), *Classic of Changes* (*Yijing*), *Record of Rites* (*Liji*), and *Spring and Autumn Annals* (*Chunqiu*). Later, the *Four Books* (*Sishu*): *Great Learning*, *Doctrine of the Mean*, *Analects*, *Mencius* — compiled into the examination curriculum by Zhu Xi in the 12th century CE.

### The Choosing

The decisive act: Emperor Wu of Han (r. 141–87 BCE) accepted the proposal of Dong Zhongshu (recorded in *Hanshu*, "Biography of Dong Zhongshu," and in Dong's own *Luxuriant Dew of the Spring and Autumn Annals*) to establish the Imperial University (*Taixue*) with five *boshi* (erudites/professors), one for each Classic, and to make Confucian learning the sole basis for official recruitment.

Dong's argument — that the cosmos, the ruler, and human morality constitute a single harmonious system — gave the Han state an ideological foundation that was simultaneously cosmological, ethical, and political. The emperor is the hinge between Heaven and humanity; proper study of the Classics attunes the scholar to the patterns (*li*, later *dao*) underlying both. This was an explicit replacement of Legalism (the philosophy of the Qin dynasty, which had briefly unified China and then catastrophically collapsed).

The *Burning of the Books* under Qin Shihuang (213 BCE) is the shadow: the Legalist state had attempted to destroy the classical canon (burying scholars alive, burning texts outside official archives). The Han restoration of Confucian texts was thus also a narrative of canonical resurrection. Texts were reconstructed from memory — the "old text/new text" controversy (*guwen/jinwen*) that divided Han scholarship for generations concerned whether the recovered texts were authentic or reconstructed.

The second major institutionalization: the *keju* (civil service examination system), from its embryonic form in the Sui dynasty (605 CE) through its full development under the Tang and Song, made mastery of the Confucian classics the *sine qua non* of official advancement. For thirteen centuries, the governing class of China was constituted by intensive memorization and commentary on a fixed textual canon.

### The Society Produced

The Confucian examination canon produced perhaps the world's most successful example of ideological reproduction through textual formation. China's remarkable administrative continuity across dynastic changes — the persistence of the *shi* (scholar-gentry) class as the governing class regardless of who held the throne — is inseparable from the canonical system. The bureaucracy reproduced itself through the canon.

But the shadow is visible in the canon's terminus: by the 19th century, the examination system was producing scholars superbly trained in Tang-dynasty poetic forms and Classical commentary, with almost no exposure to mathematics, natural philosophy, or practical administration. The *Opium Wars* and the subsequent "century of humiliation" were met by a governing class whose entire epistemology was organized around the mastery of ancient texts. The canonical system that had sustained Chinese civilization for two millennia had become a machine for producing irrelevance.

## VI. The Buddhist Canon: Council, Schism, and Transmission (~250 BCE–600 CE)

### The Texts

The Pali Canon (*Tipitaka*: Vinaya, Sutta, Abhidhamma), the Mahayana sutras (especially *Prajnaparamita*, *Vimalakirti*, *Lotus Sutra*), the Tibetan *Kangyur* and *Tengyur*.

### The Choosing

Buddhist canonization is unique in that it was organized around *councils* — formal assemblies of monks claiming to establish authoritative transmission. The First Council at Rajagaha (c. 400 BCE, the traditional date is immediately after the Buddha's death) supposedly established the oral canon through recitation: Ananda recited the *Suttas*, Upali the *Vinaya*. The primary evidence here is entirely internal — the Pali Canon's own account in the *Cullavagga* (Vinaya Pitaka, Section XI). No external corroboration exists.

Emperor Ashoka (r. 268–232 BCE) is the decisive political figure. His rock edicts (the Girnar, Kalsi, Shahbazgarhi inscriptions — primary sources preserved in stone) proclaim *dhamma* (Buddhist moral teaching) as the organizing principle of his empire, and tradition records that he convened the Third Council at Pataliputra to resolve doctrinal disputes and dispatch missionaries. The Pali account (*Mahavamsa*, the Sri Lankan chronicle, c. 5th century CE) credits the Third Council with fixing the Theravada Tipitaka and dispatching Mahinda to Sri Lanka, where the canon was written down for the first time (c. 29 BCE, during the reign of King Vattagamani Abhaya, again per the *Mahavamsa*).

The Mahayana sutras present the most theologically fascinating canonization problem: they claim to be the word of the Buddha (*buddhavacana*) while appearing centuries after his death. The *Lotus Sutra* (*Saddharmapundarika*, c. 1st century CE) explicitly addresses this by having the Buddha explain that he had *concealed* these teachings until beings were ready. This is canonization by revealed hiddenness — the text justifies its own late emergence as part of its content.

### The Society Produced

Buddhist canonization produced strikingly different civilizations depending on which canon and which mode of transmission took root. Theravada Buddhism — with the Pali Canon as its authoritative text, accessible only through the Sangha (monastic community) — produced societies organized around the monastery as the central institution. The Thai, Sri Lankan, Burmese, and Cambodian political formations were explicitly structured around monastic authority; the king gained legitimacy by supporting the Sangha; the Sangha legitimated the king. This produced considerable social stability but also significant conservatism — the Theravada societies were not, historically, places where the canon itself was questioned.

Mahayana and especially Chan/Zen Buddhism went in a different direction: the famous Zen formula "if you meet the Buddha on the road, kill him" encodes a tradition in which the canonical texts are deployed to point *beyond* themselves. The Chinese *Platform Sutra of the Sixth Patriarch* (Huineng, 8th century CE) — arguably itself a later composition — teaches that Buddha-nature is immediate, not textually transmitted. This produced intellectual traditions of enormous flexibility, capable of absorbing and transforming Confucian and Daoist elements, but also capable of collapsing into anti-intellectual quietism.

## VII. Virgil and the Aeneid: The Imperial Commission (29–19 BCE)

### The Text

Virgil's *Aeneid*, twelve books of dactylic hexameter, composed 29–19 BCE, published posthumously against Virgil's wishes (he reportedly requested it burned) by his literary executors Varius and Tucca at Augustus's direction.

### The Choosing

The primary evidence is abundant. Suetonius's *Life of Virgil* (preserved in Donatus's biography) records that Maecenas and Augustus encouraged Virgil, and that Augustus wrote from his Spanish campaigns (27–25 BCE) requesting installments: "receive, pray, something from the *Aeneid*; whether the first sketch, or any portion whatever of the work." Virgil's letters (quoted in Donatus) respond: "I must be really mad to have undertaken so great a work."

This is not informal encouragement; Virgil was Maecenas's protégé and effectively a court poet. Maecenas had systematically gathered the major poets of the age — Horace, Propertius, Tibullus as well as Virgil — into a cultural network that served Augustan ideology. The *Aeneid*'s ideological project is visible in its own text: *Anchises's prophecy* in Book VI names Augustus as the culmination of Roman destiny — "hic vir, hic est, tibi quem promitti saepius audis, / Augustus Caesar, divi genus" ("This is the man, this is he whom you so often hear promised to you, Augustus Caesar, son of a god"). This is propaganda composed from within the court that it serves.

The poem was published unfinished because Virgil died in 19 BCE returning from Greece. His deathbed request to burn the manuscript was overridden by Augustus's explicit counter-instruction. The state did not merely commission the poem; it determined even the terms of its release.

### The Society Produced

The *Aeneid* produced Roman imperial culture's most durable self-understanding: that Roman conquest was providential, that *imperium sine fine* (empire without end) was divinely ordained, that *pietas* — duty to gods, family, and state in that order — was the supreme Roman virtue. Virgil became the Roman Homer, installed in the educational curriculum across the Empire. Augustine quotes the Aeneid from memory in the *Confessions*; it is his primary literary reference.

But — and this is the text's suppressed counter-voice, brilliantly argued by Adam Parry in his 1963 essay "The Two Voices of Virgil's *Aeneid*" — the poem also preserves a profound melancholy about what Rome's founding required. Dido is abandoned, Turnus is killed with an almost gratuitous final violence, and Aeneas throughout is driven by fate rather than desire. The great question is whether Virgil embedded a critique of Augustan imperialism in the very poem he wrote to celebrate it — and whether Augustus noticed. The poem's influence was so total that this ambiguity became generative: medieval readers could find in Virgil the prophet of Christ (the famous Fourth Eclogue), Renaissance humanists could find a model of political virtue, and later readers could find an elegy for everything empire destroys.

## VIII. The New Testament: Canonization by Controversy (~50–400 CE)

### The Texts

The twenty-seven books of the New Testament, achieving their current form through a process lasting approximately 350 years, with the Festal Letter of Athanasius of Alexandria (367 CE) providing the first list identical to the current canon.

### The Choosing

New Testament canonization is the richest case study in how a textual canon is produced by *conflict* rather than consensus. The primary sources are abundant and contradictory.

**The earliest stage:** Paul's letters (c. 50–60 CE) precede the Gospels. 2 Peter 3:15-16 (c. 100–125 CE) already treats "all his letters" as Scripture comparable to "the other scriptures" — showing that the Pauline corpus was being canonized while other texts were still being composed. The *Muratorian Fragment* (c. 170–200 CE, disputed) lists most of the current NT but explicitly rejects the *Shepherd of Hermas* for public reading (while approving private use) and does not include Hebrews, James, or the Petrine letters.

**Marcion's provocation (c. 144 CE):** Marcion of Sinope proposed the first explicit New Testament canon: only the Gospel of Luke (edited to remove Jewish references) and ten Pauline letters. His rejection of the Hebrew Bible forced the proto-orthodox church to define itself against him — the *response* to Marcion accelerated canonization. Tertullian's *Against Marcion* (five books, c. 207-212 CE) is the major primary source for Marcion's position, mediated through his opponent.

**Irenaeus of Lyon (c. 180 CE):** *Against Heresies* Book III argues that there must be exactly four Gospels — "just as there are four regions of the world, four principal winds, four pillars of the church" — because the Gnostics were multiplying gospels without end (*Gospel of Thomas*, *Gospel of Philip*, *Gospel of Truth*, etc.). This is explicit canonical reasoning: the text must be bounded because the textual proliferation of heresy threatens the community. Irenaeus fixes Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John as the canonical four.

**The Council of Nicaea (325 CE)** is traditionally associated with biblical canonization in popular memory, but the primary source — Eusebius's *Life of Constantine* and *Ecclesiastical History* — makes clear that Nicaea addressed the Arian controversy (the status of the Son relative to the Father), not the canon. Constantine's commission of fifty copies of the scriptures for Constantinople (recorded in *Life of Constantine* 4.36) required a stable text; Eusebius was charged with producing them. But the selection was already largely settled by usage.

**Athanasius (367 CE):** His 39th Festal Letter (Easter letter to the churches of Egypt) provides the first complete list of the current 27-book canon, using the word *canonizomena* (canonized). It also lists the accepted-but-not-canonical texts (Shepherd of Hermas, Didache) and explicitly rejects the "apocryphal books invented by heretics."

### The Society Produced

The New Testament's canonization by controversy produced a specifically Christian intellectual tradition: the ongoing definition of orthodoxy against heresy. Christianity became a civilization that generated its boundaries through constant internal argument about what the canonical texts meant. This is both the tradition's greatest intellectual generativity (the range from Origen to Aquinas to Barth is extraordinary) and its most destructive tendency — the identification of textual interpretation with community boundary meant that interpretive disagreement regularly became social rupture.

The specific exclusions matter. The *Gospel of Thomas* — a collection of sayings attributed to Jesus, without narrative, death, or resurrection — was excluded. Had it been canonical, Christianity would have developed a Wisdom-centered rather than apocalyptic-centered tradition. The *Didache* (early Christian manual of practice) was listed by Athanasius as edifying but not canonical. Its inclusion might have produced a more practice-centered, less doctrinally focused tradition. What was excluded shaped what was produced as much as what was included.

## IX. The Quran: Canonization Under the Sword (~610–656 CE)

### The Text

The Quran in its Uthmanic recension, standardized under the third Caliph Uthman ibn Affan (r. 644–656 CE).

### The Choosing

The primary Islamic sources — *hadith* collections, particularly Sahih al-Bukhari (9th century, recording traditions traced to the Prophet's companions) and the account in Ibn Abi Dawud's *Kitab al-Masahif* ("Book of Codices") — describe three stages.

**First stage:** During the Prophet Muhammad's lifetime (610–632 CE), revelations were memorized by companions and written on "bones, leather, and palm-fronds" (al-Bukhari, *Sahih*, Book 61). There was no single codex; the Quran was primarily an oral phenomenon — *qur'an* means "recitation."

**Second stage:** After the Battle of Yamama (632 CE), where many *huffaz* (memorizers of the entire Quran) were killed, Abu Bakr (first Caliph) directed Zayd ibn Thabit to compile a written collection. The primary account (al-Bukhari 6:61:509) describes Zayd's methodology: he accepted a verse only when he had two witnesses who had heard it from the Prophet directly. This collection remained with Abu Bakr, then Umar, then Umar's daughter Hafsa.

**Third stage:** Under Uthman, as the Islamic Empire expanded, reports reached Medina of regional variations in Quranic recitation — specifically, that armies from Syria and Iraq were arguing about correct readings during military campaigns. Uthman convened a committee (Zayd ibn Thabit and three Qurayshi companions) to produce a standard codex. Uthman then ordered all variant codices burned. The primary sources record that some companions, particularly Ibn Mas'ud (who had his own earlier collection), refused to surrender their codices — an act of canonical resistance suppressed by Uthmanic authority.

The Quranic text's arrangement — 114 suras, arranged roughly from longest to shortest (with the brief opening *Fatiha* first), not chronologically — is thus an editorial decision made under political urgency in the early caliphate. The traditional Islamic view is that this arrangement reflects divine guidance; the historical view notes that it solved a concrete political problem.

### The Society Produced

The Quranic canon produced a civilization with perhaps the most intense relationship to textual recitation of any in history. The obligation to recite Quran in the original Arabic, regardless of one's native language, made Arabic the *lingua sacra* of an enormous and diverse civilization from Andalusia to Indonesia. The Arabic alphabet was adopted for Persian, Turkish, Urdu, Swahili, and dozens of other languages in the wake of Islamic expansion.

The *ijaz* (inimitability of the Quran) — the theological doctrine that the Quran's literary perfection constitutes a miracle — produced Arabic philology, grammar, and literary criticism as theological disciplines. Al-Khalil ibn Ahmad al-Farahidi's *Kitab al-Ayn* (c. 786 CE), the first Arabic dictionary, was motivated by the need to precisely define Quranic vocabulary. Sibawayhi's *Al-Kitab* (c. 760–796 CE), the foundational Arabic grammar, emerged from the same impulse.

The shadow: the canonization of a single Quranic text burned out the diversity of early recitation traditions (the *qira'at*, seven accepted reading traditions, represent a partial preservation of what was standardized away). The early Mutazilite theological school, which argued that the Quran was *created* rather than eternal, was ultimately suppressed — the doctrine of the uncreated, eternal Quran became orthodox, which made certain questions about historical context structurally dangerous to ask for long periods.

## X. Dante's *Comedy* and the Exception (~1308–1374 CE)

### The Text

The *Commedia* (later *Divina Commedia*), composed 1308–1320 CE, circulating in manuscript from approximately 1314 onward.

### The Choosing

As the opening exchange notes, this is the extraordinary exception: Dante was not chosen by power. He was exiled by it. The Florentine Black Guelph government condemned him *in absentia* in January 1302, later amending the sentence to death if he returned to Florence. He never did.

The primary source for the *Comedy*'s early reception is Giovanni Boccaccio, whose *Trattatello in laude di Dante* (1351–55, revised c. 1360) is both biography and canonization document — Boccaccio was the first to attach the adjective "divine" (*divina*) to the *Commedia*. More concretely, Boccaccio delivered a public lecture series on the *Comedy* in Florence in 1373 (the *Esposizioni sopra la Comedia di Dante*), the first public university-style treatment of a vernacular text — an explicit bid to install Dante as the Homer of the Italian tradition.

The *Comedy*'s spread was manuscript-driven, humanist-mediated, and vernacular. Unlike Virgil, unlike the biblical texts, unlike the Confucian classics, Dante's canonization came from *below* the official structures — from the humanists' enthusiasm, from the copying networks, from readers who recognized that something had happened that the official culture had missed. The political establishment of his time had every reason to suppress him: he put two popes in hell, named corrupt Florentine bankers to eternal torment, and made the vernacular — Tuscan Italian — the vehicle for a comprehensive claim about the order of reality.

Leonardo Bruni's *Life of Dante* (c. 1436) is the next major canonization document, part of a humanist project to establish a Florentine literary tradition that could rival the classical. Cristoforo Landino's *Comento sopra la Comedia* (1481), produced with the patronage of Lorenzo de' Medici and printed by Niccolò di Lorenzo della Magna, marks the moment when the *Comedy* finally received official Florentine state backing — 160 years after its composition, and 179 years after Dante's exile.

### The Society Produced

The *Comedy* produced the Italian literary language — more precisely, it was instrumental in the process by which Tuscan became the *prestige* Italian vernacular. Bembo's *Prose della Volgar Lingua* (1525), the foundational treatise on Italian literary style, proposed Petrarch and Boccaccio as models of prose and lyric respectively, but Dante was the implicit standard against which everything was measured. The *questione della lingua* — the debate about which form of Italian should serve as the national literary language — had Dante at its center for three centuries.

But the *Comedy*'s deeper formation was theological and imaginative rather than linguistic. It gave European Christianity its most vivid spatial imagination of the afterlife — Dante's Hell, Purgatory, and Heaven became *the* common reference point for thinking about judgment, suffering, purification, and beatitude. When Milton writes *Paradise Lost*, he is working in a tradition the *Comedy* had shaped. When Eliot writes *The Waste Land* (the first line of which echoes Inferno I), he is working in the same tradition.

The *Comedy*'s specific contribution to political thought: by placing both imperial and papal figures in Hell — including popes still living at the time of the fictional journey — Dante asserted that neither temporal nor spiritual power was exempt from moral judgment. This is not the same as liberalism, but it is a crucial predecessor: the claim that authority is accountable to a standard it does not itself control.

## XI. The King James Bible: Translation as State Formation (1604–1611)

### The Text

*The Holy Bible, Conteyning the Old Testament, and the New*, produced by a committee of 47 scholars organized into six companies working at Westminster, Oxford, and Cambridge, 1604–1611.

### The Choosing

The primary documents are the *Preface to the King James Bible* (written by Miles Smith, 1611) and the conference records at Hampton Court (1604) where James I authorized the new translation following a petition by Puritan leader John Rainolds. James's motivation was political as much as theological: the Geneva Bible (1560), the Calvinist translation then most popular in England, contained marginal notes that James found dangerously subversive — notes that justified resistance to tyrannical monarchs, for instance. The KJV was explicitly produced without marginal notes, stripping the text of the commentary that had made the Geneva Bible a Puritan political document.

The translators' *Preface* is explicit about the politics: the new translation is presented as a gift from the king to his people, completing the Reformation's work. "Translation it is that openeth the window, to let in the light; that breaketh the shell, that we may eat the kernel; that putteth aside the curtain, that we may look into the most holy place." This rhetoric of access conceals the standardization: one translation to replace the proliferating alternatives.

The choice to use "church" rather than "congregation" (which Tyndale had used) — preserving the institutional rather than the gathered-people sense of the Greek *ekklesia* — was a deliberate ecclesiastical-political decision favoring the established Church of England over the congregational Puritan tendency.

### The Society Produced

The KJV produced English prose style. Its influence on the rhythms and cadences of English literature from the 17th century through the 20th is so pervasive as to be nearly impossible to disentangle. Lincoln's rhetoric, Melville's periods, Faulkner's sentences — all are saturated with KJV cadence. The *Authorized Version* (its other name, marking its royal authority) also became the dominant vehicle of Protestant Christianity across the British Empire, shaping the religiosity of cultures from West Africa to India to the American frontier.

The specific theological formation: the KJV's magnificent formal English made the biblical text feel archaic and transcendent in a way that made contemporaneous Puritan theology feel *smaller* by comparison. The text was too grand for its interpreters. This produced both genuine reverence and a kind of aesthetic conservatism — the KJV's beauty became a reason not to revise it, long after scholarly work had improved understanding of the original languages.

## XII. Shakespeare and the First Folio: Canonization from the Grave (1623–1800)

### The Text

*Mr. William Shakespeares Comedies, Histories, & Tragedies* (the First Folio, 1623), compiled by John Heminges and Henry Condell, two actors from the King's Men, seven years after Shakespeare's death.

### The Choosing

This is the most ambivalent act of canonization in the list: the man who would become the English Homer had no particular interest in posterity for his plays. Shakespeare did not prepare his plays for publication; eighteen of his plays survive only because of the First Folio; he seems to have regarded them as working scripts rather than literary monuments. The text was assembled from multiple sources — prompt books, actors' memories, earlier quarto editions of varying quality — and contains significant errors and inconsistencies.

The Folio's prefatory material is the canonization document: Heminges and Condell address "the great Variety of Readers," and Ben Jonson contributes the famous encomium ("He was not of an age, but for all time!"). Jonson's poem is doing something specific: it is claiming Shakespeare for the humanist tradition of literary immortality — comparing him to Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides, Plautus, Terence, Aristophanes. This is the claim that will later become obvious; in 1623 it was a gamble.

The 18th-century canonization is the decisive moment. Nicholas Rowe's edition (1709, the first scholarly edition) introduced act and scene divisions, stage directions, and a biography. Pope's edition (1725) and Johnson's edition (1765) — Johnson's Preface being one of the greatest critical essays in English — completed the installation. Garrick's Stratford Jubilee (1769), a festival celebrating Shakespeare with no performance of his plays (rained out) but enormous cultural performance, marks the point at which Shakespeare became a civic religion.

### The Society Produced

Shakespeare's canonization — bottom-up (theatre), consolidated by editors, completed by nationalist and imperial cultural investment — produced English literary culture's central reference point. The 18th-century Shakespeare boom was partly a response to French cultural dominance: England needed a Homer to match France's Racine and Molière, and Shakespeare was retrospectively elected. The German Romantics (Herder, Schlegel) completed the universalization: Shakespeare became the model of natural genius against neoclassical rules.

The specifically imperial dimension: the teaching of Shakespeare in colonial India, West Africa, and the Caribbean as the summit of human literary achievement was a civilizational claim — not merely aesthetic but ontological. Macaulay's *Minute on Indian Education* (1835), which proposed English as the language of educated India, had Shakespeare as its implicit standard. The backlash — from Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o's *Decolonising the Mind* (1986) to postcolonial Shakespeare studies — is a response to this specifically imperial canonization.

## Patterns: What the Archive Reveals

Surveying these acts of choosing, several patterns emerge:

**1. Canonization happens at the edges of catastrophe.** The Hebrew Bible was shaped by the exile; the Quran was standardized when variant recitations threatened military cohesion; the Pali Canon was written down when memorizers died in battle; the *Comedy* was written in the shock of exile. Texts are fixed when loss threatens — loss of state, of unity, of the speaker's authority, of the living transmission chain.

**2. The choosing always excludes as much as it includes.** The Gnostic gospels, the variant Quranic codices, the apocalyptic texts rejected from the rabbinic canon, the Mahayana sutras that the Theravada councils rejected — every canonization creates a shadow library of what was deliberately set aside. The exclusions are often as formative as the inclusions: early Christianity was shaped by deciding it was *not* Gnosticism; rabbinic Judaism was shaped by deciding it was *not* apocalypticism; the KJV's English was shaped by deciding it would not be the Geneva Bible.

**3. State commission and organic canonization produce different texts.** The *Aeneid* knows it is state commission; its melancholy is the melancholy of a text that serves a power it cannot fully believe in. The *Comedy* knows it is exile literature; its fury is the fury of a text that serves a truth the powers will not acknowledge. Homer is somewhere between — the oral tradition was older than the Athenian institutionalization, and the poem retains its archaic ambivalence about heroic values precisely because it was not composed *for* the state that later canonized it.

**4. Canonical texts reshape the physiognomy of the mind.** The Confucian canon produced a particular kind of memory — enormous, precise, cross-referenced to the classical corpus. The Talmudic canon produced a particular kind of argumentation — lateral, precedent-obsessed, comfortable with unresolved dispute. The Quran produced a particular kind of recitation — embodied, rhythmic, non-paraphraseable. These are not incidental effects; they are the deep formation that civilizational texts accomplish, below the level of explicit ideology, in the texture of how trained minds move.

**5. The text eventually exceeds the choosing.** Augustus got the *Aeneid* he commissioned and something he did not commission — a poem whose subterranean grief about empire would trouble every generation that came after. The rabbis who closed the canon against apocalypticism nonetheless preserved Daniel. The Athenian democrats who institutionalized Homer could not prevent Plato from using him as the foil for philosophy. The text, once loosed into time, no longer belongs to whoever chose it. This is the paradox of canonical authority: it depends on fixing the text, but the text's power comes from its inexhaustibility.
