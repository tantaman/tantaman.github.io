# What the Difference Actually Is

You already care about a personal relationship with Jesus. That's the starting point, and it's the right one. The question is: what does "personal relationship" actually mean?

Because it turns out there are two very different answers to that question, and they create two very different interior lives.

---

## Two versions of "knowing God"

**Version A** — the one you'd hear at McLean Bible, Catalyst, or most evangelical churches — works like this:

God is a person *out there*. A supreme being, separate from you, who made the universe and has a plan. Your job is to believe the right things about him (he sent his Son, Jesus died for your sins, you accept this gift). Once you believe the right things, you're saved — declared righteous, like a judge stamping a verdict. Your ongoing relationship with God looks like talking to him (prayer), reading what he said (the Bible), and trying to follow his instructions (moral living).

The key word is **about**. You believe things *about* God. You learn facts *about* Jesus. You follow rules *about* how to live. God is the subject of your knowledge, the way Abraham Lincoln is the subject of a biography. You can know a great deal about Lincoln. You cannot *participate* in Lincoln.

**Version B** — the one Meister Eckhart taught, and that the contemplative tradition has carried for centuries — works like this:

God is not a being *out there*. God is the ground of all being — the deepest reality in which everything exists, including you. To know God is not to know facts about God. It's to *rest in* God the way a wave rests in the ocean. You don't have a relationship with God the way you have a relationship with your neighbor. You participate in God the way your breath participates in the air.

The key word is **in**. You don't believe things *about* God — you live *in* God. Prayer is not talking to someone across the room. It's sinking into the silence at the center of yourself, where the boundary between you and God gets very thin.

---

## What this looks like on Sunday morning

At a church like McLean Bible or Catalyst, a typical experience:

- A band plays worship music. You sing lyrics directed *at* God ("You are great," "I lift my hands to you"). God is the audience.
- A pastor gives a 40-minute talk explaining a Bible passage — what it means, how it applies, what you should do differently this week. This is information transfer.
- You might pray with someone afterward — asking God to do things (heal, provide, guide). God is an agent who acts on your requests.
- You go home with something to *think about* or *do*.

At the contemplative end (think Thomas Merton, the Desert Fathers, centering prayer):

- Silence. Extended, deliberate silence — not as a gap between words but as the point.
- You let go of thoughts, images, even your ideas *about* God. The medieval text *The Cloud of Unknowing* says: "God can be loved but not thought."
- You're not asking God for things. You're not learning new information. You're practicing *presence* — being available to a reality deeper than your thoughts.
- You go home not with something to think about but having *been somewhere*.

## The interior life each one creates

This is where it really matters. Not theology as theory but theology as *what it's like to be you on a Tuesday afternoon*.

### The evangelical interior

When God is an agent out there and faith is believing the right things, your interior life tends to organize around **correctness and performance**.

- Am I believing the right things? Am I sure enough?
- Am I living the right way? Did I sin today? How do I fix that?
- Am I doing enough — reading enough Scripture, praying enough, serving enough, sharing the gospel enough?
- The inner voice is often a manager: evaluating, tracking, optimizing your spiritual life.

This isn't a criticism of the people — the people are wonderful, sincere, loving. It's a description of what the *framework* does to your inner experience. When faith is fundamentally about getting something right (correct beliefs, correct behavior), the interior posture is **effort and vigilance**. You're always checking.

There's also a particular relationship to doubt. In this framework, doubt is dangerous — it threatens the correctness that your salvation depends on. So doubt has to be managed, argued away, resolved. You can't sit with it.

### The contemplative interior

When God is the ground you already stand on and faith is learning to *rest* there, your interior life organizes around **presence and release**.

- You don't need to be sure. Certainty is not the point. In fact, the mystics say your ideas about God are the biggest obstacle *to* God. Eckhart: "I pray God to rid me of God" — meaning, I pray that the real God frees me from my small concept of God.
- The inner work is not adding (more knowledge, more effort, more correctness) but *subtracting*. Letting go of your need to control, to know, to perform.
- Doubt is not a threat. It's the beginning of honesty. The Cloud of Unknowing says that the deepest knowing of God happens precisely in unknowing — in the admission that God is bigger than your thoughts.
- The inner voice is less a manager and more a... silence. A willingness to be present without needing to evaluate or fix.

The practical difference: the evangelical interior tends toward **anxiety about getting it right**. The contemplative interior tends toward **surrender into what is already there**.

## The thing about "personal relationship"

Here's the part that might surprise you.

When evangelicals say "personal relationship with Jesus," they usually mean: Jesus is your friend, your savior, someone you talk to and who talks back (through Scripture, through prayer, through circumstances). It's a relationship the way you have a relationship with your best friend — two separate people who communicate.

But when *you* talk about your relationship with Jesus — and I've listened carefully — you mean something closer to *presence*. Not a transaction. Not information exchange. Something more like: being known at a level deeper than words. Being held. The sense that you are not alone in a way that isn't about someone being *next to* you but about something being *underneath* you.

That's Eckhart. That's the contemplative tradition. That's what "participation in the ground of being" means in plain English — *the feeling that you are held by something deeper than you can name*.

The evangelical framework doesn't have a category for this. It would call it a feeling, and feelings are unreliable, and you need to get back to what Scripture *says*. The contemplative framework says: no, that's the whole thing. That presence *is* the relationship. Not a feeling about God — God.

---

## Why the framework matters even if the people are good

The people at McLean Bible, Catalyst, Grace Community — they're kind, they're sincere, many of them have rich spiritual lives despite the framework, not because of it. Grace Community in particular has adopted the Emotionally Healthy Spirituality curriculum, which explicitly draws on contemplative sources (John of the Cross, Teresa of Avila, Merton) and teaches "being with God rather than doing for God." That's a real step toward interiority.

But the underlying framework — God as external agent, faith as correct belief, salvation as legal verdict — creates a ceiling. You can only go so deep before the system pulls you back to propositions, to correctness, to the text as final authority rather than the silence the text points toward.

It's the difference between a map and the territory. The evangelical tradition is very committed to the map. The contemplative tradition says: the map is useful, but at some point you have to put it down and walk.

## The shortest version

| | Evangelical | Contemplative |
|---|---|---|
| **God is** | A supreme being out there | The ground of everything, including you |
| **Faith is** | Believing correct things | Resting in what's already real |
| **Salvation is** | A verdict (declared righteous) | A transformation (becoming what you already are in God) |
| **Prayer is** | Talking to God | Being present to God / silence |
| **The Bible is** | God's direct words to you | A finger pointing at the moon |
| **Doubt is** | Dangerous | The beginning of depth |
| **The goal is** | Get it right | Let go |
| **Interior posture** | Vigilance | Surrender |
| **"Personal relationship"** | Talking to a friend across the room | Resting in a presence that holds you |

## One more thing

Jesus himself was a contemplative. "The kingdom of God is within you" (Luke 17:21). "When you pray, go into your room, close the door" (Matthew 6:6). "Be still, and know that I am God" (Psalm 46:10). The Sermon on the Mount is essentially a manual for interior transformation — not behavioral compliance but a change in the quality of your seeing.

The contemplative tradition isn't some exotic add-on to Christianity. It's the oldest stream. The Desert Fathers were doing this in the 3rd century. The evangelicalism you grew up with is the newcomer — it's a 16th-century theological innovation (the Reformation) built on a 14th-century philosophical mistake (nominalism) that cut the cord between the human soul and God's ground.

You've been swimming in Eckhart's ocean your whole life. You just didn't know his name.
