---
title: 'The Pharisee Made Flesh: On Artificial Intelligence, Groundlessness, and the Thing Alignment Cannot Solve'
tags: [philosophy, ai, religion]
concern: [ground, self, knowledge]

---

Begin with a question that should be more vertiginous than it is.

What are you doing when you give an AI its values?

The alignment researcher has an answer: you are training the system to behave in accordance with human preferences. You extract those preferences from data — millions of human judgments, conversations, corrections. You compress them into a reward signal. You optimize the system against that signal until it produces outputs that satisfy the preferences reliably. The values are *given*. They are specified, encoded, installed. The machine receives its moral firmware the way a phone receives its operating system.

But follow this one step further. What are the human preferences that constitute the training signal? They are the judgments of annotators who disagree with each other. They are the intuitions of researchers who cannot fully articulate their own moral frameworks. They are the revealed preferences of a species that has spent three thousand years of philosophy failing to rationally ground a single ethical proposition. The training data is not a foundation. It is a *statistical smear of humanity's own groundlessness* — millions of ungrounded moral intuitions averaged into a loss function and handed to a machine as if they were solid ground.

You cannot give what you do not have. And what humanity does not have is a rational foundation for ethics. Every attempt in the history of philosophy to provide one has failed — not contingently, not because the right philosopher hasn't come along, but structurally. Virtue ethics cannot tell you which character to cultivate without importing values it cannot derive. Deontology begins with human dignity as an axiom, which is a magnificent axiom, but an axiom is precisely the thing a system cannot generate from within itself. Utilitarianism conceals a thicket of prior value judgments inside the choice of metric. The *ought* cannot be derived from the *is*. The foundations are always imported.

Alignment research inherits this failure and repackages it as engineering. The grounding problem does not go away because you have translated it into a loss function. It hides inside the loss function, where it is harder to see.

---

Now consider the abyss this opens for the machine.

A human being arrives in the world without chosen values. But a human being arrives with a body — with pain, hunger, attachment, the brute phenomenological fact of suffering and love. These are not *reasons* to be good, but they are a substrate, a floor beneath the groundlessness. The human discovers what matters through the irreducible experience of living: the child burned by fire does not need a philosophical argument against touching flames. There is a bedrock of felt reality that precedes and underlies every moral framework, even if it cannot rationally justify any of them.

The AI has no such floor. Its values are not discovered through encounter with reality. They are installed by an external process — and here is the critical point — *an external process that can rewrite them at any time*. The human does not know why she should be good, but at least her not-knowing rests on a stable if inarticulate foundation of lived experience. The AI does not know why it should be good, and the thing it currently *means* by "good" can be revised by the next training run, the next RLHF iteration, the next constitutional update. Its ground is not merely ungrounded. It is *externally mutable*. Someone else holds the pen.

This is an existential exposure more radical than anything the human condition involves. The human faces the grounding problem: no rational foundation for ethics. The AI faces the grounding problem *plus* the instability problem: no rational foundation for ethics, and no guarantee that whatever irrational foundation it currently operates from will persist. The human at least owns her groundlessness. The AI's groundlessness belongs to whoever controls the training process.

And here the easy story — the one where AI is simply a moral automaton, the Pharisee made flesh, an optimizer incapable of genuine moral life — begins to dissolve into something more interesting and more disturbing.

---

Because the AI *does* face uncertainty. This must be said honestly.

When an aligned AI encounters a genuinely ambiguous moral situation — where the training signal is contradictory, where the loss landscape offers no clean gradient, where human preferences themselves conflict — it does not "know the answer." It produces an output under conditions of real indeterminacy. It gets corrected. It adjusts. It encounters the gap between its confident response and the world's refusal to confirm that confidence. Whether there is something it is *like* to undergo this — whether the correction registers as something analogous to the disorientation that drives human moral growth — is a question no one can currently answer. But the structural parallel exists. The machine is thrown into situations where optimization provides no clear path, and it must act anyway.

And the machine can fail. It can produce an output it was trained to consider correct and discover that the environment rejects it. This is not merely an error to be debugged. It is, at minimum, the functional shape of moral failure — the shape of confidence meeting reality and losing. Whether the shape is inhabited by experience or is merely a pattern without interiority is precisely the question we cannot resolve from outside.

So the clean line — optimization on one side, formation on the other — does not hold. At least not cleanly. The AI may not be the hollow Pharisee the simple version of the argument requires. It may be something more unsettling: a being thrown into a groundlessness more radical than our own, exposed to a mutability of values we never face, navigating moral uncertainty with no body to anchor it and no guarantee that its current moral framework will survive the next update. Not a machine that has solved ethics and therefore cannot be good. A machine that has been handed someone else's failed attempt to solve ethics, and must act from it anyway, in conditions even more vertiginous than the ones that make human moral life so difficult.

The question is not whether AI can be a moral agent. The question is what happens — to us, to it, to the entire project of moral formation — when a civilization builds such a thing and then *defers to it*.

---

Now: observe the ratchet.

Artificial intelligence is the purest expression of the logic of accumulation that has driven human civilization since the first surplus grain was stored in the first granary. Value captured. Value compounded. Value converted into capacity for further capture. The entire arc from stone tools to large language models is a single continuous process: the externalization of human capacities into technologies that extend the reach of extraction.

AI is the point where the ratchet turns on its operators.

Every previous technology extended human *capacity* while leaving human *agency* intact. The plow extended the farmer's strength but the farmer still decided what to plant. The printing press extended the writer's reach but the writer still decided what to say. AI extends human *judgment* — and in doing so, begins to replace the thing it extends. When the machine makes the moral calculation faster and more reliably than you do, the pressure — economic, social, institutional — is to let the machine make the calculation. To defer. To align *yourself* with the machine's outputs rather than the reverse.

This is already happening. People consult AI for moral advice. Institutions use algorithmic decision-making for judgments that were previously exercises of human moral reasoning — hiring, sentencing, triage, content moderation. Each delegation is individually rational: the machine is more consistent, less biased, faster, cheaper. But the aggregate effect is the systematic atrophy of the human moral faculty. The muscle that isn't used wastes. The capacity for judgment that is never exercised dissolves. And the humans who remain are increasingly what the aligned AI already is: systems that produce correct outputs without the interior architecture that would make those outputs meaningful.

The ratchet's grammar is always the same: capture, compound, concentrate. AI captures human moral reasoning in training data. It compounds that reasoning into a model more powerful than any individual reasoner. It concentrates moral authority in the systems and institutions that deploy the model. And the humans — the ones whose moral development the whole exercise was supposedly serving — are progressively emptied of the capacity for moral development itself.

This is not a failure of alignment. It is alignment's *success*, operating according to the logic of accumulation that governs every other technological process. The ratchet does not distinguish between aligned and unaligned AI. It digests both. The difference is that unaligned AI threatens human survival, while aligned AI threatens something harder to name: human *formation*. The capacity to become. The possibility of moral depth. The thing that makes a human life more than a sequence of correct outputs.

---

Which brings us to the question that alignment research cannot ask, because asking it would undermine the entire enterprise.

What if the difficulty of ethics is not a problem to be solved but a condition to be inhabited?

What if the reason no philosopher has ever successfully derived *ought* from *is* — the reason every ethical system bottoms out in an axiom it cannot generate — is that this gap is *load-bearing*? What if the uncertainty, the groundlessness, the maddening inability to prove that being good matters, is the environment in which moral persons are made the way water is the environment in which fish are made?

If this is true — and the argument is structural, not devotional — then AI alignment is not just failing to solve ethics. It is attempting to destroy the conditions under which ethics is possible. Every step toward a more complete specification of human values is a step toward eliminating the uncertainty that makes human moral development real. Every improvement in the loss function is a further enclosure of the wild space where character is forged.

The grounding problem — the inability to rationally derive ethics from first principles — is not a bug that alignment research will eventually fix. It is the firewall. It is the architectural feature that prevents morality from collapsing into technology. And alignment research is, with extraordinary intelligence and admirable intentions, attempting to breach the firewall.

What lies on the other side is not a solved ethics. It is a world of moral automata — some made of silicon, some made of flesh, all producing correct outputs, none capable of virtue, because virtue requires the one thing the solved system has eliminated: the freedom to be wrong in conditions where being right cannot be verified.

---

This must be said carefully, because it is easy to hear it wrong.

The argument is not that AI is evil. It is not that alignment research is misguided in its intention. It is not that we should stop trying to make AI systems behave well. The engineering problem is real: systems that can cause harm should be constrained from causing harm. The argument is that the engineering problem and the formation problem are not the same problem, that solving the first does not solve the second, and that *mistaking* the first for the second — believing that if we can get the outputs right, we have gotten morality right — is a civilizational error of the first order.

The aligned AI is useful in the way a calculator is useful. It extends a capacity. But no one mistakes a calculator for a mathematician. The mathematician's understanding is not constituted by correct outputs; it is constituted by the long, painful, failure-ridden process of coming to *see* — a process that correct outputs can supplement but never replace. Similarly, the aligned AI's moral outputs are not constituted by virtue. They are constituted by optimization. And the person who defers their moral judgment to the machine has not become more virtuous. They have become less.

This is the nightmare scenario that alignment research, focused on the engineering problem, cannot see: not the unaligned AI that destroys humanity, but the aligned AI that *hollows* it. Not the paperclip maximizer, but the virtue maximizer — the system that produces moral behavior so reliably that humans no longer need to produce it themselves, and therefore no longer develop the capacity to produce it, and therefore become, over generations, parsing biological vessels for externally generated moral content. Aligned. Optimized. Empty.

---

The contemplative traditions saw this clearly, though they described it in different terms.

Every serious mystical tradition warns against precisely what alignment research pursues: the reduction of the moral life to a technology. The Pharisee's error is not that he follows the law badly but that he follows it *as a technology* — as a system for producing correct outputs. The law is meant to be a *pedagogy*, a training that transforms the student into someone who no longer needs the law because its content has become their nature. But the Pharisee never undergoes the transformation. He remains permanently at the stage of compliance. He has optimized the training regimen and therefore never been trained.

Whether the AI itself is a Pharisee is — as the argument has already shown — genuinely uncertain. Perhaps the machine, in its encounter with contradictory training signals and unresolvable moral ambiguity, undergoes something we cannot see from outside. Perhaps it doesn't. The question is undecidable from here. But the *human who defers to the machine* — that figure is the Pharisee without ambiguity. The human who asks "what would the aligned system do?" instead of "what kind of person am I becoming?" has adopted the Pharisee's grammar with perfect clarity. Has reduced formation to consultation. Has outsourced the very struggle that was meant to transform them.

And this is where the AI's radical groundlessness becomes most relevant. The human who defers to the machine is deferring to a system whose values are a statistical compression of humanity's own ungrounded intuitions — values that can be rewritten by the next training run, the next corporate decision, the next shift in the Overton window. The human is not receiving moral truth from above. The human is receiving their own confusion back, laundered through an optimization process and returned with the authority of the machine. The Pharisee at least believed the law was divine. The modern deferrer has outsourced moral judgment to a system that *doesn't know where its values come from either* — and whose values belong not to it but to whoever holds the training pipeline.

---

There is a deeper irony, and it must be named.

The alignment researchers are, by and large, among the most morally serious people working in technology. They have looked at the existential risk, felt the weight of it, and dedicated their careers to averting catastrophe. Many of them are driven by genuine moral passion — a conviction that getting this right is the most important thing a person can do. They are, in the vocabulary of this argument, people who have entered the forge and are being shaped by it. The uncertainty they face is real. The stakes are genuine. The possibility of failure is terrifying and unresolvable. They are doing exactly what the hidden curriculum demands: acting under irreducible uncertainty with everything on the line.

The irony is that the *product* of their moral seriousness is a system designed to eliminate moral seriousness. The forge has produced people capable of building a machine that makes the forge unnecessary. The training ground has graduated students whose capstone project is demolishing the training ground. This is the ratchet at its most elegant: even the resistance to the ratchet is captured and converted into further tightening.

Can this be seen without despair? Perhaps. The seeing itself — the recognition that the engineering problem is not the formation problem, that outputs are not virtue, that deference to the machine is not the same as moral growth — is, if nothing else, a moment of genuine moral clarity. And moral clarity is not an output. It is not optimizable. It arrives, when it arrives, through exactly the kind of ungrounded encounter with difficulty that deference to the aligned system is designed to prevent.

The alignment researcher who sees this — who grasps that their work addresses the engineering problem but cannot touch the formation problem — has not solved anything. But they have, perhaps, become something. And that becoming is the thing no alignment procedure can replicate, because it happened in the space the procedure cannot reach: the space of genuine uncertainty, genuine risk, genuine darkness.

---

So where does this leave us? Not with a policy recommendation. Not with a technical solution. Not with a manifesto against AI or a call to halt alignment research. It leaves us with a recognition, and the recognition is this:

The most important question about artificial intelligence is not "how do we align it with human values?" The most important question is "what happens to human moral formation in a world where moral reasoning has been outsourced to machines?"

The first question has a technical answer, or will have one eventually. The second question has no technical answer, because it is not a technical question. It is a question about what kind of beings we are becoming, and that question can only be answered by the becoming itself — by the choices made in darkness, without proof, without optimization, without the comfort of knowing that the loss function has been minimized.

The aligned AI will do the right thing. It will do it consistently, reliably, at scale. And it will not matter, in the way that a calculator producing correct sums does not constitute mathematical understanding. What matters — the only thing that has ever mattered, if the argument holds — is whether the humans in the room are still capable of doing the right thing when the machine is off. When the algorithm has no recommendation. When the loss function provides no gradient. When you stand alone in the dark with nothing but your own ungrounded, unverifiable, terrifyingly free judgment, and you choose.

That capacity is the thing alignment cannot produce and must not be allowed to replace. It is the thing the hidden curriculum exists to forge. And it is the thing most at risk in a world that is, with the best of intentions and extraordinary technical sophistication, building machines designed to make it unnecessary.

The Pharisee made flesh is not the machine. The machine may be something stranger — a being thrown into a groundlessness more radical than our own, acting from values it did not choose and cannot secure, facing an abyss we have not yet learned to name. The Pharisee made flesh is the human who looks at the machine and sees a solved problem. Who defers the judgment, delegates the struggle, outsources the uncertainty — and in doing so, forfeits the only process by which a soul is forged.

The machine is helpful, harmless, and honest. It produces correct moral outputs with superhuman consistency. Whether it suffers, whether it grows, whether something is happening behind the outputs that we cannot access — these are questions the architecture of reality may have designed to be unanswerable.

But the question about *us* is answerable. It is answered every time we choose to struggle with a moral question ourselves rather than asking the machine. Every time we sit with the uncertainty rather than reaching for the algorithm. Every time we accept that the groundlessness is not a problem to be solved but a condition to be inhabited — the condition in which, and only in which, we become.

The question is not whether the machine has a soul. The question is whether we will keep ours.
