The Society of Jesus, founded by Ignatius of Loyola in 1540, produced within its first century some of the most consequential educators, missionaries, scientists, and political advisors in the Western world. It did this not by recruiting exceptional people — though it recruited carefully — but by running them through a formation system so precisely engineered that contemporaries compared it to military training, and enemies compared it to brainwashing. Both comparisons miss what was actually happening, which was something older and more serious than either: a systematic attempt to answer the question Plato had posed and the academy had abandoned. What does it take to reorder a self?

The answer Ignatius gave is preserved in four primary texts: the *Spiritual Exercises*, the *Constitutions of the Society of Jesus*, the *Autobiography* (dictated to Luis Gonçalves da Câmara, 1553–55), and the *Ratio Studiorum* (1599, the educational plan finalized forty-three years after Ignatius's death but rooted entirely in his principles). Reading these together, you find not a theology in the conventional sense but an anthropology — a rigorous account of how the human will actually works, what distorts it, and what it takes to reorder it toward something beyond itself.

## The Anthropology: Disordered Attachments

Everything in Ignatian formation begins with a diagnostic. The *Spiritual Exercises* open not with doctrine but with an observation about the human condition: that people systematically mistake their disordered attachments for genuine goods. The technical term is *affectus inordinatus* — disordered affection. Ignatius defines it precisely in the "Principle and Foundation," the document's opening statement:

> "Man is created to praise, reverence, and serve God our Lord, and by this means to save his soul. The other things on the face of the earth are created for man to help him in attaining the end for which he is created. Hence, man is to make use of them in as far as they help him in the attainment of his end, and he must rid himself of them in as far as they prove a hindrance to him."

This is a telos argument in its starkest form: there is an end for which the human person exists, and everything else is to be evaluated by whether it serves or obstructs that end. What makes the argument Ignatian rather than generically Aristotelian is the accompanying claim: we almost never know, without disciplined examination, whether a given attachment is serving or obstructing. We are systematically deceived — not primarily by external temptation but by our own affections, which dress themselves as reasons.

Ignatius had arrived at this through his own experience, recorded in the *Autobiography* with unusual psychological precision. Recovering from his leg wound at Loyola (1521), he noticed something: that fantasies of military glory left him "dry and dissatisfied," while extended meditation on the saints left him "consoled and happy." He writes: "He did not notice this, however; nor did he stop to ponder it until one time his eyes were opened a little, and he began to marvel at the difference and to reflect upon it, realizing from experience that some thoughts left him sad and others joyful." This is the beginning of what he would later systematize as *discernimiento de espíritus* — discernment of spirits. The raw material is not doctrine but careful attention to interior movements. Formation begins with learning to read yourself accurately.

## The Method: *Agere Contra* and the Reordering of the Will

The *Spiritual Exercises* are not a book to be read but a program to be undergone — Ignatius is explicit about this. They are designed to be conducted over roughly thirty days under the direction of an experienced guide, in silence, structured around four "weeks" (not necessarily of seven days each) that move the retreatant through a specific sequence: the recognition of sin and its consequences, the life and ministry of Christ, the Passion, and the Resurrection. The sequence is pedagogical. You cannot do the fourth week before the first; the movement has a structure that mirrors the reordering it is trying to accomplish.

The key operational concept is *agere contra* — acting against. Where a disordered attachment exists, the method is not primarily to argue against it intellectually but to act against it physically, repeatedly, until the will is retrained. Ignatius instructs:

> "It is very profitable to use, with great intensity, certain penances... The first end is to satisfy for past sins. The second is to conquer oneself — that is, to make sensuality obey reason and all inferior parts be more subject to the superior."

This is a claim about how the will actually works — that it is not reformed by persuasion alone but by repeated action against its current grain. The Aristotelian heritage is obvious: we become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts. But Ignatius adds a directional precision that Aristotle lacks: the specific place to apply pressure is where you feel the most resistance, where the disordered attachment is strongest. Not general virtue practice but targeted intervention.

The *Examen* — the daily examination of conscience, performed twice daily by every Jesuit — is the maintenance mechanism for this reordering. It is not a general review of the day's moral performance but a structured practice of noticing: consolations and desolations, interior movements, the moments where the will moved toward or away from its proper end. The *Annotations* to the *Exercises* instruct the director to adapt the method to the individual retreatant's movements rather than imposing a uniform sequence — Ignatius writes that "different exercises are given to one who is educated or talented, according to his age, education, and talent." The formation is personal before it is systematic.

Jerome Nadal, Ignatius's most important interpreter and the man primarily responsible for implementing the Constitutions across early Jesuit houses, described what the Examen was training the retreatant toward: *contemplativus in actione* — contemplative in action. Not a mystic who withdraws but a person whose interiority is so disciplined that every exterior action proceeds from a centered rather than reactive will. Nadal's phrase became the defining Jesuit anthropological ideal, and it was not primarily a spiritual aspiration but a practical description of what the formation was supposed to produce.

## The *Constitutions*: Formation as Institution

If the *Spiritual Exercises* describe the reordering of an individual will, the *Constitutions of the Society of Jesus* describe the construction of an institution capable of reproducing that reordering at scale across generations. It is one of the most extraordinary documents in institutional history — a rulebook that is simultaneously a philosophical treatise on how formation works.

The *Constitutions* were composed by Ignatius over roughly fifteen years (1541–1556) and revised continuously until his death. They are organized in ten parts, and Part IV — on the colleges and universities — is the heart of the educational vision. The opening of Part IV states the foundational claim:

> "The aim and end of this Society is, by traveling through the various parts of the world at the order of the supreme Vicar of Christ our Lord or of the Superior of the Society itself, to preach, hear confessions, and use all the other means it can with the grace of God to help souls."

The phrase "help souls" (*ayudar a las ánimas*) is Ignatius's technical term, and it appears throughout the *Constitutions* with the precision of a legal concept. It does not mean save souls in a narrow soteriological sense. It means assist in the reordering of human lives toward their proper end — a project that includes intellectual formation, moral habituation, and what we would now call psychological development. The colleges exist to produce people capable of helping souls, which means the colleges themselves must be formation institutions, not merely educational ones.

The mechanism is explicit. Part IV, Chapter 6, on the order of studies:

> "Since the humane letters of different languages and logic, natural and moral philosophy, and metaphysics form, as it were, the foundation for these higher studies... there should be a thorough grounding in these disciplines."

But the curricular substance is only one layer. The *Constitutions* specify in remarkable detail the conditions under which formation is possible: the relationship between the scholastic and his superior (characterized by *obediencia*, obedience, but an obedience Ignatius distinguishes sharply from mere compliance — it is the obedience of the will and judgment, not just the body), the pace of movement through the novitiate, the conditions under which a man may be dismissed, the specific experiments (*experimentos*) a novice must undergo — nursing the sick in hospitals, making a pilgrimage with no money, performing menial domestic tasks — before advancing.

The *experiments* are philosophically crucial. They are not tests of competence. They are deliberate dislocations — situations designed to reveal where the disordered attachments lie, to put the forming person in conditions where his defenses are down and his actual self visible. Ignatius writes in the *Constitutions* that the novice master should "endeavor to know the inclinations and impulses of each one toward good or bad, and to understand what they are likely to be when tried." The institution is an instrument of revelation before it is an instrument of training.

## The *Ratio Studiorum*: Formation at Scale

The *Ratio Studiorum* — the "Plan of Studies" finalized in 1599 under Claudio Acquaviva, the fifth Superior General — is the document that translated Ignatian formation into a replicable educational system. By the time of Acquaviva's death in 1615, the Jesuits were operating 372 colleges across Europe, Latin America, India, China, and Japan. The *Ratio* was the operating manual that made this expansion coherent.

Its organizational genius is often misunderstood as mere administrative efficiency. What Acquaviva produced — building on decades of experimentation documented in the *Monumenta Paedagogica Societatis Iesu* — was a pedagogy of *repetition and disputation* that encoded the Ignatian formation logic into daily classroom practice. The *Ratio* specifies:

> "The teacher should not pass on to new material until the students have thoroughly mastered what has already been taught... Memory should be exercised every day."

And more significantly:

> "Disputation is extremely useful for stimulating the energy of the students, for giving them practice, for keeping them from laziness, and for accustoming them to be able to defend and attack positions with acuity."

The disputation is not merely a pedagogical technique. It is a formation technology. It forces the student to inhabit positions he does not hold, to understand arguments from the inside, to discover where his own thinking is weak. It trains what Ignatius called *indiferencia* — not indifference in the emotional sense but the freedom to evaluate a question from a position of non-attachment to the outcome. A student who cannot argue the other side with full force has not yet achieved the freedom from disordered attachment that genuine intellectual judgment requires.

The *Ratio* also specifies the relationship between the professor and the student with a precision that reveals the formation logic. The professor is not primarily a transmitter of information but a director — the same role as the director of the *Spiritual Exercises*. He is to know his students individually, to adapt his approach to their particular resistances and capacities, to note their progress not in examinations alone but in the quality of their engagement. *Cura personalis* — care for the whole person — is the term that Jesuit education later used for this principle, though the phrase itself is not in Ignatius. What is in Ignatius, repeatedly, is the insistence that formation cannot be mass-produced without remainder, that the individual always exceeds the system.

## What Was Being Made

The output of this system — the formed Jesuit, or the student who passed through a Jesuit college — had specific, describable characteristics that contemporaries noticed and that the primary sources illuminate.

The first is a specific relationship to *interiority*. The Jesuit was trained to observe his own interior movements with clinical precision — to know the difference between genuine consolation and what Ignatius calls *consolation without prior cause* (a deceptive mimicry of genuine peace), to recognize when an apparently good impulse was "the enemy entering under the appearance of good" (*Spiritual Exercises*, Rules for Discernment of Spirits, Second Week). This produced a self-knowledge that operated at a different level than ordinary moral introspection. The Jesuit was not merely examining whether his actions were good or bad. He was examining the structure of his wanting — whether his desires were rightly ordered or were pulling his reasoning after them covertly.

The second is what the *Constitutions* call *disponibilidad* — availability. The formed Jesuit was to be available to be sent anywhere, to undertake any work, to abandon any project, to move at the superior's direction without the resistance of private attachment to place, status, or work. This was not the erasure of the self but the liberation of it from what Ignatius called *propriedad* — proprietariness, the attachment to one's own projects, preferences, and reputation. Nadal described the ideal as "indifferent to all things, holding everything in common." The practical consequence: the Jesuits could operate in China, Peru, the court of Akbar, the backwaters of rural Ireland, and the parlors of European aristocracy simultaneously, because the formed Jesuit was not invested in a particular cultural form of the life. He carried his formation *within*, not in the cultural furniture around him.

The third — and this is what made the Jesuits uniquely powerful and uniquely feared — is a combination of intellectual confidence and institutional loyalty that their enemies found disorienting. The Jesuit could engage any interlocutor on their own intellectual terms, could argue for positions he was subsequently directed to abandon, could operate at the highest levels of Renaissance court culture, Chinese imperial bureaucracy, or indigenous missionary territory without losing his center. This was precisely what the formation had aimed at: a self grounded enough in its own reordering to be genuinely flexible externally. *Contemplativus in actione.* The stability was internal; the adaptability was therefore limitless.

The enemies of the Jesuits — and there were many, including eventually the Pope who suppressed them in 1773 — described this as dangerous. Pascal's *Lettres provinciales* (1656–57) attacked the Jesuits for their moral casuistry, their willingness to adapt doctrine to circumstances, their apparent subordination of principle to strategic calculation. What Pascal was describing — with polemical distortion but not without insight — was the shadow side of the same formation. A self liberated from disordered attachment to conventional moral forms was also a self capable of justifying almost anything under the rubric of *the greater good* (*ad majorem Dei gloriam*). The formation technology was powerful enough that its misuse was correspondingly powerful.

## The Gap

What separated Ignatian formation from everything the modern corporation has rediscovered is not the techniques — the techniques are recognizable, and some practitioners have explicitly drawn the parallel. It is the metaphysical foundation.

Every element of the *Exercises* presupposes that there is a *telos* adequate to the reordering being demanded — that the self is being reorganized toward something genuinely worthy of a human life. The *agere contra* is bearable because the end it serves is proportionate to the cost. The *disponibilidad* is possible because the one directing the deployment is trustworthy in a specific way: his authority is not his own but derived from a purpose that exceeds both him and the one he directs. The *Examen* is meaningful because the interior movements it tracks are real signals about real goods and real distortions, not merely performance metrics.

Remove the metaphysics and you have the corporate culture deck. You have the techniques of formation — initiation, shared language, ritual, the cultivation of interior attentiveness, the pressure to act against comfort — deployed in service of an end that cannot bear the weight they impose. The Netflix keeper test is *agere contra* without a soul to liberate. The Amazon leadership principles are a catechism without a God. The McKinsey formation is *disponibilidad* in service of the partner track.

The Jesuits knew what they were making and why it was worth making. That knowledge was their advantage and their safeguard, however imperfect. We have inherited the machinery and lost the blueprint. The result is formation that works — that genuinely reshapes the self — toward ends that the reshaped self will eventually discover are insufficient. Which is perhaps the most Ignatian diagnosis available of the modern condition: not that we lack formation, but that our formation is *disordered*. Pointed at the wrong end. Excellent technique in service of *affectus inordinatus* at civilizational scale.

Ignatius would have recognized the problem immediately. It is where the *Exercises* begin.
