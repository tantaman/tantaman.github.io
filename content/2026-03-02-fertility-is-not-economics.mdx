# The Optimization Trap: Why the Birth Rate Can't Be Fixed

The standard explanations for collapsing fertility — childcare costs, housing prices, career opportunity costs — are real enough. But they share a peculiar structure: they all frame the child as a line item in a life-optimization problem. And they all propose the same kind of solution: adjust the costs. More subsidies, more leave, more flexibility. The question worth pressing is whether the framing *is* the problem, prior to any of the particular costs it identifies — and whether the self that shows up to perform this calculation is not the natural human baseline but the product of a specific formation that modernity has made invisible to itself.

Heidegger had a name for this condition: *Gestell*, technological enframing — the mode of life in which everything, including human beings, appears as raw material to be optimized. He called its opposite *Gelassenheit* — a receptive attentiveness that lets things arrive on their own terms rather than managing them into usefulness. This essay will use plain English from here: "the optimization framework" for the former, "reception" for the latter.

But Heidegger's insight goes deeper than the word "optimization" suggests, and it's worth stating the full claim at the outset. The danger isn't that modern people are optimizing toward the wrong end — pursuing status when they should pursue family, or wealth when they should pursue meaning. The danger is that optimization has become a mode of existence *without* an end. Status is pursued to access resources for further optimization. Technical progress enables more efficient optimization. Wealth is not a goal; it is the most legible measure of optimization as such. The framework has no *for*. It has only *more*. Every institution that once supplied a telos — a vision of what human life is for — has been systematically thinned, and what remains is a process that cannot be satisfied because it isn't oriented toward satisfaction. It is oriented toward continuation.

The child is the limit case. More than anything else in human experience, a child *arrives* — as a specific, unrepeatable someone who is not you and not your project. You can optimize the conditions around conception, pregnancy, education, enrichment. But the child as a person either shows up as an encounter with irreducible otherness or doesn't show up at all. And encounter with irreducible otherness is precisely what the optimization framework is designed to eliminate. Every optimization reduces the uncontrolled, the unpredicted, the other-than-planned. The child who arrives on their own terms is the thing the framework cannot metabolize. No wonder it has become the thing modern people find hardest to choose.

The thesis of this essay is that the birth rate decline is a demographic signal of a civilization that has lost the capacity to say what human life is *for* — and that this capacity cannot be recovered by adjusting costs within the framework that dissolved it. The data, it turns out, supports this claim more precisely than the philosophy alone ever could.

## The Data Doesn't Say What We Think It Says

Across OECD countries, the total fertility rate has halved from 3.3 children per woman in 1960 to 1.5 in 2022. South Korea sits at 0.7. Every OECD country except Israel is below replacement. The conventional story is that this tracks income and education: richer and more educated people have fewer children. And at the aggregate level, that's true.

But there's a wrinkle that the standard narrative obscures. Within education levels, increasing income is actually *positively* associated with fertility. People with the same education who earn more tend to have more children, not fewer. The aggregate negative correlation between income and fertility is driven not by wealth itself but by the expansion of higher education that accompanies rising incomes.

This matters enormously. It means the suppressive force isn't money — it's the *mode of life* that credentialing produces.

Years spent in higher education are years of intensive training in analysis, optimization, and management. The credential economy doesn't just delay childbearing; it reshapes how people relate to the future, to risk, to the uncontrollable. The opportunity-cost framework that economists use to explain the education-fertility link isn't a neutral description of a tradeoff — it's a symptom of the condition it describes. You can only experience a child as "opportunity cost" if you've already been formed to see your life as a resource to be allocated.

This is the core of the problem: the danger isn't any particular technology but the way technological thinking colonizes how we relate to everything, including people. Within the optimization framework, a child can only appear as a cost, a project, a risk to be managed. The child as someone who arrives on their own terms, who disrupts your plans precisely because they aren't one of your plans — that child cannot show up within a framework where everything is a plan.

## The Wealth Metric and Its Outputs

The optimization framework isn't abstract. It needs a metric, and in modern developed societies that metric is wealth — the universal solvent of value, the medium into which all other goods must be converted to become legible. In cultures with strong religious or communal frameworks, standing could be achieved through generosity, lineage, piety, or the number of your children. A person could matter through multiple, irreducible registers. But as those frameworks thinned, wealth became the default precisely because it is the most fungible, the most universally legible. Everyone can read a number.

Once wealth occupies this position, three things happen to fertility.

First, the middle class is paralyzed. Barbara Ehrenreich identified the psychology decades ago in *Fear of Falling*: the professional middle class is haunted not by actual deprivation but by the fear of downward mobility. Experimental research confirms the fertility connection directly — merely activating a desire for status leads people to prefer fewer children. And it's *relative* position, not absolute wealth, that drives the decision. Rising prosperity paradoxically suppresses fertility because as everyone's baseline expectations ratchet up, nobody feels secure enough. In a society where wealth is the sole metric of value, to fall economically is to become not just poor but *illegible* — erased from the only framework of meaning available. A medieval peasant who lost his land still had his parish, his saint's day, his place in a cosmic order. A downwardly mobile professional has nothing. The terror isn't poverty. It's disappearance.

Second, the wealthy don't escape — they complete the framework. The data shows a partial recovery of fertility at the top of the income distribution. But the honest read is not that the wealthy have rediscovered the child as gift. A large family becomes the most expensive luxury good, the ultimate signal that you've so thoroughly won the wealth game that you can absorb what would be a catastrophic cost for anyone else. Six kids in matching linen on a Tuscan hillside. The family is not received. It is *produced*.

Third, the suppression in the middle and the recovery at the top aren't two different phenomena. They're the same phenomenon — wealth as the sole metric of value — producing different outputs at different resource levels. Below a certain threshold, the optimization says "you can't afford this." Above it, the optimization says "you can afford to display this." The child never appears outside the cost-benefit analysis.

## Three Modes, Not Two

The argument so far risks collapsing into a simple binary: captured or not-yet-captured, modern or pre-modern. A more honest account requires three categories.

**The not-yet-captured.** Populations where the optimization framework hasn't fully colonized the value structure. This describes much of the developing world's fertility story, and it is genuinely transitional. As education and urbanization advance, fertility drops — not because women are being forced into smaller families but because preferences change. But the not-yet-captured aren't simply ignorant. Many high-fertility communities operate within frameworks where children genuinely are wealth, security, kinship obligation, participation in lineage. That's not the absence of a value system. It's the presence of a different one. Calling it "uneducated" just means "hasn't yet adopted wealth as the supreme metric," which is exactly the move this essay should question rather than repeat.

**The fully captured.** Most of the developed world, across all income levels. The middle class and the wealthy are both inside the optimization framework, producing different fertility outputs at different resource levels. The child never appears outside the cost-benefit analysis.

**The otherwise oriented.** This is the category that changes the argument. Within developed nations, certain communities sustain high fertility not because they are pre-modern holdouts but because they actively maintain an alternative framework from *within* modernity. The Amish, Orthodox Jews, traditional Catholics, Latter-day Saints, and other intentional communities have full access to contraception, full awareness of opportunity costs, full exposure to the culture of optimization. They are not pre-modern. They've encountered the optimization framework and they have something that answers it.

The trivially interesting question is why people who haven't encountered modernity still have children — any standard development narrative can explain that. The genuinely interesting question is why people who *have* encountered modernity still have children in large numbers. The answer can't be ignorance or inertia. It has to be something like a living alternative to the optimization framework — a way of life in which children are legible as something other than costs or status symbols.

The otherwise oriented are, as a demographic phenomenon, exclusively religious. The most prominent attempt at secular pronatalism — the Silicon Valley camp, the Natal Conference, the embryo-screening optimizers — is exactly what the thesis predicts: the framework perfected, applied to human beings at the point of conception. Across 181 countries, the most secular people have roughly the same small number of children regardless of where they live — below replacement. In the United States, the gap between religious and nonreligious fertility has widened to unprecedented levels. By 2019, the most religious Americans had fertility rates comparable to women in India or Mexico; the least religious had rates comparable to women in Japan or Italy.

There is an apparent counterexample: some of Europe's most secularized societies — France, the Netherlands, Sweden — have sustained higher fertility than more religious countries like Italy and Poland. But this is a policy effect, not a communal one. The Nordic welfare state adjusts the cost calculation of children within the optimization framework. It doesn't constitute a secular community sustaining high fertility through shared meaning. And none of these countries have reached replacement. The ceiling holds. No secular community has produced a self-sustaining, cross-generational culture of resistance to the wealth metric. Not because secular people lack depth or love, but because the communities that sustain alternative frameworks across generations require exactly what secularity, by definition, does not provide — a shared orientation toward something that transcends individual life and its optimization.

## What the Otherwise Oriented Actually Have

If the optimization framework is the problem, then every proposed policy solution is an attempt to change the cost calculation of children *within* the framework. The Nordic ceiling has already shown us the limit of that approach. But the argument can't run in reverse, either. You can't solve the crisis by *subtracting* modernity — rolling back education, restricting contraception, returning to agrarian life. You can't undo the framework. The pre-modern state is not recoverable, and attempting to recover it would be its own form of optimization: engineering social conditions to produce a desired fertility output. Optimization all the way down.

This is why the otherwise oriented communities matter. They suggest a third possibility: not a refusal of modernity or a retreat from it, but the maintenance of a way of life in which things — and persons — can appear as more than their calculable value. And they offer something more specific than an alternative metric for success. They offer a *telos* — a vision of what human life is for that doesn't refer beyond itself to some further optimization target. Torah study isn't optimized for anything beyond itself. The liturgical calendar doesn't make you more productive. The Amish barn raising isn't efficient. These practices are telically dense — they carry within them an account of human purpose that can't be converted to fuel for further optimization. The child, within these frameworks, isn't instrumentalized because nothing is instrumentalized. The whole form of life is oriented toward participation in something — God, community, creation — that isn't a means to anything else. But what, specifically, do they have? The conventional reading is that these communities sustain high fertility through some combination of restricted contraception, patriarchal authority, and limited exit options. The romantic reading is that they sustain it through a posture of *reception* — a shared orientation toward transcendence that lets the child appear as gift rather than cost.

The data supports neither reading cleanly. It supports something harder.

## The Coherence Thesis

A 2019 Institute for Family Studies survey across 11 countries found that women in highly religious, traditional couples report higher marital satisfaction and sexual satisfaction than women in secular or mixed-religiosity couples. These women also report that household decisions are shared rather than unilateral — the internal experience of these marriages is more egalitarian than outsiders assume. Meanwhile, Stevenson and Wolfers's landmark 2009 study documented that American women's happiness has declined both absolutely and relative to men since the 1970s, even as their objective circumstances have improved by virtually every measure — more education, more income, more legal rights, more reproductive control. The decline is pervasive across demographic groups and industrialized countries.

The naive reading of these two findings together is: traditional arrangements make women happier, modernity makes them miserable. But a 2025 paper in *Science Advances* complicates this by identifying a double paradox: women report higher life satisfaction and overall happiness than men, yet experience worse outcomes in mental health and negative affect. Women are simultaneously more satisfied and more depressed than men — and this holds across cultures. European Social Survey data reveals what the researchers describe as two stable configurations that both produce female life satisfaction: one built around high education, labor market participation, and gender balance in the household; another built around traditional caring roles, conservative values, and dense communal structure.

The women who report the *lowest* well-being aren't the ones in traditional communities or the ones in fully egalitarian Nordic arrangements. They're the ones in between — holding egalitarian values in societies that haven't delivered on them, navigating the optimization framework without the communal structures that would make either arrangement coherent.

This is the coherence thesis: the variable that predicts women's satisfaction isn't the *content* of the gender arrangement — traditional or egalitarian — but the *coherence* of the surrounding framework. A woman in a Latter-day Saint community with clearly defined roles, dense social support, and a husband whose status derives from fatherhood and service rather than market competition inhabits a coherent world. A woman in a fully egalitarian Nordic welfare state with robust public childcare and genuine workplace parity also inhabits a coherent world. Both report high satisfaction. Both have above-baseline fertility. What neither inhabits is the dissolved middle — the condition of most women in developed societies, where the old framework has been dismantled but no new communal structure has replaced it, where the individual is nominally free but actually alone with the optimization problem.

Coherence explains the satisfaction data. It doesn't explain the fertility data. For that, you need a deeper claim.

## The Category Error

Coherent egalitarian communities — active Nordic churchgoers, moderate LDS families — produce fertility *above* their secular neighbors. That's the coherence effect. But they don't produce fertility dramatically above replacement. The communities that do — Amish at 6.0, Haredi at 6.6 — maintain something more than coherence. They maintain binding communal norms dense enough to override individual cost-benefit calculations about reproduction. And those norms have, so far, included traditional gender arrangements.

The standard way to describe this pattern is: *formation* shifts fertility upward, *constraint* sustains it at dramatically high levels. Formation gives people the interior resources to experience children as something other than costs. Constraint provides the external structure that holds even when formation alone would be insufficient. That framing has the virtue of clarity. It also smuggles in the very ontology the essay is trying to challenge.

To say that formation is one thing and constraint is another — that formation is the good part and constraint is the regrettable but perhaps necessary supplement — is to assume that the individual exists prior to the community. That there is a self which is first *formed* by communal practices (the nice part) and then *constrained* by communal norms (the uncomfortable part). That the formation happens *to* a self that was already there to receive it.

This is the liberal ontological assumption, and it is a category error. The self is not prior to the community. The self is constituted through it. This is not a new observation — it is the central claim of every communitarian critique of liberalism from MacIntyre to the present, and it has roots far older than that. But the fertility data gives it an empirical edge that the philosophical argument alone has never had.

A person does not arrive in the world with a fully formed interior and then submit to social arrangements by choice. The interior is built — by practices, relationships, rhythms, constraints, and encounters that the person did not select. The community isn't limiting a pre-existing person. It's participating in the production of the person. The Amish woman who experiences her seventh child as gift isn't doing so *despite* her formation. She's doing so *through* it. The capacity to receive is itself a product of having been shaped by something outside her own preference.

This is why the formation/constraint distinction dissolves under pressure. What looks like constraint from outside the framework — from the vantage of the optimization-formed individual who assumes autonomy as the baseline — is, from inside, the material through which the self was built. The liturgical calendar that "interrupts" productivity isn't constraining a self that would otherwise be optimizing. It's forming a self for whom productivity is not the supreme value. The communal expectation of large families isn't overriding a preference for fewer children. It's participating in the formation of a person for whom children are legible as participants in a shared world rather than as costs in a private optimization problem.

Eckhart understood this. His *Gelassenheit* — the releasement or letting-be that this essay has been calling *reception* — is not a lifestyle choice available to any individual at any time. It is a spiritual capacity produced by years of discipline: liturgical practice, obedience, submission to a rule of life. You become capable of receiving by being formed to receive. And formation necessarily involves encounter with what resists your preference — with what is not reducible to your will. A person whose every environment has been curated to match their preferences — the perfected consumer, the completed optimizer — has no interior surface for anything to land on. Everything slides off. Including a child.

This reframes the satisfaction data entirely. The question isn't why women in "constrained" communities report high satisfaction — as though satisfaction were surprising under constraint and needed explaining away (she doesn't know any better) or explaining up (she's achieved spiritual receptivity despite her circumstances). The formation *is* the receptivity. The communal structure that looks like constraint from outside is, from inside, the thing that built the self capable of experiencing children, marriage, and embodied life as encounter rather than management. The satisfaction is real because the formation is real. It isn't produced despite the constraint. It is produced through it.

## Testing the Thesis

If this account is right — if the capacity for reception is built by specific communal formations and not by formation in general — then it predicts something specific about the fertility gradient. Communities with robust spiritual formation but without the particular practices that form people for receptivity to children will produce devout members with low fertility. Formation is not generic. You are formed *for* specific modes of encounter by *specific* practices.

The Sikh case tests this precisely. Sikhism has robust liturgical life, strong communal identity, a transcendent orientation as deep as any in the world's religious traditions. Guru Nanak proclaimed the equality of men and women in the fifteenth century. Sikh women can lead congregations, participate in all religious rites, hold institutional authority. The community is coherent. The formation is real. But Sikh egalitarianism has left family size to individual negotiation. It has not formed its members, through specific communal practices, to encounter reproduction as something that arrives on its own terms. Worldwide Sikh fertility sits at 1.6, indistinguishable from the secular baseline.

The LDS trajectory tells the same story in slow motion. When researchers ask LDS couples who *should* perform various family functions, they score high on traditional beliefs. When asked what they actually *do*, they consistently emerge as egalitarian in practice. They have higher education and income than Protestants and Catholics. Among LDS families, income is *positively* correlated with fertility — the opposite of the general population pattern. They've reversed the income-fertility relationship, which is exactly the signature of a community operating outside the wealth metric. And yet Utah now averages 1.8 children per woman, down from three in the 1980s. The communal identity is real. The transcendent orientation is real. The alternative status registers are real. But the specific formational practices around family — the density of communal expectation, the friction that builds the interior for reception — are thinning. And fertility is converging.

The Finnish Laestadians complete the pattern. A conservative Lutheran revival movement inside the most gender-egalitarian welfare state on earth, they traditionally had families of ten or twelve. Recently that has declined to around five, as younger members increasingly treat contraception as a private matter rather than a communal practice. The key word is *private*. The moment reproduction becomes a matter of individual decision rather than communal formation, the optimization framework has already won, no matter how sincere the faith.

## Where This Leaves Us

The pattern is consistent and uncomfortable.

Communities that sustain dramatically above-replacement fertility do so not through policies or incentives but through formational density — communal structures thick enough to build selves capable of receiving children as participants in a shared world rather than managing them as elements of a private plan. That density involves what modernity calls constraint: binding norms, communal expectations, friction with individual preference, limits on the competitive ratchet that drives optimization. These are not additions to formation. They *are* formation. They are the material through which the community participates in the being of its members.

Communities that combine genuine spiritual formation with genuine individual autonomy — the LDS, the Sikhs, active Nordic churchgoers — sustain a fertility *advantage* over their secular neighbors. Coherence helps. But the advantage erodes over time and has never, in any documented case, sustained above-replacement fertility indefinitely against the pressure of the surrounding optimization culture.

One objection demands an honest answer. "The community participates in the being of its members" is also what every abusive institution says about itself. What distinguishes formation from coercion? The satisfaction data helps but doesn't resolve this — a person can be formed to report satisfaction with their own domination. The strongest answer available is the conjunction of satisfaction with genuine exit. Amish retention rates run above 85% even after Rumspringa provides real exposure to the outside world. Among communities with real exit options, people are staying — not because they can't imagine the alternative but because they can, and they prefer the coherence of a formed life to the dissolution of an optimized one. This is not proof that every practice within these communities is just. It is evidence that the formational structure as a whole produces something that its members, upon encountering the alternative, recognize as more than what the alternative offers.

The essay's deepest claim, stated plainly: the liberal individual — the unconstrained chooser, the autonomous agent selecting from an open menu of life options — is not the baseline human condition. It is a specific product of a specific formation, or rather a specific *de*-formation: the systematic dismantling of the communal structures that once built selves capable of reception. The fertility crisis is not a collection of individual choices that happen to aggregate badly. It is what happens when the formational structures that produce selves capable of encountering children as more than costs are dissolved by a framework that can only see those structures as constraints on a freedom it has defined in advance.

The attempt to instrumentalize any of this — to adopt constraint *because it produces babies*, or to adopt faith *because it sustains fertility* — is the most sophisticated form of optimization imaginable. You cannot engineer reception. You cannot optimize your way to a telos. The communities that have what modernity lacks did not acquire it by identifying a demographic problem and engineering a solution. They inherited, maintained, and were formed by practices that carry within them an answer to the question modernity can no longer ask: what is human life *for*?

The birth rate decline is the receipt for having dissolved every institution that once supplied an answer. The receipt cannot tell you how to get them back.
