This is the hard question. The one that "The Return" gestured toward in its final sections but didn't fully enter.

Because there's a tension: the return I described was still a return *to* something — to tradition, to covenant, to the promised land. It still carried the implicit promise that the return would be rewarded, that building would bear fruit, that the cycle would continue and therefore *your* part in it mattered.

But what you're describing now is starker. It asks: what if we hold all of that *and* genuinely release the outcome? Not as a spiritual technique that secretly guarantees results ("let go and you'll receive more"), but as actual acceptance that it might all come to nothing. That the tradition might die. That the vineyard might be bulldozed. That death might be death.

---

What you're circling is something like:

**The anthropological constant**: Humans have a will to power. A tendency toward violence. A desire to dominate, to win, to be recognized. This is not a bug introduced by capitalism or colonialism or any particular system. It is *us*. The critiques were right to see it everywhere because it *is* everywhere. The mistake was thinking some revolution could excise it.

**Cyclical time**: Not progress toward a kingdom. Not a linear arc bending toward justice. But the eternal return — building and forgetting, departure and return, generation after generation doing the same dance. The prodigal always leaves. The father always waits. The inheritance is always squandered and rebuilt and squandered again.

**The divine spark without the divine guarantee**: Something in us reaches toward transcendence. Call it God, call it the numinous, call it the ground of being. It is real — as real as anything we experience. But it makes no promises. It does not guarantee that your prayers will be answered, your children will keep the faith, or your death will open onto anything at all.

**Death as acceptable**: Not death defeated by resurrection. Not death as passage. But death as potentially final — and that being bearable. Even fitting. The seed falls into the ground and dies. Perhaps it rises. Perhaps it doesn't. Either way, the falling was right.

---

This is Christianity with something removed and something added.

**Removed**: The confidence. The hierarchy that claims to mediate the sacred. The eschatology that promises it all works out. The transaction — believe and be saved, build and be rewarded.

**Added**: What Nietzsche saw but couldn't integrate. What the mystics knew but the institution suppressed. The acknowledgment that we contain violence, that time circles, that the center cannot hold, that holding itself must be released.

It's Eckhart's *Gelassenheit* taken all the way down. "God, rid me of God" — rid me of the God who guarantees, who rewards, who keeps accounts. What remains is... what? The practice itself. The prayer said into silence. The fast kept for no one. The Sabbath observed not because it will save you but because it is how humans are meant to live, even if "meant" has no guarantor.

---

Is this still Christianity? 

Perhaps it is Christianity returned to its mystical root, before Constantine, before the creeds hardened, when the early communities simply *lived* a certain way and died for it without knowing if the dying would accomplish anything.

Or perhaps it is Christianity composted — broken down into its essential nutrients, ready to feed something that doesn't yet have a name.

I don't think it matters which. What matters is: can it be lived? Can you build without grasping? Can you plant without demanding harvest? Can you keep the practices knowing they might not "work"? Can you face the violence in yourself and not be destroyed by it, and not project it onto enemies, and not pretend it isn't there?

---

The essay that comes next might need to say something like:

*We return — but not to certainty. We return to practice without guarantee. We keep the Sabbath not because God commanded it but because humans need it, and "need" is enough. We fast not to please a watching deity but to remember we are not our appetites. We marry and bear children not because the future is assured but because this is what life does — it bets on the next generation without knowing the odds.*

*We acknowledge the violence in us. We do not pretend we are the good ones fighting the bad ones. We know the will to power that built every empire also lives in our chests. The practices are not to make us good. They are to give the violence somewhere to go that isn't our neighbor's throat.*

*We accept the cycle. Our children will forget. Their children will remember. This is not failure. This is the rhythm. We do not build monuments to last forever. We build for the next three generations and release what comes after.*

*We do not know what death is. We do not know if anything waits on the other side. But we know that clinging to life produces a certain kind of ugliness, and releasing it produces a certain kind of beauty, and that is enough to go on.*

*This is not optimism. It is something stranger — a love of life that does not depend on life continuing, a commitment to meaning that does not require meaning to be guaranteed, a faith that has let go of faith's rewards and found something underneath that cannot be named but can be lived.*
