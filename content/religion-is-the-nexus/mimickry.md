# The Machinery Beneath

## On Mimicry, Hierarchy, and the Question of Exit

---

### I. The Ground Before the Game

Before there were ideas, there was dirt.

Geography shapes what's possible. Rivers allow agriculture. Agriculture allows surplus. Surplus allows cities. Cities allow specialization. Specialization allows priests, scribes, philosophers — people with time to think and talk.

The Fertile Crescent wasn't destined to produce Western civilization, but it created conditions where *something* complex could emerge. The Andes produced different conditions, different possibilities. The Arctic, different still.

This is obvious but easy to forget. We spend most of our time in the world of ideas — arguing about values, debating ideologies, choosing between lifestyles. We forget that all of this floats on a material base. The conversation happens in certain places, among people with certain resources, because geography and history made it possible.

The ground doesn't determine what grows. But it constrains what *can* grow.

*See also: [Why Cultures Differentiate](https://tantaman.substack.com/p/why-cultures-differentiate)*

### II. The Mimicry Engine

Into any culture, children are born. They arrive with some innate temperament — more curious or more cautious, more active or more contemplative. But they also arrive with universal machinery.

Humans are mimics.

This isn't a cultural artifact or a learned behavior. It's built in. Infants imitate facial expressions within hours of birth. Children absorb language, accent, gesture, posture — not through instruction but through copying. We are, as René Girard observed, creatures of mimetic desire. We don't just imitate actions; we imitate *wanting*.

And we don't imitate randomly. We imitate *up*.

Status-seeking is the other piece of innate machinery. We are exquisitely sensitive to hierarchy — who's above, who's below, who's rising, who's falling. And we copy those above us. The junior employee absorbs the vocabulary of the senior. The teenager adopts the style of the cool kids. The intellectual cites the prestigious names.

This isn't shallow or optional. It's how culture transmits. Language, values, worldview, taste — all of it flows through mimicry of high-status models. You don't choose your deepest assumptions. You catch them, from the people you're trying to become.

### III. The Inevitability of Hierarchy

Some hope that hierarchy is a contingent social arrangement — that with the right reforms, the right consciousness, we could achieve equality. Flatten the structure. Even the field.

The math suggests otherwise.

In any network, some nodes will have more connections than others. This isn't a design choice; it's a structural property. Scale-free networks — which describe everything from the internet to social systems to neural connections — follow power laws. A few nodes dominate. Most are peripheral.

Even wealth distribution, started from perfectly equal conditions with random transactions, will concentrate over time. Run the simulation: give everyone the same amount, let them trade randomly, and within a few iterations, inequality emerges and compounds. Pareto dynamics aren't imposed; they're emergent.

Life itself exists in gradients. Thermodynamically, a living system is a region of local order maintained against entropy by energy flow. No gradient, no life. Perfect equilibrium is death.

This doesn't justify any particular hierarchy. It doesn't mean the current arrangement is good or inevitable in its specifics. But it means *some* hierarchy is inevitable in any complex system. The question isn't whether hierarchy exists. The question is which hierarchy, serving what ends, and what to do about it.

*See also: [The Amplifier Theory of Human Hierarchy](https://tantaman.substack.com/p/the-amplifier-theory-of-human-hierarchy), [The New Lords](https://tantaman.substack.com/p/the-new-lords)*

### IV. The Will to Power

If hierarchy is inevitable, then position in hierarchy matters. Those at the top want to stay there. Those at the bottom want to rise. This wanting has a name: the will to power.

Nietzsche saw this as the basic drive beneath all human action — not pleasure, not survival, but the expansion of one's influence and capability. Even apparent selflessness often serves the will to power: the philanthropist who shapes society in his image, the martyr whose death inspires movements.

Most people, most of the time, are running this program. Competing for status, building alliances, seeking advantage. The specific *form* varies by culture — money here, honor there, followers somewhere else — but the underlying drive is constant.

The spiritual traditions recognize this. Every serious path begins with the problem of ego, desire, attachment to outcome. The Buddhist sees craving as the root of suffering. The Christian sees pride as the first sin. The Stoic seeks indifference to external goods.

These traditions offer an exit from the will to power. But the exit is narrow, difficult, and constantly counterfeited. More on that later.

### V. The Rotating Content

Here's where "grammar" and "ideology" and "culture" come in.

At any given time, people are speaking particular languages of value. In one era, it's honor and shame. In another, sin and salvation. In another, growth and self-actualization. In another, oppression and liberation.

These vocabularies feel important from inside. People fight over them, die for them, build civilizations around them. And they're not nothing — they contain genuine insights, genuine truths, genuine wisdom.

But they rotate.

The specific content changes across centuries and societies. What stays constant is the machinery underneath: mimicry, status-seeking, hierarchy, will to power. The content is the clothes; the machinery is the body.

This explains the co-optation pattern. Something genuine emerges — a spiritual insight, a philosophical breakthrough, a social innovation. It starts pure, or pure enough. Then elites (or counter-elites) capture it. The insight becomes an ideology. The liberation becomes a new hierarchy. The spiritual practice becomes a status marker.

Christianity began as a radical revaluation — the last shall be first, blessed are the meek. Within centuries, it was the official religion of empire. Therapy began as a healing practice. It became a management tool, an HR vocabulary, a way to pathologize dissent. Every genuine thing gets captured, because the machinery never stops running.

### VI. We Shape, Then Are Shaped

Marshall McLuhan: "We shape our tools, and thereafter our tools shape us."

The same applies to institutions, ideologies, grammars. We create them to serve purposes. Then they take on a life of their own. Then they shape us more than we shape them.

A company is founded with a mission. The mission requires organization. The organization develops procedures. The procedures become culture. The culture selects for certain people and drives out others. Eventually, the company serves the perpetuation of the company. The original mission becomes a story told at orientation.

A revolution overthrows the old order in the name of the people. The revolution requires organization. The organization requires leadership. The leadership becomes a new elite. The new elite defends its position. Within a generation, the revolutionary grammar serves the same function as the old regime: legitimating hierarchy.

This isn't cynicism. It's mechanics. The tools we make to serve us accumulate their own momentum. We think we're using the grammar; the grammar is using us.

### VII. The Pinch of Incense

Here's where it gets uncomfortable.

In the Roman Empire, Christians faced a test. To participate in civic life, you had to offer a pinch of incense to the emperor's image. Just a gesture. Just a formality. You could do it while internally maintaining your Christian faith. Couldn't you?

Some Christians did. They performed the external act while preserving, they believed, their internal conviction.

But it didn't work. The external act corroded the internal state. The body taught the mind. The performance became belief. Give the pinch of incense long enough, and you stop being Christian in any meaningful sense.

This is the point the essay on grammars underweighted. It treated grammars as conceptual frameworks, as vocabularies, as ways of thinking. But we don't just think our grammars — we *perform* them. We enact them with our bodies, our habits, our daily practices.

And the performance shapes the performer.

You can tell yourself you're speaking corporate vocabulary ironically, that your *real* values are elsewhere. But show up every day, use the language, chase the metrics, and the irony dissolves. You become what you do. The grammar installs itself through practice, not just through thought.

This is why exit is so hard. You can't just think differently. You have to *live* differently. And living differently, really differently, is expensive.

### VIII. The Question of Exit

Given all this — the innate machinery, the inevitable hierarchy, the rotating content, the co-optation, the way practice shapes belief — is exit possible?

Several strategies have been tried.

**Parallel structures.** Augustine's *City of God*. You live in the earthly city — Rome, or whatever comes after — but your true citizenship is elsewhere. You participate minimally in the surrounding society while building alternative communities, alternative practices, alternative formation. The church within the empire. The monastery. The intentional community.

This can work, for a while. But parallel structures face constant pressure. They either stay small and marginal or grow and get captured. The monastery becomes a landlord. The intentional community becomes a brand.

**Seclusion.** The desert fathers. The hermit. Complete withdrawal from the social game. If the machinery runs on mimicry and status, remove yourself from all status hierarchies and all models to mimic.

This is coherent but extreme. Few can sustain it. And even seclusion has its hierarchies — spiritual achievement becomes the new status marker. The most secluded hermit gains reputation for his seclusion.

**Internal maintenance.** The most common strategy. Stay in the world, play the game, but maintain an inner citadel. Perform the grammar externally while preserving different values internally.

This is the strategy the pinch of incense refutes. The inner citadel isn't as fortified as it feels. The external performance leaks inward. Over years, decades, you become what you do. The maintenance fails.

**Transformation.** The rarest and most radical possibility. Not parallel structures, not seclusion, not mere internal maintenance — but genuine change in the machinery itself. Reduction of mimicry, reduction of status-seeking, reduction of will to power. Not suppression but transformation.

This is what the serious spiritual traditions aim at. The Buddhist path isn't about thinking different thoughts; it's about restructuring perception and desire. The Christian saints aren't just people with different opinions; they're people who've undergone some fundamental change in their operating system.

Is this real? Or is it just another status game — spiritual achievement as the ultimate flex, humility as the highest pride?

### IX. The Honest Position

Here's what I think can be honestly said.

The machinery is real. Mimicry, status-seeking, hierarchy, will to power — these aren't cultural constructs that could be deconstructed. They're built into us and into any complex social system.

The rotating content is real. Grammars, ideologies, cultures — these change across time and place. They contain genuine insight and genuine error. They get co-opted by power struggles. They shape us more than we shape them.

The co-optation is real. Whatever is genuine gets captured. Whatever is liberating becomes a new constraint. The machinery repurposes everything.

The physical shapes the internal. Practice precedes belief. You become what you do. The pinch of incense corrodes.

Given all this, the spiritual traditions are either:

1. The most sophisticated cope — a grammar for losers in the status game to claim they've transcended it while still playing it
2. A genuine exit — a real possibility of transformation that breaks the machinery

I can't prove which it is. The counterfeits are everywhere. Spiritual status-seeking is real — the guru with followers, the meditator with achievements, the saint with reputation. The machinery is very good at capturing even the attempts to escape it.

But the reports from the serious practitioners are consistent across traditions. Something happens. Something shifts. The grip of mimicry and status loosens. Not perfectly, not permanently, but really. The saints aren't just playing a different game; they're playing less.

Whether you believe this is possible depends on evidence you probably haven't gathered. It's not an argument to be won; it's an experiment to be run.

### X. What Remains

If the machinery is real and exit is difficult, what's left?

**See the machinery.** This has value even if it doesn't free you. Watch the mimicry happen. Watch the status-seeking activate. Watch the grammar switch. Watch the will to power dress itself in whatever vocabulary is available. Seeing doesn't stop the mechanism, but it creates a small gap — a moment of non-identification.

**Choose your hierarchies.** If hierarchy is inevitable, the choice isn't whether to participate but which hierarchies to invest in. Some are more conducive to human flourishing than others. Some have better feedback loops. Some are easier to exit. You can't escape hierarchy, but you can be thoughtful about which ones you feed.

**Protect the genuine.** Some things are true. Some insights are real. Some practices do help. The fact that they'll be co-opted doesn't mean they're worthless — it means they need protection. Small communities. Careful transmission. Resistance to scaling. The genuine stays genuine longest when it stays small.

**Take the physical seriously.** You become what you do. If you want to think differently, you need to live differently. The daily practices matter more than the beliefs you profess. The incense you offer shapes the god you worship.

**Stay honest about the limits.** You're probably not going to achieve escape velocity. The machinery is strong and you're not that special. But partial freedom is still freedom. Seeing the game while playing it is better than playing blind. And occasionally, rarely, someone gets out. The possibility exists even if you're not the one who realizes it.

---

### XI. Conclusion

The ground came first. Geography shaped possibility. From possibility, cultures emerged. Into cultures, children are born — mimicking, status-seeking, absorbing the grammar of whoever's above them.

The content rotates. The machinery stays constant. Genuine insight emerges and gets captured. We shape our tools and our tools shape us. The physical act trains the internal state.

Exit is possible but rare. The spiritual traditions point at something real. Most who try will fail — will substitute spiritual status for material status, will give the pinch of incense while believing they haven't, will become what they perform.

And yet.

The reports are consistent. Something loosens. Something shifts. Not for everyone, not easily, not completely. But really.

The question isn't whether the machinery exists. It does.

The question is what you do, knowing that it does.
