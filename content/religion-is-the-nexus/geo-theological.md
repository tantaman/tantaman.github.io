# Why the World Keeps Re-Polarizing

---

*Material constraints generate institutions; institutions stabilize into sacred stories; sacred stories shape cognition and motivation; motivation shapes power and economic order; economic order generates grievances; grievances re-write the sacred stories—and the cycle tightens.*

---

# Introduction: The Puzzle of Perpetual Polarization

Why does the world keep splitting apart? The question feels newly urgent, but it is ancient. Trade, communication, and scientific coordination have never been more globally integrated—yet nationalist revivals, great-power rivalries, and identity-based conflicts seem equally inexorable. The paradox troubles economists, who expected interdependence to breed peace; it troubles liberals, who expected education and cosmopolitanism to dissolve tribalism; it troubles realists, who thought the Cold War's end would yield a stable hegemonic order.

This essay proposes that the paradox dissolves once we recognize the underlying mechanism: a *geo-theological feedback engine* that has been running for millennia. The engine is not a conspiracy, nor a law of nature, nor a counsel of despair. It is a pattern—a recurring dynamic by which geography shapes institutions, institutions crystallize into sacred narratives, narratives mobilize populations, mobilized populations construct economic orders, economic orders distribute gains and losses unequally, grievances from those losses rewrite the sacred narratives, and the cycle begins again. Understanding the engine does not mean submitting to it. But pretending it does not exist guarantees that we will be surprised when it spins up once more.

# Part One: The Engine Described

## The Basic Loop

Think of any large-scale human society as running a kind of recurrent update—generation after generation, crisis after crisis—through several interlocking phases:

### **Phase 1: Geography sets constraints.** 
Arable land, navigable rivers, mountain barriers, defensible coastlines, and access to chokepoints determine what kinds of coordination problems a society must solve. As Halford Mackinder observed in his foundational geopolitical analysis, the physical configuration of landmass and water shapes the possibilities for empire and resistance. Mackinder saw Eurasia's vast interior as a "pivot area" whose control would dictate global power. He noted that the history of European civilization emerged through perpetual struggle against pressure from the Asian steppes, describing how "a series of horse-riding peoples emerged from Asia through the broad interval between the Ural mountains and the Caspian sea, rode through the open spaces of southern Russia, and struck home into Hungary." Geography was not mere backdrop; it was the engine's first gear.

### **Phase 2: Constraints force coordination problems.** 
Irrigation in arid climates requires centralized administration; seaborne trade requires port cities, naval defense, and merchant law; steppe frontiers require mobile cavalry or walled fortifications. The coordination problems differ radically: a river valley demands bureaucrats, a maritime city-state demands traders and shipwrights, a pastoral frontier demands warriors. The Chinese Yellow River, the Venetian lagoon, the Greek archipelago, the Arabian desert—each imposed distinct organizational imperatives.

### **Phase 3: Coordination hardens into institutions.** 
Solutions to coordination problems become routinized: bureaucracies, priesthoods, standing armies, legal codes, tribute systems. These institutions acquire interests of their own; they develop internal selection criteria for personnel; they codify rules that outlast any single ruler. States compete, and competition selects for states capable of extraction, coordination, and legitimation. As Charles Tilly argued in his analysis of European state formation, war-making and state-making were essentially protection rackets that evolved into recognizable governmental forms. The lords who provided security from external threats—or from themselves—gradually accumulated the apparatus we now call sovereignty.

**Phase 4: Institutions generate a moral-cognitive operating system.** Institutions do not survive on coercion alone. They need stories—stories that explain why the priests deserve tithes, why the king deserves obedience, why the merchant's profit is not mere theft, why the warrior's violence is not mere murder. These stories become sacred: embedded in ritual, transmitted through education, enforced through shame and honor. They constitute what we might call a society's *operating system*—not mere beliefs, but a grammar for reasoning about duty, purity, hierarchy, time, and the good life.

The Confucian tradition exemplifies this phase. The Analects systematically encode governance as moral cultivation. Confucius taught that good rule flows from personal virtue, transmitted through ritual and study. He insisted that filial piety formed the root of all moral character, declaring that filial devotion and obedience provide the foundation from which the Way itself will grow. This was not merely ethics; it was statecraft rendered as metaphysics. The ruler who cultivated virtue would find that the people naturally inclined toward virtue themselves, just as the Pole Star commands the homage of the multitude of stars without leaving its place.

**Phase 5: The operating system determines what narratives can mobilize.** Not every story can move a population to sacrifice. The operating system constrains which appeals will resonate: honor, covenant, harmony, progress, purification, manifest destiny, victimhood vindicated. Elites can attempt to manipulate these narratives, but they cannot invent them from scratch; they must work within the grammar their society has inherited. Benedict Anderson's insight into "imagined communities" captures how print capitalism enabled national consciousness—but the content of that consciousness depended on pre-existing sacred grammars.

**Phase 6: Mobilized societies choose economic forms that fit the operating system.** Here Max Weber's thesis becomes crucial. Weber did not argue crudely that Protestantism "caused" capitalism. Rather, he showed that certain religious ethics made certain economic behaviors morally intelligible and personally sustainable. The Calvinist doctrine of calling transformed worldly labor into a form of worship. The peculiar Puritan asceticism turned the reinvestment of profit from greed into duty. As Weber observed, this religious epoch bequeathed to its successors an amazingly good conscience in the acquisition of money, so long as it took place legally. The religious basis eventually died away, but the economic form it legitimated persisted. Modern workers and entrepreneurs no longer need Calvinist theology to show up for work; the system selects for them regardless.

But Weber also understood that different operating systems would generate different economic rationalities. His comparative studies of Confucianism and Taoism, of ancient Judaism, of Hinduism and Buddhism, showed that rationalization proceeds along different axes depending on the foundational metaphysics. The Confucian literati had no doctrine of calling; their path to status ran through bureaucratic examination, not entrepreneurial accumulation. The economic form reflected the moral grammar.

**Phase 7: Economic forms create distributional tensions.** Any economic order produces winners and losers. Market expansion enriches some regions and devastates others; technological change rewards some skills and renders others obsolete; financial crises wipe out savings while protecting debtors or creditors depending on policy choices. These distributional outcomes generate grievances—grievances that demand explanation and redress.

Karl Polanyi's "double movement" describes the resulting dynamic: the push for market expansion triggers a protective counter-movement from those who experience the expansion as dispossession. Marx captured the phenomenon in more confrontational terms when he described religion itself as an expression of real suffering and a protest against real suffering—a sigh of the oppressed creature in a heartless world. The economic order produces suffering; the sacred operating system must either explain that suffering or be revised to protest it.

**Phase 8: Grievance updates the operating system.** Here the cycle closes and begins again. Grievances do not simply dissipate; they crystallize into new heresies, reformations, revolutionary ideologies, and identity movements. The operating system mutates. Sometimes the mutation is conservative—a "return" to an imagined purer past. Sometimes it is revolutionary—a leap toward an imagined redeemed future. But the mutation always draws on the existing grammar even as it transforms it.

Reinhart Koselleck's studies of historical time illuminate this process. Koselleck showed that the modern concept of progress—history as a meaningful arc toward improvement—represents a secularized form of Christian eschatology. Medieval Europeans inherited from Augustine a sense that history moves toward a definite end; Enlightenment thinkers kept the directional structure while replacing salvation with "progress." The present becomes a "former future" (vergangene Zukunft)—a moment that was once anticipated and now demands reinterpretation. Koselleck demonstrated how our concepts of historical time themselves have a history, shaped by the crises that forced societies to reconceive their temporal orientation.

# Part Two: The Intellectual Lineage

The engine I am describing is not a novelty. It synthesizes several major traditions of thought that developed across centuries. Understanding where these insights came from helps us see why the synthesis is compelling.

## Geography as Constraint

The recognition that physical environment shapes political possibility runs from Montesquieu through Mackinder to contemporary realists. Montesquieu argued that laws and social temperaments correlate with climate and terrain; he saw the vastness of Asia as favoring despotism and the fragmented geography of Europe as encouraging liberty. Mackinder formalized this intuition into geopolitics proper, arguing that control of the Eurasian "Heartland" would dominate world politics. He warned that "every explosion of social forces, instead of being dissipated in a surrounding circuit of unknown space and barbaric chaos, will be sharply re-echoed from the far side of the globe."

Fernand Braudel extended the geographical sensibility into historical method. His concept of the *longue durée*—the long duration—distinguished slow-moving structural constraints (geography, climate, demographics) from medium-term conjunctural patterns (economic cycles, state formations) and short-term events (battles, treaties, assassinations). For Braudel, the deepest historical forces operate below the threshold of conscious decision; geography and ecology provide the stage on which dramas unfold, limiting the range of possible performances.

John Mearsheimer represents the contemporary realist synthesis. His "offensive realism" holds that great powers inherently seek to maximize their share of world power because the international system offers no reliable guarantor of security. The American circumstance is geographically exceptional: protected by two oceans and surrounded by weak neighbors, the United States can project power abroad while remaining secure at home. Mearsheimer and Stephen Walt articulate this as "offshore balancing"—a strategy that lets regional powers check each other while the United States intervenes only to prevent a peer hegemon from consolidating control over Europe, Northeast Asia, or the Persian Gulf. The strategy reflects geography: America's insulation permits a restraint unavailable to land-pressured powers.

## Political Economy as Institutional and Moral Order

The second tradition recognizes that markets are not natural but politically constituted, and that their legitimacy depends on moral narratives. Weber's Protestant Ethic argument is foundational. Weber observed that capitalism in its modern form requires not just the pursuit of profit but the continuous, rational pursuit of renewed profit through disciplined enterprise. This peculiar orientation—treating acquisition as an end in itself, combining it with the strict avoidance of spontaneous enjoyment—appears, from the standpoint of happiness or utility, entirely irrational. Yet it became compelling for Calvinists precisely because their theology made worldly labor a sign of divine election.

Weber traced the psychological mechanism: Calvinist predestination created unbearable anxiety about one's salvation; pastoral advice directed this anxiety toward intense worldly activity as evidence of election. The result was a systematic rationalization of conduct, a methodical ordering of life toward productive labor and reinvestment. The economic consequences outlasted the theology: modern workers and entrepreneurs inherit the discipline without needing its religious justification.

Polanyi extended this insight by showing that the very idea of a self-regulating market required the deliberate destruction of traditional social protections. Markets in land, labor, and money do not emerge spontaneously; they must be politically constructed, often against fierce resistance. But once constructed, they generate dislocations that provoke counter-movements—demands for social protection that reshape political coalitions. The "double movement" describes the dialectic between market expansion and protective response that characterized the nineteenth century and arguably characterizes our own era.

## The Moral Grammar of Modernity

The third tradition examines how modern secular politics retains the structure of religious thought even after abandoning its explicit content. Karl Löwith argued that modern philosophies of history—from Hegel through Marx—secularized Christian eschatology, replacing divine providence with historical progress while keeping the narrative arc of fall and redemption. Koselleck refined this insight by analyzing how specific concepts—revolution, progress, crisis—changed their meaning during the eighteenth century transition to modernity, acquiring futural orientations they previously lacked.

Nietzsche approached the problem from a different angle. His *Genealogy of Morals* argued that modern moral sentiments—especially the valorization of the weak, the suffering, the "good"—represent a transvaluation of ancient aristocratic values performed by resentful slaves and their priestly allies. The "slave revolt in morality" inverted the equation of good with noble, powerful, and beautiful, making good equivalent to humble, suffering, and gentle. For Nietzsche, this inversion carried tremendous creative force—resentment could generate new values—but it also embedded a persistent structure of judgment and guilt into Western morality.

René Girard added a mechanism: mimetic desire and the scapegoat. Girard argued that human beings imitate not only behaviors but desires; we want what others want because they want it. This mimetic structure produces rivalry—two people desiring the same object become antagonists. Accumulated rivalry threatens social cohesion until a community channels its violence onto a single victim, a scapegoat whose elimination restores peace. Ancient religion, Girard contended, arose from the ritualized repetition of this founding murder; myth obscures the victim's innocence while ritual re-enacts the cathartic violence. Christianity, in Girard's reading, uniquely exposes the scapegoat mechanism by presenting the victim as innocent—thereby delegitimizing scapegoating even as societies continue to practice it. Modern politics, stripped of explicit sacred authorization, retains the scapegoat structure under new guises: the witch hunt, the show trial, the moral panic.

# Part Three: Evidence from Three Civilizations

The engine's plausibility rests on whether it illuminates actual historical trajectories. The following sketches are not proofs—proof is too strong a word for historical explanation—but they demonstrate how the engine's phases interlock across radically different contexts.

## The Levant: Covenant, Land, and Permanent Contestability

The geography of the Levant placed ancient Israel at the crossroads of empires—Egypt to the south, Mesopotamia to the east, Anatolia to the north, Mediterranean powers to the west. This vulnerable position generated a distinctive coordination problem: how could a small people maintain identity and cohesion against vastly more powerful neighbors? The institutional solution was covenant: a binding agreement between God and Israel that defined chosenness, demanded exclusive loyalty, and tied religious identity to territorial possession.

The Deuteronomic code crystallized this operating system. The covenant promised security and prosperity in exchange for obedience; it explained suffering as punishment for disobedience; it sanctified the land as divine gift and obligation. Crucially, the covenantal framework made the land's possession not merely political but ontological—a matter touching the deepest questions of cosmic order and national destiny.

This sacred geography has proven extraordinarily durable. When ancient Israelites faced Babylonian exile, the prophetic tradition reinterpreted the covenant rather than abandoning it—exile became purification, return became redemption. When rabbinic Judaism developed after the Second Temple's destruction, the land remained central to liturgy and aspiration even as political control vanished. When modern Zionism emerged, it drew explicitly on covenantal geography even among secular founders. And contemporary conflict in Israel-Palestine remains so intractable precisely because both parties operate within frameworks that make territorial compromise feel like metaphysical betrayal.

The engine here is clear: geography (crossroads vulnerability) generated institutions (covenantal religion), institutions produced an operating system (chosenness tied to land), the operating system constrained viable narratives (exile and return, purification and redemption), narratives justified economic and political arrangements (settlement, defense), and grievances generated by those arrangements (dispossession, resistance) update the operating system on all sides.

## Greece: Fragmented Terrain and Agonistic Culture

Greek geography was the opposite of Levantine: mountainous, peninsular, insular, radically fragmented. No river valley invited centralized administration; no easy routes connected city to city. The coordination problem was local autonomy—how could small communities defend themselves and govern their own affairs?

The institutional solution was the polis: the self-governing city-state. Citizenship, military service, and political participation fused into a single package. The operating system that emerged was agonistic—competitive, honor-driven, oriented toward excellence displayed before peers. The Greek gods themselves were portrayed as rivals; Greek athletics, rhetoric, drama, and warfare all shared a common structure of public contest for glory.

The Analects of Confucius provide a striking contrast that illuminates the Greek specificity. Where Confucius taught that the superior man seeks harmony and avoids contention—that the rule of virtue operates like the Pole Star commanding homage without competition—Greek culture celebrated contest as the arena where virtue became visible. The agonistic operating system generated the narratives of Homeric epic, the political theory of citizen equals, the rhetorical practices of democratic assembly—all forms that assumed competition as the medium of excellence.

This same operating system produced Thucydides' realism. The Melian Dialogue's famous dictum—that the strong do what they can while the weak suffer what they must—reflects an agonistic worldview in which power relations are natural and unadorned by covenantal obligation. Thucydides analyzed interstate competition with cold precision because his culture had already normalized such competition within the polis itself.

The Greek trajectory also illustrates the engine's destructive potential. Agonistic rivalry among poleis prevented consolidation; Athens and Sparta exhausted themselves in internecine war; Macedon and then Rome exploited Greek fragmentation. The operating system that generated philosophy and democracy also generated the conditions for conquest.

## China: Harmony, Bureaucracy, and the Moral State

Chinese geography presented yet another configuration: vast territory bisected by major rivers, surrounded by steppe frontiers and mountain barriers. The coordination problems were hydraulic (managing irrigation and flood control), military (defending against nomadic incursions), and administrative (governing enormous populations across great distances). The institutional solution was bureaucratic empire—centralized authority exercised through a trained administrative class selected by examination.

The Confucian operating system legitimated this arrangement. The Analects taught that governance is an extension of personal moral cultivation; that the ruler's virtue radiates outward to transform society; that proper ritual and proper relationships—father-son, ruler-subject, elder-younger—maintain cosmic as well as social order. Confucius declared that one should guide the state by virtue and keep order through ritual propriety, whereupon the people would feel shame and reform themselves. Punishment might compel outward compliance, but only virtue could transform the heart.

This operating system produced a distinctive rationalization of life—but not the capitalist rationalization Weber analyzed in Protestant Europe. The Confucian literati pursued cultivation, bureaucratic advancement, and family honor rather than entrepreneurial accumulation. Weber noted this contrast explicitly in his comparative studies: Confucian ethics oriented toward adjustment to the world, toward maintaining proper relationships within a hierarchical order, rather than toward transforming the world through disciplined labor in a calling.

The Chinese case also illustrates how operating systems shape responses to external challenge. When nineteenth-century European imperialism shattered Chinese assumptions of civilizational centrality, the resulting grievances generated competing mutations of the inherited grammar: reformist Confucianism that sought to graft Western technique onto Chinese essence; revolutionary nationalism that rejected the entire imperial past; Maoist Communism that combined Marxist eschatology with indigenous peasant rebellion traditions. Each response worked within and against the Confucian inheritance; none could simply escape it.

# Part Four: The Engine Today

If the engine is real, what does it predict about contemporary global politics? Not specific events—the engine is a biasing dynamical system, not a crystal ball—but broad patterns and pressures.

## Great-Power Rivalry as the Engine's External Manifestation

The U.S.-China relationship is not a failure of diplomacy or a misunderstanding that better communication could resolve. It is the engine's external gear turning. Two great powers with different operating systems, different geographical situations, and different historical grievances find themselves competing for position in a system that offers no arbiter. Mearsheimer's realism captures the structural logic: the United States, as an offshore power that has long sought to prevent any peer from dominating Eurasia, faces a rising power that seeks regional hegemony in its own backyard.

The operating systems intensify the competition. American liberalism frames global order as a moral project—spreading democracy, upholding human rights, maintaining a "rules-based international order." Chinese nationalism, still processing the "century of humiliation," frames the same order as Western hegemony dressed in universalist rhetoric. Each side's narratives legitimate its position while delegitimating the other's. Grievances accumulate: American complaints about intellectual property theft and market access; Chinese complaints about containment and interference. The engine spins.

## Transnational Capital as Coupling Mechanism

Globalization did not eliminate the engine; it coupled it. Capital now flows across borders at electronic speed, linking production chains, financial markets, and labor markets into a single interdependent system. This coupling has two effects.

First, it creates shared stakes that raise the costs of conflict. Supply chain disruptions, financial contagion, and technological dependency all give great powers reasons to avoid direct war. This is the liberal peace thesis, and it is not wrong—just incomplete.

Second, coupling synchronizes instabilities. A financial crisis in one region propagates globally; a pandemic disrupts everywhere simultaneously; a chokepoint closure (whether the Suez Canal or the Taiwan Strait) threatens multiple economies at once. The double movement operates at world scale: market expansion generates global backlash. Workers in Ohio and Guangdong both experience dislocation from the same processes; their grievances differ in form but share structural origins.

The engine thus accelerates. Events that once took decades to propagate now unfold in months. Narrative contagion spreads at network speed; identity movements coordinate across continents; financial panics cascade before regulators can respond. Braudel's "event time" now punches upward into the deeper structural layers faster than institutions can metabolize the shocks.

## Secular Sacralization

Modern politics is "post-religious" in content but not in structure. The operating systems inherited from religious traditions persist beneath secular vocabulary. Progress remains eschatological—a narrative of history moving toward redemption. Purity and pollution codes remain operative—applied now to ideological rather than ritual categories. Scapegoating remains functional—channeling accumulated grievances onto designated enemies.

Girard's framework illuminates contemporary moral panics, cancel culture, and political polarization. Mimetic rivalry generates undifferentiated conflict; communities restore order by identifying a victim whose exclusion unifies the majority. The victim's guilt or innocence matters less than the cathartic function of the exclusion. But because Christianity (and its secular descendants) delegitimated scapegoating in principle, each scapegoating episode produces bad conscience, counter-accusations, and eventually a new scapegoat. The mechanism continues without the sacred authorization that once stabilized it.

Nietzsche anticipated this dynamic. The death of God did not eliminate the moral structures God underwrote; it destabilized them. Resentment continues to generate values, but without transcendent sanction, those values remain contested. Each grievance produces a transvaluation; each transvaluation produces counter-grievances; the engine churns.

# Part Five: Slowing the Engine

If the engine cannot be stopped—and this essay has argued that its roots reach too deep for any single intervention to halt—it can perhaps be slowed. Slowing the engine means designing institutions that dampen rather than amplify its phases: reducing chokepoint vulnerabilities so that geography's constraints bind less tightly; building coordination mechanisms that do not require sacralization; creating narratives that acknowledge grievance without demanding scapegoats; constructing economic forms that distribute gains widely enough to prevent catastrophic backlash.

None of this is easy. The engine's phases interlock precisely because each phase reduces the options available in the next. Once an operating system has sacralized a territorial claim, "rational" compromise becomes betrayal. Once a grievance has crystallized into identity, material compensation feels like insult. Once a scapegoat has been identified, evidence of innocence looks like conspiracy.

Yet historical examples suggest that deceleration is possible. The post-World War II European settlement—however imperfect and now fraying—represented a deliberate effort to bind former enemies in institutional frameworks that raised the costs of war while distributing the gains of peace. The settlement did not eliminate the engine; it slowed it for seventy years. Whether comparable settlements are possible for the U.S.-China rivalry, for the Middle East's nested conflicts, for the global economy's distributional tensions remains to be seen.

What is certain is that pretending the engine does not exist guarantees failure. Elites who imagine that rational argument will dissolve sacred attachments will be surprised when it does not. Reformers who imagine that economic growth will eliminate grievances will be surprised when new grievances emerge. Cosmopolitans who imagine that education will dissolve identity will be surprised when education becomes a site of identity conflict. The engine has been running for millennia. Understanding its phases is the first step toward building the friction that might slow it.

# Conclusion: The Engine Spins Again

Halford Mackinder, writing in 1904, warned that the age of European expansion was closing and a new era of "closed political space" was beginning. From the present time forth, he wrote, we shall have to deal with a closed political system of worldwide scope, where every explosion of social forces will be sharply re-echoed from the far side of the globe.

We are living in Mackinder's prophecy. The system is closed; the echoes are immediate; the engine spins faster than ever before. Geography still constrains—now through semiconductor chokepoints and energy transit routes as much as through mountain passes and river valleys. Institutions still harden—now as platform monopolies and financial architectures as much as as priesthoods and bureaucracies. Operating systems still shape cognition—now as algorithmic curation and identity politics as much as as sacred texts and ritual calendars. Narratives still mobilize—now as viral memes and influencer campaigns as much as as imperial propaganda and religious revival. Economic forms still generate grievances—now as platform precarity and asset inflation as much as as industrial exploitation and agrarian dispossession. And grievances still mutate the operating systems—now as populist nationalism and "woke" progressivism as much as as reformation and revolution.

The content changes; the structure persists. That is the engine's lesson. And understanding the structure is the beginning of wisdom about how to live—and perhaps how to build—within a world that keeps polarizing despite every effort to hold it together.
