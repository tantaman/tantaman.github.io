# The Unraveling

## How Materialism Destroys What It Built

There is a deeper irony that Dostoevsky and Simone Weil would insist we see: materialism did not ultimately serve even the elite who selected it.

It dissolved meaning. It undermined loyalty. It produced nihilism. It eroded legitimacy. The same framework that made empire efficient made it spiritually brittle.

This is not a failure of implementation. It is the logic of the system working itself out. Materialism is not a stable resting point. It is a phase—a civilizational tool that works until it empties the soul that wields it.

The evidence is all around us. But to see it clearly, we need the diagnosticians who named it before it became obvious.

---

## I. Dostoevsky's Prophecy: Bread Without Meaning

In 1880, Fyodor Dostoevsky published *The Brothers Karamazov*, which contains a parable so prophetic that the twentieth century seems like a series of footnotes to it. In "The Grand Inquisitor," Christ returns to earth during the Spanish Inquisition and is immediately arrested. The aged Inquisitor visits him in his cell—not to worship, but to explain why Christ must be executed again.

The Inquisitor's argument is devastatingly simple: You made a mistake. You offered humanity freedom when what they needed was bread and security.

> "Dost Thou see these stones in the desolate and glaring wilderness? Command that these stones be made bread—and mankind will run after Thee, obedient and grateful like a herd of cattle."

Christ refused to bribe humanity with bread. He insisted on the terrible dignity of free choice. And the Inquisitor regards this as cruelty:

> "Thou didst refuse to accept the offer for fear of depriving men of their free choice; for where is there freedom of choice where men are bribed with bread?"

The Inquisitor's solution is to give humanity what it actually wants: material satisfaction without the burden of meaning.

> "Oh, never, never, will they learn to feed themselves without our help! No science will ever give them bread so long as they remain free."

This is the Grand Bargain of materialism: *security in exchange for transcendence*. The masses will be fed, housed, entertained—and they will surrender their souls in gratitude.

But here is what the Inquisitor knows and cannot say: **this bargain is unstable**.

> "For the mystery of human being does not solely rest in the desire to live, but in the problem—for what should one live at all? Without a clear perception of his reasons for living, man will never consent to live, and will rather destroy himself than tarry on earth, though he be surrounded with bread."

This is the prophecy: a civilization that offers only bread will eventually face people who, surrounded by unprecedented material abundance, find existence unbearable. Not because they lack comfort, but because they have been systematically deprived of any answer to the question *why*.

## II. The Tower That Cannot Stand: Girard's Mimetic Collapse

René Girard saw something that connects Dostoevsky's prophecy to the mechanics of social dissolution. Human desire, Girard argued, is fundamentally *mimetic*—we want what others want, not because of the object itself, but because others desire it.

> "Passive, submissive imitation does exist, but hatred of conformity and extreme individualism are no less imitative. Today they constitute a negative conformism that is more formidable than the positive version."

In traditional societies, this mimetic tendency was contained by what Girard called the *scapegoat mechanism*. When mimetic rivalry threatened to tear a community apart—when everyone wanted what everyone else had—violence was channeled onto a single victim. The sacrifice restored order, and the victim, having brought peace, was retroactively sacralized.

> "The one way to stop the wave of violence and keep societies from destroying themselves is through a sacrifice... The scapegoat must be both part of the community yet somehow apart from it."

Here is the crucial point: **this mechanism required a certain kind of ignorance**. The community had to believe in the guilt of the victim. The ritual worked precisely because no one understood it as a ritual. It was experienced as justice, not as psychological displacement.

Christianity, Girard argued, exposed the mechanism. The Gospels tell the story from the victim's perspective. They reveal the scapegoat as innocent. And this revelation, once made, cannot be unmade.

> "Christ is the only man to overcome the barrier erected by Satan. He dies in order to avoid participating in the system of scapegoats."

But here is the problem: materialism inherits this revelation while discarding the framework that could absorb it. Modern societies know, at some level, that their scapegoats are arbitrary. They can no longer believe in the guilt of the excluded. And yet they have no transcendent reference point that might provide an alternative basis for social cohesion.

The result, Girard predicted, is escalating mimetic crisis without resolution:

> "A dynamic force seems to be drawing first Western society, then the rest of the world, toward a state of relative indifferentiation never before known on earth, a strange kind of nonculture or anticulture we call modern."

Without the scapegoat mechanism and without transcendence, mimetic rivalry has no release valve. Everyone imitates everyone. Everyone competes with everyone. And the competition has no endpoint, no moment of resolution, no sacred center that might interrupt it.

This is visible in the texture of contemporary life: the infinite scroll, the perpetual comparison, the race for status that can never be won because there is no finishing line. Girard's theory predicts that such a society will oscillate between paralysis and spasmodic violence, seeking scapegoats it can no longer fully believe in, finding temporary relief that immediately dissolves.

## III. Kierkegaard's Diagnosis: The Sickness That Cannot Die

Søren Kierkegaard named the condition before it became epidemic. In *The Sickness Unto Death* (1849), he described a malady worse than physical death: *despair*.

> "The torment of despair is precisely this, not to be able to die. So to be sick unto death is not to be able to die—yet not as though there were hope of life; no, the hopelessness in this case is that even the last hope, death, is not available."

This is not the ordinary despair of difficult circumstances. It is something stranger: the despair of having a self that one cannot bear to be, yet cannot escape.

> "An individual in despair despairs over something. So it seems for a moment, but only for a moment; in the same moment the true despair manifests itself. For in the fact that he despaired of something, he really despaired of himself."

Kierkegaard distinguished forms of despair by their relationship to consciousness. The lowest form is the despair of those who do not even know they have a self—who live entirely in the finite, the immediate, the material. They are in despair without knowing it.

> "The greatest danger, that of losing one's own self, may pass off as quietly as if it were nothing; every other loss, that of an arm, a leg, five dollars, a wife, is sure to be noticed."

The materialist framework *produces* this unconscious despair as its default condition. By defining the human being as a bundle of preferences, a utility-maximizing machine, a consumer with a biological substrate, it systematically denies the existence of the self that might despair. And so people lose themselves without noticing—because the framework has no vocabulary for what they have lost.

But despair does not disappear simply because it is unrecognized. It manifests in other forms:

> "With every increase in the degree of consciousness, and in proportion to that increase, the intensity of despair increases: the more consciousness the more intense the despair."

As consciousness rises—as people begin to sense that something is wrong, that their lives lack meaning, that they are not who they are supposed to be—the despair intensifies. And because the materialist framework offers no resources for addressing it, the despair becomes *defiant*:

> "The more consciousness there is in such a sufferer who in despair wills to be himself, the more his despair intensifies and becomes demonic."

The demonic form of despair is the refusal of healing. It is the perverse attachment to one's own suffering as a form of protest against a universe that has failed to provide meaning. Kierkegaard saw people who would rather be right about their misery than accept grace:

> "Once he would gladly have given everything to be rid of this agony, but he was kept waiting; now it is too late, now he would rather rage against everything and be the wronged victim of the whole world."

This is the psychology of a civilization that has been promised everything and given only bread.

## IV. Foucault's Reversal: When Power Becomes Its Own Prison

Michel Foucault traced how materialist administration actually works—the techniques by which modern societies produce "docile bodies" suitable for economic and political management.

> "Discipline increases the forces of the body (in economic terms of utility) and diminishes these same forces (in political terms of obedience). In short, it dissociates power from the body."

The disciplinary society operates through three primary techniques: *hierarchical observation* (everyone is watched), *normalizing judgment* (everyone is measured against a standard), and *examination* (everyone is documented, classified, ranked).

> "It is the fact of being constantly seen... that maintains the disciplined individual in his subjection."

The Panopticon—Bentham's design for a prison where inmates could be watched at any moment without knowing when—became for Foucault the model of modern society. Not a literal prison, but a form of power that operates through visibility:

> "The Panopticon produces a state of conscious and permanent visibility that assures the automatic functioning of power."

But here is what Foucault understood that the architects of this system did not: **disciplinary power is unstable**. It produces resistance not as an accident but as a necessary correlate. The more total the surveillance, the more strategies emerge for evading it. The more comprehensive the normalization, the more pathologies are created at the margins.

> "Where there is power, there is resistance, and yet, or rather consequently, this resistance is never in a position of exteriority in relation to power."

The disciplinary society does not produce satisfied, stable subjects. It produces subjects whose entire existence is defined by their relationship to power—either conforming or evading, either normal or deviant. The system that was designed to create order perpetually generates the disorders it then must manage.

This is the paradox: the more efficiently materialism administers human life, the more life becomes *nothing but* administration. And beings who are nothing but administered lose the capacity to feel that their lives are worth administering.

## V. Byung-Chul Han: When Exploitation Becomes Self-Inflicted

The South Korean-German philosopher Byung-Chul Han has extended Foucault's analysis to describe what comes *after* disciplinary society—and what he sees is even darker.

> "Today's society is no longer Foucault's disciplinary world of hospitals, madhouses, prisons, barracks, and factories. It has long been replaced by another regime, namely a society of fitness studios, office towers, banks, airports, shopping malls, and genetic laboratories."

We have moved, Han argues, from the *disciplinary society* to the *achievement society*. The transition is subtle but decisive. Disciplinary society operated through prohibition: *you may not*. Achievement society operates through infinite possibility: *you can*.

> "The positivity of Can is much more efficient than the negativity of Should. Therefore, the social unconscious switches from Should to Can."

This sounds like freedom. It feels like freedom. But Han argues it is something far more insidious:

> "The achievement-subject stands free from external instances of domination forcing it to work and exploiting it. It is subject to no one if not to itself. However, the disappearance of domination does not entail freedom. Instead, it makes freedom and constraint coincide."

The achievement society does not need to coerce you. You coerce yourself. You become your own taskmaster, your own prison guard, your own exploiter:

> "Excess work and performance escalate into auto-exploitation. This is more efficient than allo-exploitation, for the feeling of freedom attends it. The exploiter is simultaneously the exploited."

This is why burnout has become the characteristic pathology of our age. Not because the work is harder than before—by most measures, physical labor has never been easier. But because the work has no boundary. There is no external authority against which one might rebel, no limit that one might reach and rest. The imperative to achieve comes from within, and it is insatiable.

> "The complaint of the depressive individual, 'Nothing is possible,' can only occur in a society that thinks, 'Nothing is impossible.'"

Depression, in Han's analysis, is not a random malfunction. It is the logical consequence of a system that demands infinite achievement from finite beings. The depressive has not failed to achieve; the depressive has exhausted themselves attempting to achieve what cannot be achieved—to be everything, to optimize infinitely, to succeed without limit.

> "Achievement society is the society of self-exploitation. The achievement-subject exploits itself until it burns out."

And here is the final twist: this self-exploitation serves capital more efficiently than any external oppression ever could. The worker who exploits himself requires no overseer. The consumer who chases infinite self-improvement requires no advertising. The system has successfully externalized its costs onto the psyche of its subjects.

> "Auto-exploitation is more efficient than allo-exploitation because a deceptive feeling of freedom accompanies it."

## VI. Simone Weil: The Destruction from Within

Simone Weil saw all of this coming. Writing in the 1930s and early 1940s, she diagnosed the condition with terrible precision.

> "All the natural movements of the soul are controlled by laws analogous to those of physical gravity. Grace is the only exception."

Gravity, for Weil, was the automatic tendency of consciousness toward self-expansion, self-assertion, domination. It is what happens when the soul is not actively pulled upward by something beyond itself. And materialism, by definition, removes everything that might pull upward.

What remains is pure gravity—the soul's tendency to fill all available space, to consume, to acquire, to dominate. And this tendency, unchecked, is self-destructive:

> "Relentless necessity, wretchedness, distress, the crushing burden of poverty and of labour which wears us out, cruelty, torture, violent death, constraint, disease—all these constitute divine love. It is God who in love withdraws from us so that we can love him."

Weil understood something that the materialist framework cannot grasp: human beings require resistance in order to grow. They require something that pushes back against their self-expansion. Without this resistance, the soul collapses into itself.

The materialist project is precisely the attempt to remove all resistance—to engineer a world where every need is met, every desire satisfied, every obstacle overcome. And Weil saw that this project, if successful, would destroy the human beings it claimed to serve:

> "Imagination is continually at work filling up all the fissures through which grace might pass."

A soul that has no fissures—no wounds, no limits, no failures—is a soul through which grace cannot enter. And without grace, there is only gravity. The soul that has everything still falls.

## VII. The Evidence All Around Us

The diagnosticians have spoken. Now look at the evidence.

**Depression and anxiety have reached epidemic proportions**, precisely in the societies with the highest material abundance. The World Health Organization reports that depression is the leading cause of disability worldwide. This is not despite material progress; it is, at least in part, *because of* it—because material progress without meaning produces the precise conditions Kierkegaard described.

**Loneliness has become a public health crisis**. In societies saturated with connection technology, people report unprecedented levels of isolation. The United States Surgeon General declared loneliness an epidemic. This is not a paradox but a confirmation: connection without depth, relationship without transcendence, produces isolation disguised as contact.

**Trust in institutions has collapsed**. Across Western democracies, trust in government, media, churches, corporations, and expert authorities has plummeted to historic lows. This is not simply cynicism; it is the natural consequence of institutions that have been systematically drained of any legitimacy beyond material delivery. When the only justification is utility, any failure of utility destroys justification.

**The birth rate has fallen below replacement** across nearly all developed nations. This is not merely an economic calculation about the cost of children. It is, at some level, a civilizational vote of no confidence—a collective judgment that the future does not warrant perpetuation. People who see no meaning beyond their own lifespan do not invest in what comes after.

**Political systems oscillate between paralysis and spasm**. The moderate center, which once held through shared assumptions about progress, has fragmented. What remains are increasingly extreme alternatives—not because people have become more extreme, but because the center had nothing to hold onto except material improvement, and material improvement has ceased to satisfy.

**The young, in particular, report unprecedented levels of hopelessness**. Studies consistently show that younger generations are less happy, more anxious, and more likely to report that their lives lack meaning than any generation on record. This is the cohort that has grown up entirely within the achievement society. They are Han's achievement-subjects from birth.

## VIII. The Logic of Collapse

These symptoms are not accidents. They are the logic of materialism working itself out.

Materialism promises to make human beings happy by giving them what they want. But human beings do not know what they want until they see what others want (Girard). And what they want, at the deepest level, is not any object but *meaning*—a reason to live, a story that makes sense of suffering, a transcendent framework that can answer the question *why* (Dostoevsky).

Materialism cannot provide meaning because it has defined meaning out of existence. What cannot be measured cannot be administered. What cannot be administered does not exist. And meaning—by its very nature—cannot be measured or administered.

The result is a civilization that is phenomenally successful at producing goods and services, yet systematically incapable of producing the one thing human beings actually need to survive: a reason to go on.

> "Without a clear perception of his reasons for living, man will never consent to live, and will rather destroy himself than tarry on earth, though he be surrounded with bread."

The Grand Inquisitor's bargain—security for transcendence—has been accepted. And the bill is coming due.

## IX. What the Rulers Cannot See

Here is the final irony, the twist that Dostoevsky and Weil would insist upon: **materialism does not even serve the interests of those who selected it**.

The elite who chose materialist frameworks because they made populations legible and governable now find themselves governing populations that cannot be governed—not because they resist, but because they have lost the capacity for the loyalty, sacrifice, and long-term thinking that governance requires.

An army of nihilists will not fight.
A workforce of the depressed will not produce.
A citizenry without meaning will not defend institutions.
A civilization without transcendence will not reproduce itself.

The framework that made empire efficient has made empire *spiritually brittle*. It optimized for short-term control and sacrificed long-term legitimacy. It created docile bodies and discovered that docile bodies, pushed far enough, become corpses—sometimes literally, more often metaphorically.

The rulers are caught in their own trap. They cannot restore transcendence—they have spent centuries destroying the conditions for it. They cannot offer meaning—they have no vocabulary for it. They can only offer more of what has already failed: more stimulation, more entertainment, more consumption, more therapy, more medication.

And so the cycle accelerates. Each failure of meaning is met with intensified administration. Each intensified administration produces more failures of meaning. The achievement society produces burnout; burnout is treated with more achievement. The disciplinary society produces resistance; resistance is met with more discipline.

Weber saw this coming:

> "Not summer's bloom lies ahead of us, but rather a polar night of icy darkness and hardness."

---

## X. The Question That Remains

The diagnosticians do not agree on what comes next. Some see collapse as inevitable. Others see transformation as possible. None see a return to the status quo ante.

What they agree on is this: **materialism is not stable**. It is a phase, a transitional condition, a tool that destroys the hand that wields it. The question is not whether it will end, but how, and what will follow.

Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor knows that his system will ultimately fail:

> "For having begun to build their tower of Babel without us, they will end, of course, with cannibalism."

The tower falls. The only question is what is built from the rubble.

Kierkegaard offers the possibility of faith—not as belief in propositions, but as the reconciliation of the self with the power that established it:

> "In relating itself to itself, and in willing to be itself, the self rests transparently in the power that established it."

Weil offers attention—the capacity to wait, to suffer, to remain open to what cannot be grasped:

> "Attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity."

Girard offers the imitation of Christ—not the scapegoating Christ of institutional religion, but the Christ who refuses to participate in the cycle of violence:

> "Christ is the only man to overcome the barrier erected by Satan."

These are not programs. They are orientations. They are ways of standing in the ruins.

The materialist civilization will not be reformed by argument. It will be outlasted by those who found, within its bleakest winters, the resources for something else—who discovered that the silence of infinite spaces was not, after all, the last word.

Weil wrote:

> "Grace is the only exception to gravity."

The exception remains possible. Whether it will be seized—whether enough souls will turn toward what pulls upward before the structure collapses entirely—is the open question of our time.

---

## Coda: The Tool That Devours

Materialism was selected because it worked. It made populations measurable, predictable, governable. It dissolved rival authorities. It increased productive capacity. It built empires.

But it worked by consuming the substrate on which it depended. It extracted meaning from culture the way industrial agriculture extracts nutrients from soil. For a time, the yields were spectacular. And then the soil gave out.

The twentieth century was the century of materialist triumph—and of materialist catastrophe. The most scientifically advanced, administratively sophisticated civilizations in human history produced the Holocaust, the Gulag, the Great Leap Forward. Not despite their sophistication, but *through* it. The same techniques that made populations legible made them eliminable.

The twenty-first century is the century of materialist exhaustion. Not catastrophe in the old sense—no world wars, no mass famines—but a slower dissolution. The diseases are quieter: depression, loneliness, meaninglessness, the inability to reproduce, the loss of confidence in the future.

> "Disciplinary society's negativity produces madmen and criminals. In contrast, achievement society creates depressives and losers."

The Grand Inquisitor thought he was building paradise. He was building a cemetery.

The question now is whether anything can grow from the graves.
