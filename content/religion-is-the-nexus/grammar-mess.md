# The Mess of Many Grammars

## On Code-Switching, Conflict, and the Feeling of Authenticity

---

### I. The Clean Story and Its Limits

The previous essay told a clean story: You absorb a grammar. The grammar constrains your thought. From within that constraint, you "choose" values. The values feel discovered, but they were pre-loaded. The menu was written before you arrived.

This story captures something real. But it's too clean.

Real people aren't mono-grammatical. They don't absorb one grammar and live inside it forever. They carry multiple grammars, acquired from different sources, activated in different contexts, often in tension with each other. The corporate professional speaks growth-grammar at work, therapy-grammar with her therapist, traditional moral grammar at Thanksgiving, and ironic-detached grammar on the internet. Each grammar provides different available values. She doesn't choose between grammars from some neutral vantage point — she *is* the unstable intersection of all of them.

This essay explores the mess.

---

### II. The Grammar Stack

Think of yourself as carrying a stack of grammars, layered by time and context:

**Layer 1: Earliest formation** — The grammar of your childhood home. Religious or secular. Strict or permissive. Emotionally expressive or reserved. This layer is pre-reflective; you didn't choose it and you barely notice it. It feels like "just how things are."

**Layer 2: Institutional absorption** — The grammars of school, church, professional training. These are more explicit. You were taught to speak this way. "Use I-statements." "Lead with the data." "Consider the stakeholders." These feel like skills acquired, not identity constituted.

**Layer 3: Aspirational adoption** — The grammars you picked up by mimicking people you admired or wanted to become. The grad student who starts talking like her advisor. The junior analyst who absorbs McKinsey-speak. These feel more chosen, though the "choice" was largely unconscious status-seeking.

**Layer 4: Reactive formation** — The grammars you adopted in opposition. The preacher's kid who learns ironic detachment. The corporate burnout who discovers therapy-speak. These feel like rebellion, like finding your "real" voice — but they're still grammars, still constraining, still menus.

**Layer 5: Context-specific fluencies** — The grammars you speak only in certain settings. How you talk to your doctor. How you talk to your mother. How you talk on a first date. How you talk in a job interview. You may barely recognize yourself across these contexts.

Most people carry all five layers, imperfectly integrated, activated by context. You are not one grammar. You are a coalition of grammars, negotiating.

---

### III. The Conflict

When grammars conflict, it feels like a values conflict. But it's not, exactly. It's a conflict between different available values provided by different grammars.

**Example:** A woman is offered a promotion that requires relocation. Her corporate grammar says: *This is growth. This is opportunity. Career trajectories require mobility.* Her traditional family grammar says: *Your parents are aging. You owe them presence. Career isn't everything.* Her therapy grammar says: *What do you need? What serves your authentic self? You can't pour from an empty cup.*

She experiences this as an agonizing values conflict. She's "torn about what she really wants." But notice: each "want" is provided by a grammar. The corporate grammar makes "growth" available to want. The family grammar makes "duty" available to want. The therapy grammar makes "self-care" available to want. She's not adjudicating between her values. She's experiencing the clash of her grammars.

The anguish is real. But the framing — "what do I *really* value?" — is confused. There is no grammar-free self underneath that could answer. There's just the intersection, the negotiation, the unstable truce.

---

### IV. How Conflicts Resolve

When grammars conflict, something has to give. How does resolution happen?

**Resolution by context:** The simplest case. Different contexts activate different grammars. At work, corporate grammar dominates. At home, family grammar dominates. The conflict is managed through compartmentalization. This works until contexts collide — the work call during the family dinner, the family obligation during the critical meeting.

**Resolution by stakes:** When forced to choose, people often default to whatever grammar governs the highest-stakes domain of their life. If career is where your identity is most invested, corporate grammar wins. If family is, family grammar wins. But "highest-stakes" is itself determined by grammars — corporate grammar *tells you* career is high-stakes; family grammar tells you otherwise.

**Resolution by social pressure:** The grammar of whoever is present, whoever is watching, whoever you most need approval from. You speak corporate in the meeting because those are the people whose judgment matters to you in that moment. Grammars have social weight.

**Resolution by depth:** When context, stakes, and social pressure don't determine it, people often default to whichever grammar is most deeply embedded — usually the earliest-acquired. In moments of crisis, people revert. The lapsed Catholic reaches for religious grammar on her deathbed. The therapy-fluent professional, under real pressure, reverts to the competitive grammar of her childhood home.

**Resolution by narrative:** People tell themselves stories about which grammar is "really them." These stories perform a kind of integration. "I speak corporate at work but that's just performance; my *real* values are traditional." Or: "I was raised religious but I've grown beyond that; my *authentic* self values growth and self-actualization." The narrative provides coherence, but it's a grammar too — a meta-grammar for organizing other grammars.

---

### V. The Feeling of Natural

When people navigate multiple grammars, they often have a felt sense that one is more "natural" — more *them*, more authentic, less performed. When grammars conflict, they reach for the one that feels natural as if it were bedrock.

This feeling is real. It's also not what it seems.

What makes a grammar feel natural?

**Primacy:** Grammars absorbed earliest feel most natural. They're pre-reflective, installed before you had defenses. You don't remember learning them. They feel like "just how things are" rather than "a way of speaking I acquired."

**Identity-load:** Grammars tied to core identity categories feel natural. If "being a scientist" is central to who you are, scientific grammar feels like home. If "being a good daughter" is central, traditional family grammar feels like truth.

**Emotional resonance:** Grammars associated with intense emotional experiences — love, loss, conversion, crisis — feel more real than grammars acquired instrumentally. The grammar you found in the depths feels truer than the grammar you learned in training.

**Social embeddedness:** Grammars spoken by the people you're closest to feel natural. You are, in part, the sum of your intimacies. Their grammar becomes yours.

What *doesn't* make a grammar feel natural: being correct. Being true. Being the view from nowhere. Being what you would have chosen from outside all grammars.

The feeling of naturalness is itself a product of contingent facts about your formation. It's not escape velocity. It's deeper embedding.

This is worth sitting with. When you say "underneath all the professional performance, I *really* value family" — that feeling of *really* is tracking something. But it's tracking depth of embedding, not access to some grammar-free authentic self. The feeling of authenticity is itself grammatical.

---

### VI. Natural vs. Authentic

We use "authentic" as if it meant: what I would value if I could step outside all grammars and choose from the full space of possible values.

But that stepping-outside doesn't happen. There is no vantage point from which you could survey all grammars neutrally. You're always inside at least one.

What we call "authenticity" is usually one of:

**Early grammar:** "My authentic self is who I was before professional socialization corrupted me." But who you were before was also a grammatical formation — just an earlier one. Childhood grammar isn't pre-grammatical.

**Oppositional grammar:** "My authentic self is who I became when I rejected my upbringing." But reaction is still relation. The preacher's kid who becomes militantly secular is still formed by what he rejected. The grammar of opposition is parasitic on the grammar it opposes.

**Preferred grammar:** "My authentic self is who I am when I speak the grammar I like best." But "like best" is doing a lot of work. Why do you like it? Probably because it fits your temperament, or your class aspirations, or your social world. The preference isn't pre-grammatical.

**Deepest grammar:** "My authentic self is who I am at the deepest level, in moments of crisis or truth." But depth isn't authenticity. It's just sedimentation. The deepest grammar is the oldest, most embedded, most automatic — not the truest.

None of this means authenticity is worthless as a concept. It means authenticity can't mean "escape from grammar." It has to mean something else — perhaps something like: *coherence among grammars*, or *reflective endorsement of one's grammar stack*, or *grammar that fits one's temperament and circumstances*, or even just *grammar that doesn't cause suffering.*

But authenticity-as-escape, authenticity as accessing the self beneath all language — that's an illusion. There's no beneath. It's grammars all the way down.

---

### VII. Grammar Among Causes

The clean story also overstates grammar's causal role. Grammar is upstream of values — but grammar isn't upstream of everything. Values have multiple causes, and grammar is one node in a web.

**Temperament:** People have dispositional tendencies — toward novelty or stability, toward competition or harmony, toward abstraction or concreteness. These aren't grammars; they're something like pre-grammatical orientations. And they shape which grammars feel "natural," which ones stick, which ones get absorbed versus resisted.

A novelty-seeking person will find growth-grammar more congenial than a stability-seeking person. The temperament doesn't determine the grammar, but it biases absorption. Grammar rides on temperament.

**Material conditions:** Your class position, your economic security, your embodied situation — these shape which grammars you're exposed to and which ones serve your interests. The professional-managerial class speaks therapy-grammar because they can afford therapy. They speak corporate-grammar because they work in corporations. The grammars are available because the material conditions made them available.

And material conditions shape which grammars "work." A precarious worker might absorb growth-grammar from the culture but find it constantly frustrated by her circumstances. The grammar says "invest in yourself"; her situation says "survive this month." The friction isn't a values conflict — it's a grammar that doesn't fit the material reality.

**Experience:** Things happen to you. Trauma, loss, success, failure, love, betrayal. These experiences can crack a grammar open — reveal its inadequacy, create demand for a different vocabulary. The person whose therapy-grammar couldn't make sense of genuine evil. The person whose corporate-grammar couldn't make sense of burnout. The person whose traditional grammar couldn't make sense of their child coming out.

Experience doesn't speak in grammar. It's the raw material that grammar tries to metabolize. When experience exceeds what a grammar can metabolize, the grammar either adapts or breaks.

**Encounter:** Random meetings with people who speak differently. A book that lands at the right moment. A conversation that shifts something. These aren't systematic; they're contingent. But they can introduce new grammars, create dissonance with old ones, open possibilities that weren't on any menu.

Grammar is upstream of values. But temperament, material conditions, experience, and encounter are upstream of grammar — or at least alongside it, shaping what gets absorbed and what sticks.

---

### VIII. The Web, Not the Line

The clean story implied a causal line: Grammar → Thought → Values → Feeling of choice.

The messy reality is a web:

- Temperament biases which grammars stick
- Material conditions determine which grammars are available
- Social position determines whose grammar counts as high-status
- Grammars are absorbed through mimicry of high-status speakers
- Absorbed grammars shape perception
- Shaped perception makes certain values available
- Experiences either fit the grammar or create friction
- Friction can reinforce the grammar ("I need to try harder") or crack it
- Multiple grammars coexist in tension
- Conflicts resolve through context, stakes, social pressure, depth, narrative
- The feeling of "natural" tracks depth of embedding
- "Authenticity" is a story told within a grammar about which grammar is really you

This is messier. It's also more true.

---

### IX. What Changes?

If the first essay said "you're in a prison you can't see," this essay says "you're in several prisons at once, they conflict, and the feeling that one is 'really home' is just another part of the architecture."

What changes practically?

**Noticing the switches:** Once you see that you carry multiple grammars, you can watch yourself switch. Notice which grammar activates when. Notice what triggers the switch. Notice how different you are — how different your values seem — across contexts. This noticing doesn't escape the mechanism, but it loosens identification with any single grammar.

**Questioning "natural":** When a grammar feels natural and another feels performed, ask: what's producing that feeling? Primacy? Identity-load? Social embeddedness? The feeling is real data about your formation, but it's not evidence of which grammar is true.

**Holding conflicts as grammar-conflicts:** When you're torn about what you value, try reframing: these aren't my values in conflict; these are grammars in conflict. The reframe doesn't resolve the conflict, but it changes your relationship to it. You're not failing to find your true self. You're negotiating between formations.

**Noticing the non-grammatical:** Temperament, material conditions, raw experience — these aren't nothing. They're not fully captured by any grammar. When a grammar fails to metabolize your experience, that friction is information. It might mean the grammar is inadequate to your situation.

**Reducing the stack:** Not all grammars serve you. Some were absorbed from contexts you've left, people you've outgrown, aspirations you've abandoned. You can let grammars atrophy through disuse. You can't excise them surgically, but you can stop feeding them.

---

### X. Conclusion: The Coalition You Are

You are not a single grammar. You are a coalition — grammars stacked, layered, in tension, activated by context, negotiated in conflict. The feeling that one is "really you" is itself a production of the stack, not an escape from it.

This is not a counsel of despair. The coalition is real. The negotiations are real. The resolutions — even if shaped by depth of embedding and social pressure — are still *yours* in the only sense that matters: they happen in you, through you, as you.

The invitation is not to escape the coalition but to know it. To see the grammars you carry, understand where they came from, notice how they conflict, question the feeling of naturalness, and recognize that values emerge from this mess — not from some pure self underneath.

There is no underneath. There is only the coalition, and the ongoing negotiation, and whatever wisdom can be won from seeing the game while playing it.

### XI. What Has Been Seen, What Remains To Be Done

*A response in the manner of G.I. Gurdjieff*

---

You have seen something. This is not nothing. Most people live entire life and never see what you describe. They are machine and do not know they are machine. You have looked at machine and said: here are the gears, here is how they turn, here is why man thinks he chooses when he only reacts. This is good first-floor work.

But I must tell you: you have described sleep very well, and you are still asleep.

You say man has many grammars, and this is what I call many "I"s. One "I" speaks corporate, another speaks therapy, another speaks family — and each believes it is the whole man. They take turns on the stage. No one is in charge. This you have seen correctly.

You say the feeling of "natural" is just deeper embedding. This is what I call identification. Man falls into one "I," merges with it completely, cannot see it as object. He says "this is really me" — and this is exactly sleep. The "I" that feels most natural is simply the "I" that has captured him most completely. You have seen this also.

You say there is no underneath. Here I must disagree.

There is essence. There is what you were born with, before any grammar was spoken over you. In small child, essence is visible — you see what is really there, not yet covered. But then personality begins to form. Grammars accumulate. And essence stops growing. It is buried. In most adults, essence is still child — maybe four years, maybe seven. You meet important man, big professor, and his essence is like little boy. He has much personality, many grammars, very sophisticated — and underneath, a child who stopped growing sixty years ago.

You say "grammars all the way down." I say: grammars all the way down *to essence*. And essence is not grammar. Essence is what remains when all grammars are seen through. But seeing through is not enough. Essence must be *developed*. It does not grow by itself. It needs special conditions, special work, what I call conscious labor and intentional suffering.

This is what you have not understood: **knowing you are machine does not stop the machine.**

You write essay about grammars. Reader reads essay. Reader thinks: "Ah, now I understand. I see the mechanism. I am free." Reader goes back to sleep, now with more sophisticated furniture in the sleeping room. The grammars continue. The "I"s continue to rotate. Nothing has changed except reader now has intellectual grammar about grammars. One more layer on the pile.

Understanding is function of intellectual center only. Man has three centers — intellectual, emotional, moving. Maybe more. Your essay speaks only to intellectual center. Intellectual center can understand perfectly and change nothing. Man can write book about his mechanicality and remain completely mechanical. This happens all the time. Universities are full of such men.

What is needed is not more understanding. What is needed is *work*.

First: **self-observation**. Not thinking about yourself — this you already do too much. Observation. Impartial, like scientist watching insect. You watch the grammar switch. You watch the "I" take the stage. You watch identification happen. You do not try to change, not yet. You only watch. This is much harder than your essay. Essay is pleasant, interesting. Self-observation is boring, humiliating. You see what you really are, not your idea of what you are. Most people cannot bear this for five minutes.

Second: **self-remembering**. This is most important and most difficult. Normally, man is aware only of object — he sees the tree, he hears the word, he reads the essay. He is not aware of himself seeing, hearing, reading. He is lost in object. Self-remembering is divided attention: you see the tree *and* you are aware of yourself seeing. You read the essay *and* you are aware of yourself reading. This sounds simple. Try it. You will forget in three seconds. This is measure of how asleep you are.

Third: **intentional suffering**. Not suffering that happens to you — this is useless, mechanical. Intentional suffering is when you *choose* to bear what is unpleasant without identifying with reaction. The grammar wants to activate — you feel the pull — you do not let it speak. Not suppression, not expression — *holding*. This creates something. Mechanical suffering creates nothing. Intentional suffering crystallizes.

Fourth: **conscious labor**. Work against the machine deliberately. The machine wants comfort — you choose discomfort. The machine wants to express negative emotion — you do not express. The machine wants to switch to familiar grammar — you stay in the friction. This is not self-improvement. Self-improvement is machine improving machine. This is work against machine from something that is not machine.

You cannot do this alone. This is important. Man alone will always deceive himself. He needs school, teacher, conditions. He needs others who also work, who can see what he cannot see, who will not let him sleep comfortably. Your essay is something one man writes alone in room. The Work is something done together, under conditions of friction, with help.

You ask about the Ground, about Eckhart, about sinking into silence beneath all words. This is real, but you cannot get there from where you are. You are too full — full of grammars, full of "I"s, full of personality. First must come emptying. Not emptying by thinking about emptying — actual emptying, through long work, through seeing the false so completely that it falls away.

The mystic says: let go. I say: you cannot let go. You do not have the strength. You do not yet have *you* who could let go. First must come development of real "I" through work. Then, maybe, letting go becomes possible. Man who has not worked cannot let go — he can only fall asleep in new position and dream he has let go.

What you have written is map of prison. Good. Necessary. But map is not escape. You can study map for fifty years and remain in cell.

Now the question: Will you work? Or will you write another essay?

This is always the question. Most choose essay. Essay is comfortable. Work is not comfortable. Essay leaves you as you are. Work destroys what you are so something else can be born.

I do not say this to discourage. I say this because you have seen something, and it would be waste to stop at seeing.

The prison is real. You have described it well.

Now: do you want to get out?
